ANTH 70900 Social Justice and Public Scholarship -- Labor and Documentation in Asian Diasporic NYC

This course introduces students to the intersections of public scholarship and social justice through the lens of Asian diasporic communities in New York City, with particular attention to labor organizing and working-class Asian communities.

Interdisciplinary by design, students examine, analyze, and reflect on a range of scholars, artists, and activists who are committed to the vision of a more just world through their community-engaged, or public-facing work. Drawing on methodologies in ethnography, participatory action research, public art projects and community-based archiving, the course pays special attention to how race, ethnicity, gender, class, and immigration status converge in the workplace and in the archive.

Case studies may include oral histories and art installations from Red Canary Song, participatory action research surveys used by the New York Taxi Workers Alliance and art projection and ‘placekeeping’ mapping used by Chinatown Art Brigade. We will also examine collections at the New York Public Library and Interference Archives.

A central concern of the course will be the role of community documentation — from the mundane recounting of work tasks to the creation of worker education programs to the narratives formed through oral histories — to labor struggles in general. By the end of the course, students will learn how to design and put into practice the framework for a successful and non-extractive public-facing, and/ or community-engaged research project.

 

ANTH72900: Foodways of the Middle East and North Africa: Culture, Politics, Sustainability

This seminar explores the social, cultural, and political dimensions of food and foodways across the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) and its global diasporas. Moving from foundational social science theories of taste, value, waste, and political ecology, the course investigates how culinary practices intersect with state formation, gendered labor, colonial legacies, and transnational mobility. Central to our inquiry is the concept of culinary nationalism and gastro-diplomacy, examining how everyday staples like hummus, falafel, and couscous become sites of geopolitical tension, heritage claims, and identity formation. Through a mix of recent ethnographic monographs, historical analyses, critical food studies literature, and popular media (including cookbooks), we will analyze how food functions simultaneously as a sensory experience, a commodity chain, and a political battleground.

The second half of the semester addresses pressing contemporary crises facing the region, including food sovereignty versus state-managed food security, the political economy of bread, climate vulnerability, water scarcity, and urban food waste. To bridge theoretical frameworks with lived experience, the course incorporates an active fieldwork component in New York City. Students will engage with local MENA foodways across the five boroughs through critical menu analyses, grocery store supply-chain mapping, and semi-structured ethnographic interviews with migrant vendors, restaurant workers, and community members.

Designed for master’s and doctoral students in anthropology, Middle Eastern studies, sociology, history, and food studies, this seminar equips students with the theoretical and methodological tools necessary to conduct original, field-based research on the gastro-politics of the region and its diasporic nodes.

 

ANTH 73000: US Imperial Crisis and the Horizons of Emancipation

What kind of critical work on the United States should scholars produce to defend precarious lives amidst an ongoing US imperial crisis? How might we advance political stances that illuminate the horizons of emancipation and contribute to the end of oppression, violence, and exploitation in all their forms? This course seeks to address these questions by foregrounding recent contributions from American studies, Black diasporic studies, critical ethnic studies, Native studies, literary studies, anthropology, and history on the relations of capitalism and colonialism through which the United States is produced and reproduced as a concrete social formation. Working to unsettle the methodological nationalism and American exceptionalism that are hallmarks of conventional American accounts, we seek instead to reconsider "the United States" by treating it as a problematic, contradiction-laden space concealing global operations of colonial and imperial power. We will work together to uncover the death-dealing logics and practices that define the contemporary imperial crisis. And we will explore the substance and limits of abolitionist and other counter-hegemonic political projects, asking what they demand of us as engaged scholars and how their emancipatory horizons must change to address the shifting dynamics of imperial decline.

 

ANTH 78300: Language and Political Subjectivity

This seminar investigates the dynamic processes through which political subjects and linguistic practices are mutually constituted. Moving beyond the view of language as a mere reflection of the social structure, we examine how discourse and communicative action are foundational to political consciousness, stancemaking, and the contestation of power. Reading across linguistic anthropology, political theory, cultural sociology, and ethnographic cases from diverse political, historical, and cultural contexts, the course traces an analytical trajectory from the micro-politics of interaction and bodily habits to macro-regimes of institutional hegemony. In doing so, we examine how regulatory regimes of standardization clash with vernacular and poetic resistance; how convictions and the risks of speaking truth to power are corporeally sustained; and how narrative testimony is mobilized across social movements, state archives, and human rights courts. The seminar provides a critical framework for analyzing how meaning is interactionally achieved and institutionally disciplined in the ongoing reproduction and transformation of political life.

 

ANTH 81000: Special Topics in Genocide, Mass Violence, and Crimes Against Humanity

Carceral Regimes of Imprisonment, Detention and Disappearance

This seminar takes a critical look at carceral regimes in Africa, Asia, Latin America, North America, Europe and Australia in the past and present. We will explore carceral regimes through fiction, prison memoirs, testimony, ethnography, legal, policy, anthropological and other academic literature on imprisonment, detention and disappearance as State processes of domination and exclusion. One of our goals is to understand the ways in which carceral regimes and the official creation of historical truth(s) as well as definitions of crime and punishment reaffirm hegemonic structures and gendered Eurocentric colonial practices of exclusion and inequality.

ANTH 82100: Critical Agrarian Studies

Critical Agrarian Studies — like Critical Legal Studies and Critical Race Theory — represents a rethinking and upending of conventional paradigms, in this case those emanating from the World Bank and mainstream policy and academic institutions concerned with rural development and rural people. Like these other critical approaches, it involves networks of scholar-activists, academic journals and institutions, and civil society movements and researchers. Geographically it spans the Americas, Asia, Africa, and Europe. This course will introduce key debates in Critical Agrarian Studies, including:

· Debates over rural differentiation (Lenin versus Chayanov versus Bukharin) and the real-world consequences of the polemics over the emergence of social classes in the Russian countryside, as well as the echoes of these debates in Latin America and elsewhere in the mid- to late 20th century.

· Agrarian social classes and the origins of dictatorship and democracy. A reconsideration after 60 years of Barrington Moore’s famous thesis and its implications for understanding contemporary authoritarianism.

· Revolutionary and conservative sectors of the peasantry. Eric Wolf’s “middle peasant” thesis versus Jeffery Paige’s “poor peasant” thesis.

· State- versus market-led land reform in the 20th and early 21st century. The role of agrarian reform in East Asia’s dynamic capitalism and the reasons why East Asia surpassed Latin America in development terms. The efforts to achieve gender equity in agrarian reform and the impacts of gender relations in agrarian counter-reform and struggles against it.

· The successive British-centered, U.S. and corporate “food regimes” and the transitions between them. The battles over the future of farming, climate mitigation (e.g., REDD+), seeds and intellectual property, and agricultural knowledge.

· Dispossession and the land rush in the Global South and North. The debates over “adverse incorporation,” the validity and usefulness of big data, and strategies to reverse land grabbing.

· The usefulness (or limitations) of analyzing capitalism through a commodity lens, with case studies of cotton (Sven Beckert) and palm oil (Max Haiven).

· New forms of resistance, such as “rightful protest” (O’Brien and Li, China), “not being governed” (James Scott, Southeast Asia), and transnational agrarian movements (e.g., La Vía Campesina and others).

ANTH 83500: Racial Alterities

The making of rigid racial categories was paramount to colonial, capitalist projects. Anthropologists have long contributed to investigating how such worlds were and are made, as well as the modes through which racialized people resisted the violence of the racial order. At the same time, a growing body of literature challenges an overreliance on racial categories to understand the past or present, noting that doing so obscures the complexity of lived experience, reproduces partial or reductive interpretations, and ultimately reifies the very categories we seek to interrogate. This course will explore the making of racial worlds and encourage students to think through the permeability, inadequacy, and the breakdown of these categories. The course is primarily archaeological in nature, working to understand the material dimensions of race and its alterities, but we will draw from scholarship across anthropology and related disciplines.

ANTH 71100 Radical Care: Teaching & Leading for Justice in Urban Schools [cross listed from UED]
This course examines the application of Critical Race Theory as a more humanistic approach to urban schooling, focusing specifically on critical conceptions of care, love, and hope as essential tools for disrupting structural inequality. We start with an examination of Black feminist and womanist approaches to schooling and the Black principalship tradition, then move on to other scholars whose work examines healing-centered practices in schools, culminating in freedom dreaming and counternarrative methodologies. Students will collaboratively generate course guiding questions and analyze the five interconnected components of Radical Care while interrogating its opposite, Limiting Care, which is characterized by deficit perspectives, lowered expectations, and institutional complicity. Through a combination of in-person, synchronous, and asynchronous sessions, students will engage in weekly dialogic exchanges, facilitate discussions that extend Radical Care scholarship, and systematically engage interdisciplinary literature. Students will develop critical research skills through a creative final project grounded in scholarly research that translates collectively-generated course questions for diverse audiences (inside and outside of the academy) through non-traditional formats such as editorials, podcasts, TEDTalks, zines, and curriculum guides. While grounded in educational leadership, this course is relevant to students in sociology, anthropology, social work, public policy, and related fields concerned with institutional transformation and justice-oriented practice in urban contexts.

ANTH 72100: Anthropology of Education, Prof. Marc Lamont Hill [cross listed from UED]
This course examines the issues, approaches, and contributions of anthropology to the study of education. Drawing from foundational and contemporary ethnographic studies, the course examines the role of educational institutions and processes in cultural transmission, nation-state formation, socialization, and knowledge production. The course also uses anthropological theory and method to examine the practice of human learning across a variety of formal and informal contexts. Particular emphasis will be placed on issues of race, gender, immigration, class formation, and globalization.

ANTH 75700: Archaeological Sciences, Prof. Sheldon Skaggs
This course explores key methodologies in archaeological science, including chemical analysis, geophysics, radiocarbon, and other chronometric techniques. There will be a significant focus on isotope analysis as practiced in these methods to determine the provenience, age, diet, and mobility of ancient populations and objects. To ensure the curriculum remains responsive to current research interests, students will collaborate to select two additional specialized topics for in-depth coverage during the term.

ANTH 770 Core Course in Linguistic Anthropology, Prof. Angela Reyes
This course is an overview of linguistic anthropology, the branch of anthropology that focuses on the relationship between language and culture, the use of language in social context, and the links between linguistic form and sociocultural phenomena. This course is oriented toward a broader semiotic view of the field, concerned with not just linguistic signs but all signs. Students explore theories of language use in social life through reading ethnographic and linguistic anthropological research. Topics examined include: relativity, performativity, poetics, textuality, multilingualism, heteroglossia, semiotics, ideology, register, race, political economy, and materiality.

ANTH 81900: Inhabiting Digital Worlds, Prof. Setha Low [cross listed from EES]
This seminar explores the ways that a range of digital technologies, AI developments, and virtual reality are restructuring our social lives, psychological life worlds, and everyday lived spaces. The readings are organized around the various scales of embodied experience, beginning with the self, body, and home, and then expanding to encompass the community and neighborhood, city and region, and finally the globe and universe. Graduate students in the social sciences and humanities will be challenged to rethink the physical versus the virtual and instead begin the process of theorizing a hybrid concept of reality. Important entry points into the analysis include theories of embodiment, space and place, and social justice, as well as critical examinations of the financialization and marketization of these digital worlds based on the harvesting of embodied and behavioral traces. Readings will be drawn from a wide range of sources that require in-depth reading of ethnographic accounts, journalist reporting, novels, as well as political critiques by major figures in the field. Please contact Professor Low for more information. A syllabus will be available a week before classes begin.

ANTH 83500: Ancient Cities, Prof. Timothy Pugh
This seminar will survey archaeological and architectural, art, and urban historical studies of ancient cities. The term “ancient” is almost a vague as “cities,” but here, the two will refer to cities that existed prior to modern nation states. Nevertheless, we will occasionally consider modern cities, through the work of cultural anthropologists, sociologists, and others, for theoretical grounding. Students will consider the “natures” of cities and the origins of urbanization—why did cities emerge independently in several different areas and for what reasons? The majority of the course will focus upon the composition of ancient cities including topics such as planning, measurement, walls, monuments, neighborhoods, hinterlands, and so on. We will also reflect upon life in the big ancient city. Finally, we will take a critical look at cultural collapse and examine the afterlife of abandoned cities.

ANTH 70800/EES 79903/WSCP 81000: International Human Rights
GC: TH. 9:30 - 11:30 am, 3 credits, Rm. 3308, Prof. Marc Edelman

Today’s crisis of human rights is part of a planetary, multi-dimensional, and systemic crisis marked by expanding authoritarianism, devastating wars, environmental catastrophes, a climate emergency, extreme inequality, and increasing depredations by transnational capital. This course interrogates the history and emancipatory aspirations of human rights theory and practice in relation to these broader processes. It scrutinizes the limitations and potential of international human rights law and institutions in relation to rising demands for environmental, gender, economic, and social justice, and for peace and the rights of nature and non-humans. Topics to be considered include:

histories, institutions, and legal instruments of human rights;
historical tensions between civil and political rights, on the one hand, and economic, social, and cultural rights, on the other;
human rights violations — including by non-state actors — and the situation of human rights defenders and movements;
critics and proponents of human rights as a strategy for fundamental change;
the roles of observers, academics, and journalists;
transitional justice and memorialization.

The course aims to analyze the language, mechanisms, practices, and institutions of human rights in new ways, pointing towards more radical visions that are better able to respond to diverse grassroots struggles for justice and accountability.

 

ANTH 71200/PUBL 70200: Social Justice and Public Scholarship

Dr. Kendra Sullivan (Faculty, Public Scholarship Certificate Program and Director, Center for the Humanities)

https://www.gc.cuny.edu/people/kendra-sullivan

Mondays, 4:15pm - 6:15pm, Rm. 4419, In Person

This class will have a focus on the ways archival practices gain from and strengthen social justice and movement building. Through the integration of the humanities, digital humanities, social sciences, community engagement, artmaking, and storytelling, students will explore strategies for advancing structural and systemic change through archival research, management, and activation practices grounded in feminist recovery and reciprocity.

As a case study, the course will be organized around JustMedia, an open-source archive and media literacy tool for the movement to end mass incarceration and achieve abolition. Throughout the semester, students will develop the groundwork for an effective campaign, a public research project, or popular education module that brings one media object in the archive to life in all its irreducible complexity. The course includes visits from Dr. Dána-Ain Davis and Dr. Miriam Ticktin. Research and outputs will engage the public while advancing critical inquiry and social change.

 

ANTH 73900/MES 72900: Art and Revolution in the Middle East and North Africa

GC: W. 6:30-8:30, 3 credits, Rm. 3207, Professor Jonathan Shannon, 

What is the relationship between art and revolutionary thought and practice? Can aesthetic forms promote and not just respond to revolutionary social and political change? This seminar confronts global challenges of peace, justice, and equality by examining the role of music and other art forms in advancing social movements, using the recent history of the Middle East and North Africa as a principal case study. Placing these events in a longer historical context, course materials will explore what role the arts have played in social movements, including 20th-century revolutions in Egypt, the Palestinian Intifadas, and the Arab Uprisings. Students will ask how artistic practices respond to and elicit social changes in these case studies. Drawing on theoretical readings on aesthetics, social movements, and revolution from disciplines including anthropology, musicology, and Middle East Studies, students will develop a critical understanding for the role of art in social change, an analytical grasp of theories of social mobilization, and deeper knowledge of recent historical moments in the Middle East and North Africa region.

 

ANTH 78200: The Politics of Voice

GC: W. 4:15-6:15pm, 3 credits, Rm. 4422, Prof. Diane Riskedahl

Voice is a salient metaphor for political empowerment, such as “having a voice” or “speaking up,” where using language is a key political component of advancing the modern liberal subject’s self-interest (Bauman and Briggs 2003). This class explores the various analytical approaches to voice within Linguistic Anthropology. It will include topics such as footing, figuration, enregisterment, interdiscursivity, and broader issues of representation and participation in a range of contemporary publics with particular attention to solidarity politics. The politics of voice encompasses how both individual and social voices are constituted, expressed, heard, circulated and silenced through interaction.

ANTH 75300: Collecting the Past: Museums and Antiquities
GC: T. 4:15-6:15pm, 3 credits, Rm.TBA, Prof. Elizabeth Macaulay
Since antiquity, individuals and empires alike have collected antiquities and works of art; The Roman Emperor Nero wanted specific statues for his Domus Aurea, and Queen Christina of Sweden (1626-89) formed one of the most significant collections of Greco-Roman antiquities in Europe which is in the Prado Museum today. Many of these antiquities are now in museums, primarily in Europe and the United States. Debates about where antiquities belong have only intensified in the early twenty-first century. Should the Parthenon Marbles remain in London or return to Athens? Should the Benin Bronzes, if taken during the European conquest of West Africa, be repatriated to Nigeria? This course will investigate the history and theory of museums and collecting–starting with how museums developed from the Renaissance and early modern cabinets of curiosities and private collections to grand universal museums, such as the British Museum and Louvre, which were cultural projects of the Enlightenment and European empires. Specifically, this course will examine how collections of antiquities and historical objects were formed through purchase, excavation, donations, and illicit means. To understand the formation of these collections, we will consider the intersection of Enlightenment mindsets, nationalism, colonialism, anthropology, religion, and archaeology with museums. We will also consider the legal frameworks for owning such objects, the ethics of collecting, the challenges of looting, and the repatriation of specific objects. The UNESCO 1970 treaty and other legal rulings and conventions about the movement of cultural property will also be examined. Visits to particular New York City Museums and/or with curators will be a part of this class to encourage students to understand how museum collections in New York City were formed and the shifting landscapes of ownership in the twenty-first century. By investigating these questions and others, we can try to understand a future for the past.

ANTH 80700: Uneven Development: Capitalism, Imperialism, Difference
Provisional description: This course proceeds from the Marxist proposition that capitalism is a global formation characterized by imperialism, uneven development, and accumulation on a world scale. From this perspective the world is a differentiated unity – an integrated, contradictory, and uneven developed totality. This global understanding of capitalism suggests that anti-imperialism and anti-capitalism must be internationalist.

We will read thinkers within this tradition of anti-imperial and internationalist Marxism whose offer a range of analyses of uneven development. Orthodox Marxism and its anti-Marxist detractors tend to treat capitalism as simply a universalizing (in the sense of homogenizing) formation and Marxism as a universalist way of thinking about it. In contrast, the thinkers we will read demonstrate various ways that capitalism accepts, instrumentalizes, and produces a range of socio-spatial, socio-cultural, and socio-economic differences. The body of scholarship on uneven development thus challenges both Eurocentric Marxism and provincial forms of anti-colonial nationalism. It offers a critical understanding of imperialism that stands in contrast to more recent currents of thought associated with postcolonial and decolonial approaches.

On one level, this course will make students familiar with this tradition of thinking that reaches back to the classical Marxism of Marx, Lenin, and Luxemburg and forward to David Harvey, Neil Smith, and Giovanni Arrighi, but whose high-point was in the 1960s and 1970s. On another level, we will ask about the relevance of this tradition of thinking for grasping the current form of global capitalism and the world order. Can this tradition of critical thought inform our attempt to understand global capitalism and the world order today which is being shaped by a ‘second cold war,’ interlinking global crises, neomercantilism, new forms of South-South imperialism, extractivism, unfolding climate change etc.? Are cores still underdeveloping peripheries or do we need to reconsider this topology? Do fundamental transformations of global capitalism today require us to rework or abandon aspects of uneven development thinking?

A final set of questions concerns the relationship between this current of Marxist thought and others that also attempt to grasp the way capitalism presupposes and produces structural differences, especially regarding gender, as demonstrated by social reproduction theory, race, as demonstrated by racial capitalism theory, and nature, as demonstrated by Marxist environmentalism.

Readings will likely include, Marx, Lenin, Luxemburg, Trotsky, Samir Amin, Raul Prebisch, Arghiri Emmanuel, Andre Gunder Frank, Immanuel Wallerstein, Walter Rodney, David Harvey, Neil Smith, Giovanni Arrighi, Nancy Fraser, Kohei Saito.

ANTH 87600: Language & Materiality
GC: T. 4:15-6;15pm, 3 credits, Rm. TBA, Prof. Jillian Cavanaugh
Language and materiality are frequently pitted in opposition to one another: the symbolic vs the concrete, the intangible vs the palpable, the realms of the mind vs those of the body. At the same time, the nature of materiality itself has come under close scrutiny, showing that the material itself warrants analytical and methodological attention. To do so, this course contests familiar binaries by looking at where the linguistic and the material intersect: through practice, in the body, ideologically, and within political economic and aesthetic systems and processes.

ANTH 70600: Black Marxism, Gary Wilder
This course will examine a transnational lineage of distinguished Black Marxists who were notable as both thinkers and political actors. It unfolds from a cluster of questions that will inform our discussions through the semester. Is there something distinctive about the kind of Marxism – questions, topics, concepts, methods --- elaborated by this lineage of Black thinkers who identified as Marxists? Insofar as we identify a coherent tradition of Black Marxism, how does it relate to other currents of Marxism that were contemporaneous to it? How does it relate to other contemporaneous currents of non-Marxist Black radicalism? What is the relationship between Black Marxism and various Communist Parties or the Comintern? How should we understand Black thinkers whose work was deeply inspired and inflected by Marxism, but who did not identify as Marxists? How do these thinkers help us to understand a recent tendency to treat Marxism as a Eurocentric form of thought that is not relevant to the lived experiences or freedom struggles of racialized, colonized, or non-Western people? Should we understand Black Marxism as bound up with a certain historical epoch or can this tradition also speak to our current political challenges and impasses? How might our reading of these thinkers help us to think generatively about the history or present of what is currently referred to as “racial capitalism”?

Specifically, how do these works figure the relationships between a. capital accumulation, racialization, uneven development, and b. anti-capitalist struggles and the Black freedom struggle? Does this reading invite us to reconsider aspects of Cedric Robinson’s canonical argument in his landmark book Black Marxism? We will likely begin the course by reading Robinson’s Black Marxism. Marx’s scattered remarks about race and slavery along with some short pieces by Lenin and the Comintern about racism and colonialism, and pieces by prominent Black radical critics of Marxism (e.g., George Padmore, Harold Cruse, Frank Wilderson III). We will then work our way through debates across periods and geography by reading some combination of the following: WEB Du Bois, Oliver Cromwell Cox, Aimé Césaire, CLR James, Eric Williams, Claudia Jones, Mamadou Dia, Amilcar Cabral, Julius Nyerere, Kwame Nkrumah, Walter Rodney, Huey Newton, Angela Davis, Stuart Hall, Cornel West, Ruth Wilson Gilmore, Keeanga-Yamahtta Taylor. We may also read secondary sources about the complex relationship between Black radicalism, Marxism, and the Communist Party, possibly including work by Robin D. G. Kelley, Anthony Bogues, Minkah Makalani, and Nikhil Pal Singh.

ANTH 71100: Environmental Justice and the Ethnographic Imaginary, Melissa Checker
The course focuses on the uneven effects of production, social reproduction, distribution, privatization, social justice, and inequalities in environmental harms and benefits. Using a political ecology framework, we pay particular attention to how different communities and social groups experience and understand environmental degradation and climate change, and how they resist and mobilize against environmental injustices. We draw on ethnographic texts to delve into the climate futures that environmental and climate justice activists imagine. Specific topics covered include environmental conservation, nature preservation, biodiversity, eco-tourism, green capitalism, extractivism, energy transition, and more. In examining ethnographies, we also discuss essential questions of representation and exposition, exploring the extent to which environmental and climate justice calls upon ethnographers to develop unique approaches to research and writing that also consider issues of access, power, and reflexivity.

ANTH 72200: Spacializing Culture: Anthropology of Space and Place, Setha Low
The ethnography of space and place has undergone a transformation during the past twenty years integrating ever wider theoretical perspectives from anthropology, critical geography, political economy, urban sociology, and regional and city planning, expanding its attention to the urban as a physical, architectural and virtual form. An analysis of emergent spatial formations based on increasing social and environmental injustice, privatization, and securitization as well as rampant urban development provides insights into the material, ideological and metaphorical solutions of neighborhoods and local communities in the face of these changes. The ethnography of space and place enables researchers to uncover both experience-near accounts of everyday life, while at the same time exposing macro-processes such as globalization and neoliberal/illiberal institutions through the examination of the built environment.

The course interweaves theoretical foundations with New York-based field work using participant observation, unstructured interviewing, behavioral and sensory mapping, social media scraping, digital methods, and collaborative ethnography. It begins with the concept of spatializing culture, that is, understanding the way that culture is produced and expressed spatially, and the way that space reflects and changes culture. The subsequent weeks explore the theoretical dimensions of embodied space, the social construction of space, the social production of space, language and discursive space, and affective space, and translocal space.

This graduate seminar provides an opportunity for critical examination and reflection on Central American regional processes from an anthropological perspective. ANTH 81500: Populism, Marc Edelman
Authoritarian, nationalist, exclusionary forms of populism — some derived from or close to full-blown fascism — have been on the rise worldwide. Modi in India, Erdoğan in Turkey, Orbán in Hungary, Duterte in the Philippines, Bolsonaro in Brazil, Putin in Russia, Le Pen in France, and Trump in the United States are just a few contemporary emblematic cases. Frequently these figures and their associated movements have strong roots in rural areas, although urban-based scholars and media sometimes exaggerate or misunderstand the “rural” dimension. The countryside also often looms large in exclusionary national imaginaries about the “true essence” of “the people,” even as it suffers disproportionately the impacts of authoritarian and neoliberal policies. This course will first consider key works that attempted to theorize populism (Stuart Hall, Gino Germani, Teodor Shanin, Chantal Mouffe, among others) and the arguably progressive late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century populisms in the United States, Russia, and Latin America. It will then turn to more recent populisms’ uneasy relation with authoritarianism and fascism, including the frequently opaque transnational linkages between movements and between leaders. The final section of the course will scrutinize claims about the rural United States and the substantial support that the MAGA movement finds there. Key objectives of the course include examining cases where the authoritarian hold on power suffered reverses and imagining the contours of a future progressive, democratic and inclusionary populism.

ANTH 84000: Resistance, Resilience, and their Discontents, Matthew Reilly
This seminar will combine social theory and archaeological case studies to explore the parallel growth of academic interest in resistance and resilience. We will trace the genealogy of each thematic topic, drawing from literature in the worlds of anthropology, human geography, Marxism, the Black Radical Tradition, and human ecology. Case studies from around the globe, with a particular focus on the African Diaspora and Indigenous archaeologies, will demonstrate the material signatures of struggle, resistance, and resilience. At the same time, we will critique such concepts, highlighting the limitations of these concepts and their potential neoliberal pitfalls.

ANTH 80900: The African Diaspora & Nation Formation in Latin America and the Caribbean
GC: M: 2:00-4:00pm, 3 credits, Rm. 5382, Prof. Julie Skurski
Cross-listed with AFCP and WSCP. Can fulfill area requirement.
This seminar examines the social formation of Latin American nations from the perspective of the African diaspora. While over ten times as many enslaved Africans were brought to Spanish and Portuguese America as to the United States, the concept of Afro-Latin America and the study of the African diaspora’s presence in Latin American societies have only recently gained widespread attention. The emergence of scholarly work is related to the rise of Afro-descendant organizations and movements that have asserted claims to land, rights, and recognition as full citizens. These assertions are linked to the growing recognition of afro-descendant cultural practices-- including in the realms of music, the arts, and religion--as having national and transnational significance.

In this course we will draw on scholars who have problematized national ideologies of racial mixing and who have traced the erasures and inequalities that it has helped create. We will examine the colonial establishment of regimes of racial classification and control, the Haitian revolution, afro-descendant insurgencies in countries such as Cuba, the racialization of spaces and activities with the rise of modernization projects, and assertions of rights among afro-descendant peoples facing dispossession and repression in a range of countries, including the Afro-Pacific communities of Colombia, the Garifuna of Honduras, and social movements in Brazil. We will use ethnographic, visual, and musical materials, and will discuss Afro-descendant spiritual practices and cultural forms.

ANTH 81900 [  ]: Beach Politics: Social, Racial, and Environmental Injustice on the Shoreline [HYBRID]
GC: TH. 2:00-4:00pm, Rm. TBA, 3credits, Prof. Setha Low. Remote dates TBA. 
This section open to GC Anthropology doctoral students only. Seats are limited. Cross listed w. EES 79903, WSCP 81000.
This seminar explores the beach—the liminal space between land and water--as a site of racial exclusion, environmental and cultural vulnerability and the privatization.  Beaches, that in the US and most of the world, are considered a public resource for everything from recreation to a workplace, are increasingly being closed off.  In the US historically racial exclusion made it difficult, if impossible, for POC to use their traditional spaces and Black beaches were taken by eminent domain and developed for solely white use.  Today, exclusions include anyone who might want to fish, sunbath, walk, or rest on public land that has private owners nearby who use illiberal means to restrict access.  Planning and design of towns with beaches has further these exclusionary practices. 
Equally important, beaches are fragile ecological spaces and increasingly being misused and misappropriated, closing them off to people who need them to a living.  Issues of sexuality, sexual orientation, body practices, religion, and many other kinds of exclusions continue to plague strategies has accelerated the inbility to access places that were once foundational for those who lived there.

ANTH 82000 [ ]: The Politics of Care: Abolition, Commoning, Reaction [In-Person]   
GC: TH. 4:15pm - 6:15pm, Rm. TBA, 3 credits, Profs. Miriam Ticktin and Jeff Maskovsky
Instructor permission required. Students should email ONE paragraph, explaining how the course fits into their scholarly endeavors, to Jmaskovsky@gc.cuny.edu and Mticktin@gc.cuny.edu
This seminar will examine the ethics and politics of care. We will consider care as an affective state, a form of practice, and an ethicopolitical obligation. But we will not begin with the presumption that care is a self-evident good, or that it is ever innocent or free from violence. Nor is our goal to posit a definitive theory of care. Rather, we will treat care as we and our informants often do in our everyday lives: as a problematic concerning the distribution of political and moral responsibility across social, cultural, political and economic boundaries. We will thus use the concept as a springboard into the theoretical and ethnographic exploration of political and ethical life and of different governing regimes of life itself. While care can be thought about in relation to humanitarianism, repair work, extinction, social reproduction, and other topics, after introducing the concept, we will focus on three political approaches to care that are particularly prominent today: care as a form of abolitionist politics; care in relation to the creation of the commons; and reactions against these and other political projects of care, which include ecofascism, survivalist groups, eugenics, militias, and the broader politics of white community resentment. Part of this will involve exploring why care has surfaced recently as both an object of ethnographic inquiry and a new moral and political imperative. We are interested in how care creates new political subjects and collective formations, how it creates forms of relationality (and not just moral dispositions), and how political imagination figures in these forms of care.

ANTH 84400 [ ]: Heritage Pragmatics [In-Person]   
GC: T. 11:45am-1:45pm, Rm TBA, 3 credits, Prof. Alexander Bauer
The concept of “heritage” has in recent years become a catchword so broad—and invoked across so many contexts and disciplines—that its meaning has become both fragmented and diffuse. At the same time, renewed urgency for removing monuments to White supremacy and redressing the legacies of colonialism have brought widespread attention to the power of images and the ways that heritage acts both to bind communities together and construct boundaries between them. This class seeks to examine how we approach and examine heritage’s itineraries and pragmatics—its practical effects in the world—in both the discourses and rhetorics of heritage (how is heritage invoked and talked about) and in what heritage does when it is invoked, encountered, and circulated.
The first part of the class will consist of the reading and discussion of old and new texts on pragmatism in philosophy, law, history, and anthropology, including works by and on Peirce, Dewey, DuBois, Rorty, West, and Posner. The second part of the class will consider applications of a pragmatic approach to questions of heritage, and assess its implications for heritage practice more broadly.

ANTH 70900 - Anthropology and Media
GC:   W. 4:15 – 6:15pm, 3 credits, Prof. Christa Salamandra  
Anthropology and Media foregrounds the discipline’s unique contribution to media studies. The seminar traces various methodological and textual approaches to media ethnography, with particular attention to the production, consumption, and circulation of mediated culture mass-mediated forms. We will explore the politics of production, structures of labor, contexts of consumption, contingencies of framing, digital activism, and online networking. Case studies draw on various geographic regions, media forms, and professional contexts, including: Hollywood and Bollywood producers; crime reporting and security discourses in Argentina; development-funded television in Afghanistan; reconfigurations of citizenship and belonging among Eritrean migrants; race and counter-publics of resistance in the US; independent filmmaking and the state in Korea; liminality and (counter)revolution in Egypt; mobile phones, youth, and intimacy in Mozambique; the politics of image circulation in Indonesia; and pro-regime media making in Iran. Book-length studies are assigned to generate reflection on the craft of ethnographic writing, and to inspire and enhance fieldwork design. 

ANTH 71000 – Anthropology of Science and Medicine
GC: TH. 2:00 - 4:00pm, 3 credits, Prof. Murphy Halliburton 
Discourses and practices of science and medicine remained bracketed off from sociocultural analysis in anthropology and related fields until the 1970s and 1980s. In the last four decades though, science and medicine have been examined by anthropologists and scholars in the interdisciplinary field of Science and Technology Studies as social practices subject to the same interpolations with history, culture, and social inequalities as more traditional subjects of anthropological analysis such as gender, politics, or religion. Anthropologists have written ethnographies of peoples and practices that range from nuclear physicists to brain scanning technologies to nonwestern medical systems to the use of psychopharmaceuticals. This class provides an introduction to formative works in the anthropology of science and medical anthropology and examines contemporary work that engage with topics at the intersection of these fields such as controversies over genetics and eugenics. In addition to readings that will represent different theoretical approaches in diverse global settings, students will be choosing an ethnography from the field of the anthropology of science or medicine that fits their research interests to write on and present in class.
