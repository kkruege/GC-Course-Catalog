Bres 70003: Black Feminist Poetics & Aesthetics: “Becoming & Going: Under the Eyes of the Black Self”

Allia Abdullah-Matta, In-person
Tuesdays 4:15 - 6:15 p.m.

Course Overview:
This course engages and explores literary and cultural production using Black Feminist poetic and aesthetic lenses. Students will examine different methods of thinking about Black feminist praxis as a site of inquiry/research. Our course theme, “becoming and going under the eyes of the black self” signifies the ways in which Black poetry symbolizes becoming, going, and defining the black self. Centering Black feminist theory/praxis and the concept of “symbolic voice” [identity/utterance/agency] as representative of the Black experience, struggle, and resistance, students will consider how Black women’s art and poetry is reflective of historical periods and cultural moments, the political and socio-economic circumstances of Black people, and expressions of agency and resistance. This course uses frameworks and concepts such as Black art, Black aesthetics, and Black feminist poetics/aesthetics to emphasize and situate the historical and cultural contexts that create and make-meaning of Black Feminist experience(s) and voice(s) as reflected in African Diasporic art, music, and poetry. Students will examine a cross-genre of texts (theory, fine art, poetry & hip-hop) across artistic, cultural, and literary movements.

BRES 70002: Interdisciplinary Theories and Research on Race and Ethnic Studies

Eric Tang, In-person
Thursdays 4:15 - 6:15 p.m.

Course Overview:
This seminar introduces major interdisciplinary theoretical perspectives that inform research and scholarship on race and ethnic studies in the U.S. and beyond. The seminar highlights the centrality of interdisciplinary, multidisciplinary, and transdisciplinary approaches to the study of race and ethnicity. It also examines the core concepts that have informed the field, including intersectionality, indigeneity, diaspora, transnationalism, racism, and decolonization. It also introduces core components of research design, including the politics and ethics of research, how to develop research projects, issues on sampling, measurement, and operationalization, and an introduction to the collection, management, and analysis of qualitative and quantitative data.

BRES 85000: Gender, Race, and Social Justice

Katarzyna Celinska, In-person
Mondays 6:30 - 8:30 p.m.

Course Overview:
This interdisciplinary course examines how gender and race shape social experiences and inequalities in contemporary society. The course draws on concepts, theories, and current scholarship from a variety of disciplines, including sociology, criminology, criminal justice, gender studies, history, and economics. Topics may include mass incarceration and justice-involved women; gendered migration; sexual victimization and gender-based violence; drug use, mental illness, and the “War on Drugs” in the United States; educational inequality and achievement; reproductive rights; capitalism and labor markets; and contemporary social movements, among others. By the end of the course, students will gain a deeper understanding of major contemporary social problems and the ways in which race, gender, class, and other social identities shape individual experiences, life opportunities, and achievements. Students will learn to critically analyze social issues through an intersectional lens, evaluate current policies, and consider alternative approaches to promoting social justice, equality, and social change. The course will culminate in student presentations on topics of their choice related to gender, race, and social justice.

BRES 85000/ MALS 73500 Africana Studies: Global Perspectives

Nathalie Etoke
Wednesdays 4:15 - 6:15 p.m.

Course Overview:
This course examines problems of existence and freedom posed by black life. We will explore how the racialization of people of African descent through the means of violence and oppression translates into an existential predicament addressing the human confrontation with hope and hopelessness, freedom, and human degradation, being and non-being. We will discuss the existentialist implications, challenges, and possibilities of black life in Africana literature, film, and music. How do cultural expressions of black people simultaneously engage being acted upon by the external forces of enslavement and racism, while acting against those forces? Through critical analyses of music, film, fiction, and contemporary events, this class will generate theoretical interventions embedded in the poetics and politics of (self) representation, freedom, and social constructions of black existence.

BRES 85000/ MALS 70600 Introduction to Global Modern and Contemporary Studies

Karen Miller, In-person
Thursdays 6:30-8:30

Introduction to Global Modern and Contemporary Studies, MALS 70600, In-person
Professors Debarati Biswas and Karen Miller

Course Overview:
Colonialism and postcolonialism have profoundly shaped and continue to shape the modern and contemporary world. In this class, we will explore a range of case studies and theoretical pieces that ask questions about colonial and racial capitalism, indigeneity, gender and sexuality, carcerality, transnational organizing, social movements, borders, art, and other themes. We will focus on the experiences of marginalized and minoritized groups across the globe in order to understand the impact of colonial and settler colonial histories and legacies on their lives. We will also consider how the embodied axes of race, gender, class, sexuality, ability, and national origins determine the limits of material success, mental and physical well-being, access to healthcare and education, and struggle. The class will be organized thematically. Each week, we will examine a narrow set of questions that scholars have posed within a specific subfield through scholarly and creative writing as well as film and visual culture.

BRES 85000/ MES 73900: Slavery and Social Status in Middle Eastern History

Professor Anna Akasoy, In-person
Mondays, 6:30-8:30 p.m.

Course Overview:
In this class, we will explore social, political, economic, legal, and cultural aspects of slavery in premodern Islamic history. Starting in the late antique Mediterranean, we will consider the emergence of a variety of forms of slavery in the Islamic Middle East, including military slavery and agricultural slavery, but focus especially on the enslavement of women. We will end with the complex relationship between Islam and transatlantic slavery and various ethical and political implications of the history of religiously validated enslavement. We will consider a range of sources, including legal material and popular literature. Prior knowledge of Middle Eastern or Islamic history is not required.

BRES 85000/ MES 75900: Demography, Politics, and Societies in the Middle East and North Africa

Professor Elhum Haghighat
Thursdays, 6:30-8:30 p.m.

Course Overview:
The demographics of the Middle East show a highly populated, culturally diverse region spanning three continents. This course examines population trends and their implications for national and international politics and policies in the MENA region as well as their impact on social and cultural changes. Topics include demographic transitions, population growth and the environment, public health policies, gender and family patterns, control of fertility, mortality, forced and voluntary migration, boom and bust cycles, and graying of populations.

BRES 85000/ MALS 74900 Special Topics in Public Scholarship

Professor Prithi Kanakamedala, In-person
Wednesdays 4:15-6:15 p.m.

Course Overview:
While there is a myriad of ways to do public scholarship, the GC holds a long radical tradition of research that is non-extractive, multidisciplinary, committed to the public good and rooted in Black Studies, Ethnic Studies and Queer Studies. Special Topics in Public Scholarship will honor these roots by exploring three interweaving public pillars of knowledge production: Oral Histories, Archives, and Zines. We will study the work of feminist BIPOC oral historians, explore the politics of creating archives, and look at how radical histories interweave with Black feminist zine making practice. Students will work on a collective oral history project, learn how to curate oral histories for various archives, and deepen their zine-making practice.

BRES 85000/ PUBL 70200 Social Justice and Public Scholarship - Labor and Documentation in Asian NYC

Professor Linta Varghese, In-person
Thursdays 4:15pm-6:15 p.m.

Course Overview:
This course introduces students to the intersections of public scholarship and social justice through the lens of Asian communities in New York City, with particular attention to labor organizing and working-class Asian communities.

Interdisciplinary by design, students examine, analyze, and reflect on a range of scholars, artists, and activists who are committed to the vision of a more just world through their community-engaged, or public-facing work. Drawing on methodologies in oral history, participatory action research, public history, ethnography, public art projects and community-based archiving, the course pays special attention to how race, ethnicity, gender, class, and immigration status converge in the workplace and in the archive. Case studies may include oral histories and art installations from Red Canary Song, participatory action research surveys used by the New York Taxi Workers Alliance and art projection and ‘placekeeping’ mapping used by Chinatown Art Brigade. We will also examine collections at the New York Public Library and Interference Archives.

A central concern of the course will be the role of community documentation — from the mundane recounting of work tasks to the creation of worker education programs to the narratives formed through oral histories — to labor struggles in general. By the end of the course, students will learn how to design and put into practice the framework for a successful public-facing, and/ or community-engaged research project.

Exploring Asian America: Migrations, Histories, and Identities

Yung-Yi Diana Pan, Hybrid Synchronous
Mondays, 2-4 p.m. (in person meetings on 2/1; 3/8; 3/29; 4/12; and 5/3)

Course Overview:
Asian American and Pacific Islanders are often an underexamined population in the United States. Yet their presence and histories have not only made contributions to the United States but were also fraught by discriminatory and racist laws. Select Asian ethnic group's successes after World War II have also been used to justify racialized stratification in the American imagination. This course takes an interdisciplinary examination of the critical issues in Asian American and diasporic communities. We will discuss Asian American and Pacific Islander migration and community histories, especially the relevant aspects missing from mainstream knowledge. Specifically, this course will cover major themes, such as historical and contemporary immigration laws, social movements, racialization theories, gendered experiences, comparative educational outcomes, and community politics.

MALS 72000: Thesis Writing Course

FOR MA STUDENTS WHO ARE WRITING OR JUST ABOUT TO WRITE CAPSTONE PROJECTS (You must have an advisor committed in order to sign up.)

Online

The Thesis Writing Course - MALS 72000 is a three-credit research and writing course open to MALS students working on their theses. The course is designed to help students develop strategies for composing, drafting, and crafting their theses. Students will work on analyzing and synthesizing their materials in order to sharpen their approach to their scholarly topics. They will also work on developing their writing in logically framed and rhetorically convincing graduate-level prose. Students will produce multiple drafts, consider the best organizational approaches for their projects, develop and hone their authorial voices, develop strategies for building arguments that are supported by the evidence they have collected, and reflect upon their individual research and writing practices. Students will give and receive feedback from their peers as well as from the professor. Students normally have at least 18 credits when they are given permission to enroll in this course. Typically, students take MALS 72000 either the semester before they enroll in MALS 79000 or concurrent with MALS 79000.

If you would like to sign up for the thesis writing course, permission from the department is required. Please email Amber and Gabrielle with your name, thesis advisor and where you are in the process. This is a 3-credit course and it is not a substitute for BRES 79000.

BRES 70001 3 credits Foundations of Race and Ethnic Studies

This seminar introduces the theoretical foundations for the interdisciplinary study of race and ethnicity, with an emphasis on historical, global, and intersectional approaches to the study of race and ethnicity. This multidisciplinary field of scholarship calls us to address unequal relationships of power and domination by analyzing the historical and global construction, emergence, and consequences of ethnoracial categories and distinctions. Drawing on case studies from the Americas and elsewhere, this course aims to establish a foundation of key terms, theories, and ideas in the field, as well as familiarize students with a broad survey across time and regions. Major topics include how racial categories have formed and evolved over time and across societies and how racial categories have informed ideas about power, oppression, and liberation. The seminar will draw on various classic and contemporary texts from critical race theory, history, feminist studies, postcolonial studies, sociology, anthropology, and other disciplines.
