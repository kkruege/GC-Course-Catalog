CLAS 71100, The Homeric Iliad 
Prof. Joel Christensen
Monday 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m. (3 credits), In-person
This course studies the Homeric Iliad. We will pay particular attention to features of the Homeric dialect and its meter and differences between reading epic in the modern sense and engaging in it as part of a performance culture. Participants will be expected to read the Iliad in its entirety in translation outside of class; course translation will focus on book 1 and then on the single day battle that occupies books 11–18. Discussions will survey different scholarly approaches to Homer, but will emphasize frameworks from performance studies, cognitive and affect theory, and insights from trauma studies. These different perspectives will support a deeper understanding of the interconnection between the structures of Homeric poetry and audience responses.

Knowledge of ancient Greek is required; but previous experience with Greek poetry is not. Students will be expected to read and discuss Greek texts in class.

CLAS 72800, Ancient Medicine 
Prof. Bronwen Wickkiser
Tuesday 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m. (3 credits), In-person
(Cross listed with WSCP 81000)
This course will explore a variety of textual and material evidence for health and healthcare in Greek and Roman antiquity. We will begin with a modern biography of Galen (who lived 129-ca.216 CE) to help situate us within Greco-Roman culture more broadly, as we prepare to delve into specific aspects of medical practice. Topics include the development of ‘scientific’ medicine alongside traditional beliefs that pointed to the gods as the cause of illness, the devastating social and physical effects of plague and other epidemics, theories and practices concerning female bodies, and alternatives to doctors such as temple healing and magic. Knowledge of ancient Greek, while helpful, is not required.

CLAS 72800, The Art of Late Antique and Medieval Letters 
Prof. Brian Sowers
Monday 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m. (3 credits), In-person
(Cross listed with MSCP 70100 and MALS 74400)
This course introduces students to ancient, late antique, and early medieval letters and epistolography (the art of writing, circulating, and publishing letters). This course focuses on a single literary genre (letters) in a way that introduces students to wider research and reading methods relevant to premodern studies (ancient, late antique, medieval). Course readings will be in English; students with mastery of additional ancient/modern languages will be expected to incorporate these language skills in their research papers.

CLASS-GA 3000 Latin Poetry in Late Antiquity
Tuesday 12:30 - 2:30 p.m., Adam Becker
This course will meet at 8 Washington ​Mews Room 105. This seminar introduces both the well-known and lesser-known Latin poets of Late Antiquity. We will examine the origins of Christian poetry (Commodian), the Christian Latin epic (Juvencus, Sedulius, Arator), the cento (Proba), and panegyric (Claudian, Sidonius Apollinaris, Corippus). We will read poets who engaged with and reshaped classical models (Prudentius, Dracontius, Pseudo-Prosper of Aquitaine, Boethius) and conclude with the prolific poets of Merovingian Gaul (Avitus of Vienne, Venantius Fortunatus). In addition, we will consider Christian theorizing about the correct purpose of literature, such as in Augustine’s De Doctrina Christiana and Paulinus of Nola’s rejection of his mentor Ausonius’s poetic model. While tracing the reception of classical poets, in particular Virgil and Ovid, we will also observe the parallel emergence of new formal developments such as the proliferation of accentual verse.

CLASS-GA 3002.001 Social Mobility in the Greek World, 800-50 B.C.
Wednesday 4:55 - 6:55 p.m., Marios Anastasiadis 
The social movement of people up and down the socioeconomic ladder is a phenomenon primarily associated with the modern, industrial world. Did it exist in the Greek Mediterranean? And what forms did it take in practice? What role did one’s legal status, sex, occupation, competence, and social networks, among other factors, play in shaping their opportunities, or lack thereof? To answer these questions, this seminar combines contemporary social science research on status, stratification, and inequality with close analysis of ancient evidence. Particular attention will be paid to epichoric differences (i.e., the ways individual city-states chose to regulate, promote, hinder, and react to social mobility), while the phenomenon itself will be studied over the longue durée. Our grounding in the Greek Mediterranean will provide ample opportunity for comparative work, and students will be exposed to different genres of evidence, including epic poetry, comedy, oratory, inscriptions, papyri, historiography, and ancient law and political thought.

CLASS-GA 3002.002 Ancient Logic (Same as PHIL-GA 2320.002)
Thursday 11:00 a.m. - 1:00 p.m., Marko Malink
THIS COURSE ORIGINATES IN THE DEPARTMENT OF PHILOSOPHY
This is an overview of the main developments in formal logic in Greek antiquity. Topics to be covered include: Aristotle’s theory of the categorical syllogism and its application in demonstrative science, Theophrastus' theory of the hypothetical syllogism, Stoic propositional logic, Galen’s account of relational syllogisms, and the reception of Peripatetic and Stoic logic in late antiquity, a comparison between ancient and modern logical theories. Our main text will be the earliest surviving handbook of logic in Greek, Galen’s Introduction to Logic (which we will read in the new edition with commentary by Barnes and Morison). This course is a comprehensive survey of Latin literature from the Republican to Early Imperial era. We will read, translate, and analyze a selection of key works — including Republican comedy, history, philosophy, and oratory as well as Augustan epic and love poetry — to sharpen language skills and broaden our appreciation for major literary characteristics and trends. CLASS-GA 1002 Archaeologies of the Athenian Acropolis
M 4:00-6:00 p.m., Joan Connelly
This course investigates the archaeologies of the Athenian Acropolis through its transformations from early settlement, to Mycenaean citadel, to sacred precinct of Athena, to Late Antique town with Parthenon as Church of the Virgin Mary, to administrative center of Latin Duchy of Athens with Parthenon as the Cathedral Notre Dame D’Athènes, to Ottoman garrison with Parthenon as mosque and Erechtheion as Governor’s harem, to world famous ruin, to archaeological site, to iconic epicenter Western Art and Culture. We will examine the geology, landscape, archaeoastronomy, topography, and topology of the Athenian Acropolis with an eye toward understanding the interrelation of landscape, myth, cult, and ritual. Topics include: the architectural phases of the Acropolis buildings and monuments, their programs of sculptural decoration, their relationships to one another, the foundation myths that lie behind their meanings, and the cult rituals celebrated within the sacred precinct. Issues of reception, projection, and appropriation will be examined as will the history of the conservation and reconstruction of Acropolis buildings. Longstanding efforts to secure the reunification of the Parthenon sculptures will be reviewed within the broader context of global cultural heritage law.

CLASS-GA 3000 Greek Dialects
T 4:55-6:55 p.m., Alexander Forte
This course is an advanced introduction to the linguistic dialects of Greek during the archaic and Classical periods. Expertise in Attic Greek grammar will be assumed. Topics will include the phonological, morphological, and syntactic features of Attic-Ionic, Arcado-Cypriot, Doric, and Aeolic dialects, the position of Mycenaean in the history of Greek, issues of evidence for epichoric and poetic dialects (and their scripts), dialect mixture in early Greek poetry, ancient sociolinguistics, prescriptive dialectology in Hellenistic editing, and competing conceptual models of linguistic filiation, among others.

CLASS-GA 3001 Roman Governmentality
W 4:55-6:55 p.m., Alessandro Battaglia
This course offers a survey of both primary sources and secondary scholarship on the Roman government from the late Republic until the fifth century CE. How did the Romans conceptualize their government? What was the ideological framework underpinning the relationship between Roman citizens and the Republican and later imperial government? How did Roman authors perceive changes in the governing philosophy of the Roman state? By addressing these questions, students will be able to acquire the tool to better understand those institutions and the governing philosophy that continuously characterized Roman life from the Republic until the fall of the Western Roman empire in 476.

CLAS 74100: Desire and the Body in the Ancient World
Prof. Rachel Kousser
Wednesday 4:15 pm-6:15 pm, 3 credits
GC: in-person

At a time when questions of gender and desire are increasingly politically fraught, this course offers a long history of the desirable and desiring body in Greek and Roman art. We will consider whether and how contemporary terms and approaches — e.g. feminist, queer, trans, etc. — can illuminate ancient realities. We’ll look at how Greek and Roman gendered ideals were expressed visually, and also at representations of bodies that were understood as shocking, denigrated, or abject. We’ll analyze both canonical artworks such as Athenian red-figure vase paintings and Roman marble statues, as well as less familiar but significant works such as funerary monuments of working women from Ostia and Gandharan sculptures. The goal is a deeper engagement with the classical human figure, a central subject of Greek and Roman art.

CLAS 71200: Ancient Aesthetics
Prof. Nick Pappas
Monday 6:30 pm-8:30 pm, 3 credits
GC: in-person

The word “aesthetics” is a modern coinage. But interpreted broadly to mean the study of art, beauty, and the experience specific to art and beauty, aesthetics has many ancient precursors. This survey will mainly examine what philosophical authors wrote about beauty, poetry, visual art, and inspiration. After excerpts from Homer, Aristophanes, and other pre-Platonic authors, primary readings come from Plato, Aristotle, “Longinus,” Plotinus, and Clement.

Secondary selections will add overviews of such topics as: the visual arts in antiquity; the appreciation of nature; nudity. The seminar will end with a look at the reception of ancient aesthetics by Aquinas, Shelley (“Defence of Poetry”), and Nietzsche (Birth of Tragedy).

CLAS 71100: Longus' Daphnis and Chloe: The novel, its influences, and its legacy
Prof. David Petrain
Monday 4:15-6:15 pm, 3 credits
GC: in-person

Daphnis and Chloe, with its tale of foundlings who grow up on the island of Lesbos, fall in love, and after several misadventures get married, has been enormously influential since its rediscovery in late 15th-early 16th c. Italy, inspiring not only countless translations and literary adaptations, but in the last century alone illustrations by Marc Chagall, a score by Maurice Ravel, even movies such as The Blue Lagoon. Longus was influenced in turn by a wide range of earlier authors, whose works he responds to and redeploys in the texture of his own novel.

In the first part of the course, we will read Daphnis and Chloe in ancient Greek along with selections from the authors who (may have) influenced Longus, a survey including Homer, the lyric of Sappho, the historiographical prose of Thucydides, and the bucolic poetry of Theocritus, Virgil, and others. Then we will turn to Longus' reception as each student begins work on a research project to be presented to the class toward the end of the semester.

Students should be prepared to work with the ancient Greek language at the graduate level. Students who are not studying Latin will be allowed to complete any Latin readings in translation, where available, or else will be given an alternative assignment. The varied knowledge and expertises that students bring to the course–of other languages, or of art, music, dance, other modes of artistic expression–will help shape our exploration of the novel and its legacy.

CLASS-GA 3002 The Divine Council
Tuesday 4:55-6:55pm, Alessandro Barchiesi
The Divine Council (and its ugly twin, the Council in Hell) is the most long-lasting institution in the history of epic imagination: it has roots in Mesopotamian epic, becomes almost unavoidable in Greek and Roman epic, and has a surprising vitality in Christian epic of the Medieval and early modern era. The class offers a seminar-style discussion of a number of texts in their distinctive approach to the Council: all the original languages will be translated and commented upon by the instructor. Some of the main authors include Homer, Ennius, Virgil, Ovid, Statius, Claudian, Petrarch, Ariosto, Tasso and Milton, plus a number of minor figures. There will be space for discussion in terms of comparison and reception within other cultures, and a debate on which modern texts qualify as continuation of the Council in different genres (James Bond and Spectre plots, conspiracy theories, dystopic novels, and so on). The question of how the fictional council compares to contemporary political institutions will be particularly instructive.

Requirements: interest in epic and other forms of fiction; willingness to offer a presentation and a final paper.

CLAS 71100: Homeric Poetry
Prof. Andrea Kouklanakis
Wednesday 6:30-8:30 pm, 3 credits
GC: in-person

This course welcomes everyone interested in Homeric poetry, those reading Homer for the first time, and those with previous experience with the epic genre. The course aims to develop students’ skills in reading Homeric poetry towards a comprehensive and thorough understanding of the epic genre. Reading Homer effectively is not synonymous with literally translating verses, even as we consider different translation approaches and practices. Instead, we will read as much as possible in Greek, absorb Homeric vocabulary, and learn to rely on the aspects of orality and formulaic language to promote more rapid and more comfortable sight reading. We will pay attention to meter, forms, grammar, syntax, semantics, and style. Our communal, collaborative, and close reading sessions will familiarize our eyes and ears with the epic mode, its language, recurrent themes, and meaning-generating diction. We will discuss foundational questions of Homeric scholarship, such as composition, performance, and audience, as well as interpretive and creative questions, including reception studies. Students will gain confidence in articulating their questions and interpretations based on discreet episodes, scenes, and passages with attention to embedded myths, storytelling, and the interrelatedness of performance and meaning. By the end of this course, students should feel confident about having solid knowledge of Homeric poetry while accepting that there will always be more to learn.

CLAS 71200: Historiography and Ancient Theater
Prof. Melinda Powers
Wednesday 11:45 am-1:45 pm, 3 credits
GC: in-person

Using Euripides’ Bacchae as a case study, this course will introduce students to key methods and problems in studying the historical theatrical event. Through a close study of the ancient sources as well as current scholarship on Athenian drama, students will learn how to identify sources, construct evidence, and create narratives, while investigating the methodological problems that arise in the process of developing arguments based on the limited material documenting the performance of Bacchae in 405 BCE. Asking questions such as how do we know what we know, what are the problems with what we know, and how do we manage the gaps in our understanding, the course will explore the historiographical problems and debates surrounding topics such as, fifth-century theatrical space, audience, performance style, costuming, properties, gesture, and mask. Requirements: weekly discussion questions (Theatre students) or weekly translation (Classics students), one in-class presentation, and a fifteen-page research paper on an approved topic.

CLAS 74100 cross listed with MALS 74400: Collecting the Past: Museums and Antiquities
Prof. Elizabeth Macaulay
Tuesday 4:15-6:15 pm, 3 credits
GC: in-person

Since antiquity, individuals and empires alike have collected antiquities and works of art; The Roman Emperor Nero wanted specific statues for his Domus Aurea, and Queen Christina of Sweden (1626-89) formed one of the most significant collections of Greco-Roman antiquities in Europe which is in the Prado Museum today. Many of these antiquities are now in museums, primarily in Europe and the United States. Debates about where antiquities belong have only intensified in the early twenty-first century. Should the Parthenon Marbles remain in London or return to Athens? Should the Benin Bronzes, if taken during the European conquest of West Africa, be repatriated to Nigeria? This course will investigate the history and theory of museums and collecting–starting with how museums developed from the Renaissance and early modern cabinets of curiosities and private collections to grand universal museums, such as the British Museum and Louvre, which were cultural projects of the Enlightenment and European empires. Specifically, this course will examine how collections of antiquities and historical objects were formed through purchase, excavation, donations, and illicit means. To understand the formation of these collections, we will consider the intersection of Enlightenment mindsets, nationalism, colonialism, anthropology, religion, and archaeology with museums. We will also consider the legal frameworks for owning such objects, the ethics of collecting, the challenges of looting, and the repatriation of specific objects. The UNESCO 1970 treaty and other legal rulings and conventions about the movement of cultural property will also be examined. Visits to particular New York City Museums and/or with curators will be a part of this class to encourage students to understand how museum collections in New York City were formed and the shifting landscapes of ownership in the twenty-first century. By investigating these questions and others, we can try to understand a future for the past.

The Practice of Close Reading
This seminar seeks to demystify a type of literary analysis that often remains unexplained, even though it is a mainstay of academic work in the humanities. CLASS-GA 1002 Archaeologies of the Ptolemaic Mediterranean World
Monday 4:00 p.m. - 6:00 p.m.,
Prof. Joan Connelly
NYU: in-person

This course examines evidence for the Ptolemaic Empire across the last three centuries BCE, from Alexandria and coastal Egypt to the Eastern Mediterranean, Cyprus, Coele Syria, Caria, Lycia, Pamphylia, Cilicia, and on to the Aegean Islands and Cyrenaica. Focusing on cities, harbors, garrison sites, sanctuaries, and cemeteries we will consider interconnectivity, resources, and trade as well as military expansion and the workings of the Ptolemaic army and fleet. Meanwhile, we shall look at interactions between local cultures and the Ptolemaic Imperial presence in the administrative, economic, religious, social, and artistic orbits. The critical role of the Ptolemaic army in city and temple building across the Mediterranean and on the Red Sea will be investigated along with networks of communication, including the utilization of fire beacon towers to transmit signals and aid in nocturnal navigation.

Central to this seminar is the examination of archaeological sites and surviving architectural remains, wall paintings, sculpture, ceramics, inscriptions, papyri, coins, seals, mosaics, metalware and other decorative arts. We will look at the interaction of Greek and Egyptian religious systems, cults, and ritual practice as well as Ptolemaic funerary architecture and burial customs. We will evaluate the methodological constructs that have been employed in the study of Ptolemaic material culture including “Alexandrianism,” “Egyptianness,” “Hellenicity,” “Hybridity,” “Fusion,” “Double Vision,” and “Entanglement.” Finally, we shall look at the interaction of the Ptolemies with late Republican Rome, the consequential reign of Cleopatra VII, and the end of the Ptolemaic Dynasty. This vibrant period in Mediterranean maritime history holds endless fascination and importance for our understanding of wider cultural and religious spheres, economic and social histories, as well as art historical traditions caught between East and West.

In this course, we will explore ancient paratexts. CLAS 71100, Plato and the Foreigner in Philosophy 
Tuesdays 6:30 p.m. - 8:30 p.m. 
Prof. Nickolas Pappas 
In-Person at the GC

The course will build to and then undertake a reading of the Republic, with attention to 1) governance by philosophers in the newly constituted city and 2) the identification of foreigners in the city’s homogeneous population. The question for the course is how the Platonic proposal for governance based on enlightenment intersects with the wish for a unified citizenry. The two come apart when ignorance and knowledge both signify foreign status.

With its hope for governance by philosophers, the Republic represents a landmark attempt to reform both politics and philosophy. The famously impractical subject of philosophy brought to the cynical practicalities of politics: Plato’s Republic assigns itself this task in a bid to re-imagine philosophy so that it engages with politics, and to re-imagine politics in a way that opens it to such engagement.

If governance by philosophy identifies the Republic at its most idealistic, the effort to maintain a unified citizenry reveals a most un-ideal enterprise of purification: the identification of foreigners. In the Republic this will mean identifying natives as alien and expelling them.

Course narrative:

(One session.) Introduction. The first page of Republic Book 1 and the first two pages of Book 5. Thrasymachus and Polemarchus as visitors to the city of words.
(Two sessions.) Two ways of thinking the unknown ones. Outside arrivals in tragedy and the histories. The paradigm of ignorant foreigner in Aeschylus Suppliant Women. Against this arrival consider the knowledgeable stranger with secret information that can help the city: Sophocles Oedipus at Colonus, Herodotus on the Persians’ dialogue with the Ethiopians. From Anacharsis to Solon, the wandering sage fits both paradigms.
(Four sessions.) Strangers in Plato. A survey of the dialogues reveals a range of attitudes expressed toward non-Athenians and other non-Greeks. Recent writings by Rebecca LeMoine and Demetra Kasimis seek to exonerate Plato from charges of xenophobia. The broad sampling of passages includes both derogatory and complimentary references to non-Athenians, as well as efforts at universalism. Among these characterizations of the xenos some assess the outsider as either knowledgeable or ignorant. The itinerant sage/sophist again complicates this picture. Extended readings of passages from Protagoras on secret wisdom elsewhere; Lysis and Meno on slavery; Phaedrus and Timaeus on Egypt; Menexenus on non-Athenian Greek cities.
(Four sessions.) The Republic’s nativism and xenophobia. Within the structure of ideas already established, we can locate the concerns and characterizations in the Republic. Book 1 as conversation with xenoi, and Book 10 with the fates of Greeks and non-Greeks after death, raise the question of the foreigner. The city is established as pure and closed but repeats the gestures of expulsion with playwrights, adults over ten, and other groups. Even the soul contains foreign elements.
(Two sessions.) The philosopher is native yet also alien. As soon as the Republic identifies rulers as philosophers, it reinscribes their native status. The plan for establishing philosophical rule follows the pattern of overthrow by insiders, and rivals to power are depicted as foreign. But the argument also draws on the second tradition of outsider. The allegory of the cave casts the ruler in the role of stranger to the city ruled by philosophy. We read the allegory alongside readings by LeMoine and Andrea Nightingale. In this commonwealth dedicated to unity among citizens, all citizens end up foreign to one another.
(Two sessions.) The rulers’ secret knowledge. The philosophical rulers fit the description of knowledgeable foreigners in their expertise but also in possessing a beneficial secret. This is literal when the rulers are shown coordinating eugenic breeding, but also describes their philosophical understanding of justice. The Oedipus they resemble became a tyrant. We read Sophia Connell’s analysis of the parallels between the Republic’s philosopher and the Greek tyrant. To the foreigners observing, the city’s takeover will make it tyranny. The city will need to keep its philosophical governance a secret, but in doing so it risks abandoning the hope of transforming politics.

Ancient Feasting

CLAS 82500
Prof. Rachel Kousser
The Graduate Center
Thursday 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m.
In-Person

Feasting was central to Greek and Roman culture.  From the elaborate meals of Homer’s heroes, to the aristocratic symposia of Archaic Greece, the courtly banquets of Hellenistic kings, and the outrageous dinner parties of Roman emperors and arrivistes, the communal consumption of food was a key religious, political, and social act.  In recent years, anthropologists such as Michael Dietler have offered a useful theoretical lens through which to analyze feasting, and classicists have focused considerable scholarly attention on some topics, such as the Archaic symposium and Roman convivium.  This class seeks to pull together these strands of research to understand the broader context of ancient feasting and its development over time.

With an eye on the doctoral reading list at the Graduate Center, we will analyze major texts including Homeric epic, Greek sympotic poetry, Plato’s Symposium, and Petronius’ Cena Trimalchionis.  We will also examine historical accounts of important banquets — for instance, those of Alexander the Great and the emperor Nero — and food-centered works like Apicius and Athenaeus’s Deipnosophistai.  Topics to be addressed include sacrifice and communal religious feasts; real and ideal symposia; Classical Greek pottery and drinking; Hellenistic courtly banquets; and the material culture of the Roman convivium.  Students’ final presentations and papers will be case studies, grounded in the material of their choice, that add to our understanding of ancient feasting practices.

This course will investigate Roman legal culture by asking, and answering, two essential questions: How did the Romans go about making law? How was that law implemented? In short, this course is more about something that might be called legal culture.

CLAS 82600 Race, Ethnicity, and Power in the Ancient World

Instructor: Jennifer Roberts
Thu 4:15 PM-6:15 PM, 3 credits
The Graduate Center

This interdisciplinary course will explore concepts of race and ethnicity in the ancient world in  readings in English in both primary and secondary sources, with emphasis on the Greek, Roman, and Hellenistic worlds. No knowledge of Latin or Greek is required, although students who can read either or both of those languages may periodically wish to meet with me for close analysis of a particular text.

Greek and Latin literature is full of references to groups that the authors felt were “not like us.” The Greeks developed the term “barbarians” (people whose incomprehensible speech sounded like bar, bar, bar) for non-Greeks; their feelings about them were mixed, but for the most part they enjoyed articulating their own superiority. In addition, the individual Greek city-states were exclusive about their citizenship, not enfranchising immigrants or the children of immigrants, and a number of them had elaborate myths designed to explain the special characteristics they possessed that set them apart from, and above, others. Matters were more complicated in the later Greek world (the Hellenistic period of 323-30BCE) when the conquests of Alexander had spawned sprawling multi-ethnic empires, and the people we call “the Romans” were a very diverse group faced with a founding legend that painted them as the descendants of criminals and slaves.  The Roman elite was increasingly multi-ethnic as time went on; the emperors Trajan and Hadrian were both from Spain, and reign of the African emperor Septimius Severus—who spoke Latin with an accent--ushered in an era in which emperors came from all over the Mediterranean world. Despite this diversity, Roman authors enjoyed lobbing ethnic slurs at other “nationalities.”

Profiting from our own diverse backgrounds and training, we will examine the very complex picture presented by ancient notions of race and ethnicity, and students will pursue projects that grow out of their particular backgrounds and interests.

Herodotus, The Histories (any translation)
Tacitus, Germania (any translation)
Rebecca Futo Kennedy, C. Sydnor Roy, and Max Goldman, Race and Ethnicity in the Classical World: An Anthology of Primary Sources in Translation (Indianapolis: Hackett, 2013
Denise McCoskey, Race in Antiquity and Its Legacy (London: Bloomsbury Academic, 2012)

CLAS 82800 The “Short” 3rd Century BC in Roman History

Instructor: David Levene
Tue 4:15 PM-6:15 PM, 3 credits
NYU

One of the less documented periods in Roman history is also one of the most important.  Following their victory at Sentinum over a coalition of Italian nations in 295 B.C., the Romans obtained dominance - though far from unchallenged - in central and southern Italy.  Seventy-five years later, on the eve of the Second Punic War, they were not only in effective control of almost the entire Italian peninsula, but they had also made their first forays into overseas conquest and had defeated Carthage to become the most powerful empire in the Western Mediterranean.  The same period saw major cultural changes at Rome, not least the first recorded Latin literature, and also economic changes, including the first introduction of Roman coinage.

In this course we will be examining the various processes that transformed Rome in so short a time from a regional force to a Mediterranean power.  One major focus will be on tracing Rome's military and diplomatic engagement, both hostile and otherwise, with her potential rivals and challengers; we will also consider the way in which the Romans developed networks of power via colonies and alliances in Italy, both formal and informal.  Another important question is how far the Romans had (as our sources sometimes seem to suggest) genuinely overcome the legacy of social and political discord; we will also examine the cultural relationship between Rome and her neighbours inside and outside Italy, and the various influences in both directions.  We will analyze these issues in light of what are still some of the most intensely contested questions in classical scholarship, including the nature of Roman imperialism, the relationship between Rome and her colonies, the extent of aristocratic control over Roman military and political decision-making, and the reasons for the cultural shifts in the city.  We will draw on the widest possible set of evidence, primarily literary, but also archaeological, epigraphic, and numismatic.

CLAS 81800 What is Hellenistic Religion? Texts, Archaeology, Epigraphy

 

Instructor: Barbara Kowalzig
Wed. 4:15 PM-6:15 PM, 3 credits

‘Hellenistic religion’ used to be thought a stepping stone on the way to the monotheisms of the common area, with a focus on individual experience, spirituality and interiority, henotheism, and gradual erosion of communal ritual in the city. Recent research in Hellenistic history and epigraphy has entirely overturned this picture; we now understand that civic religion was alive and well, continuously adapting and transforming itself in the cosmopolitan Mediterranean of the Hellenistic empires while also developing new forms of religious experience, including a different role for emotions and the senses. CLAS 75200 Aristotle and Neo-Aristotelian Virtue Ethics

 

Instructor: Iakovos Vasiliou
Monday and Tuesday, 4:15-6:15 PM, 3 credits
The Graduate Center, Room TBA
[NOTE: This course meets twice a week for half the semester, from March 23 through May 12.]

According to most standard accounts, modern virtue ethics begins with Elizabeth Anscombe's essay "Modern Moral Philosophy" in 1958 and develops over the second half of the twentieth century as an alternative to deontological and consequentialist moral theories.   Rather than obligation as the centerpiece of moral theory, it is commonly held that virtue ethics focuses on human flourishing and the virtues of character that constitute it.  Aristotle is the patron saint of this movement.  Over the last twenty years, some have begun to question what virtue ethics is, how and whether it differs from other types of ethical theory, and even to what extent Aristotle should be called a virtue ethicist.  Some now avoid the term “virtue-ethics” and prefer to speak instead of “Neo-Aristotelian” ethics; under this label one might include the work of Julia Annas, Phillipa Foot, Rosalind Hursthouse, Alasdair MacIntyre, John McDowell, Martha Nussbaum, and Michael Thompson.  While all of these philosophers discuss virtue, only some would identify their positions as belonging to “virtue ethics.”

We shall examine what sort of ethical theory contemporary Neo-Aristotelian ethics is and how it fits with what we actually find in Aristotle.  What does it say about the relationships between agents and actions?  How does it contrast with deontology or consequentialism? What does it mean for ethics to be "virtue-based", "character-based" or "agent-based/centered", as opposed to "rule-based" or "act-based/centered"?  What is the role of (human) nature in Neo-Aristotelian ethics? Does practical reason operate differently in Neo-Aristotelian ethics than in other types of ethical theory?

We will do a close reading of major parts of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, including his discussions of eudaimonia, the virtues of character, practical reason, moral psychology, decision and deliberation, voluntary action, and the unity of virtues.   We will interweave this with readings from secondary literature on the Ethics as well as from the “Neo-Aristotelians” mentioned above.

It will be important for us to work from the same translation of the Nicomachean Ethics.  For various reasons, we shall use Terence Irwin's translation, second edition, Hackett Press; I ask you all to acquire a copy.  We will also consult the Rowe/Broadie translation from Oxford, and the "Revised Oxford Translation" by Ross and revised by Urmson, published in the Complete Works of Aristotle (ed. J. Barnes, Princeton University Press). 

Please read Book I of the Nicomachean Ethics for the first class. First class will meet on 3/23, not 3/17 (there will be a make-up class, TBA). The seminar fulfills Distribution Area C or D-Ancient.  Philosophy students wishing to satisfy Distribution Area D-ancient with this seminar must write a term paper that focuses on Aristotle’s ethics.

CLAS 71800 Thucydides, Politics, Philosophy

 

Instructor: Jennifer Roberts
Mon. 6:30-8:30 PM, 3 credits
The Graduate Center, Room 3306

This interdisciplinary course will be guided in part by the particular interests of the students who choose to enroll in it: historians, classicists, archaeologists, political scientists, philosophers.  Although there will be common readings, students are encouraged to pursue their own perspectives on Thucydides while at the same time coming to appreciate his relevance to other disciplines.  The text will be read in English, but I am happy to meet separately with students who would like to read selections in the original Greek.

A masterpiece of both narrative and analysis, Thucydides’ account of the war between the Athenian Empire and Peloponnesian League also merits study as a work of profound philosophical import.  The work of a man filled with a plangent sense of the sorrows of the human condition, Thucydides’ history offered a non-fiction counterpart to the tragic drama of his contemporaries Sophocles and Euripides.  

The father of political science, Thucydides has often been labeled the father of political realism.  We will explore in what ways this is and is not accurate.  Thucydides has been co-opted by one generation after another, on one continent after another, as a spokesman for its own society and identified as the one person who best understood the problems of the day.  From monarchists to republicans in Europe to 20th and 21st century American neoconservatives, his readers have proudly cited him in defense of their ideologies. Today students of international relations wring their hands over the newly dubbed menace, “the Thucydides trap,” a concept that draws parallels between the diplomatic situation that led up to the Peloponnesian War and America’s growing tensions with China.  Both Thucydides and his legacy will be the subjects of this course.

CLAS 71900 Divinity, Commodity and the Sea in the Mediterranean World

 

Instructor: Barbara Kowalzig
Wed. 4:15 PM-6:15 PM, 3 credits
NYU, Silver Center, Room 503A

This seminar will examine how Greek polytheism engages the economic sphere. How is the divine world implicated in production, supply and economic exchange in the Mediterranean? In particular, it will address the relationship between individual gods or sets of gods and the ‘product’ they are commonly associated with, such as Demeter and the grain, Dionysos and the wine, Athena and the olive etc. A fundamental question will be how a god’s power (‘mode of action’), such as Demeter’s ability to effect ‘production’ and ‘growth’, comes to bear on economic activity and institutions, shaping a city’s political economy, or maritime networks of supply. The course will start by introducing students to the methods used in the study of Greek polytheism, to relevant approaches in economic anthropology and economic theory and to recent work on the Mediterranean as an interconnected historical space. A number of sessions will then be dedicated to the divine world involved in agricultural production, that is to say cult, myth, and ritual associated with the Mediterranean ‘triad’ (grain, olive, wine); followed by mining and metallurgy, slave labour and slave trade; luxury commodities, such as textiles, spices and perfumes. A final part will tackle the mechanisms of divine intervention in economic transactions, i.e. examine the ‘gods of the market place’ (theoi agoraioi); and ‘gods of trade’ and ‘exchange’ such as Hermes and Aphrodite. This course will start on Wednesday, 12 September.

 

CLAS 81800 Modern Views on Ancient Historiography

 

Instructor: Liv Yarrow
Thu. 4:15 PM-6:15 PM, 3 credits
The Graduate Center

Ancient historians were self-conscious creators of narrative.  This course will explore themes traditionally associated with the genre of history in the Greco-Roman world, including, but not limited to: universalism, local histories, the role of speeches, use of sources, intersection with other genres, the role of the historian in society, intrusion of authorial voice, narrative focalization, and explanations of historical causation.  The course will simultaneously examine how these themes and related issues have been treated by both classicists and modern historical theorists.  All general course materials provided in digital format.  Students will each select a different ancient historian on which to focus their individual work for the semester.

CLAS 70200 Latin Rhetoric and Stylistics

 

Permission of EO required
Instructor: Matthew McGowan
Thursday, 4:00-6:00 PM, 3 credits
NYU, Silver Center, Room 503

This course offers an introduction to composition in Latin and a survey of prose styles from Cato the Elder to the Vulgate. Each week we will tackle a different author from D.A. Russell's Anthology of Latin Prose (Oxford) and review individual points of syntax and stylistics via practice exercises and free composition. It is hoped that by the end of the course students will have gained a deeper knowledge of Latin sentence structure and idiom and a greater appreciation for a broad range of prose styles. Weekly assignments (pensa) will include composition and reading from E.C. Woodcock's A New Latin Syntax and from other scholars analyzing a particular author's style. The scholarly essays will provide the background for the brief report (= breviarium) that every student will be asked to do at least once over the course of the semester.

Required Texts (all available on Amazon): D.A. Russell, Anthology of Latin Prose (Oxford) E.C. Woodcock, A New Latin Syntax, and Gildersleeve's Latin Grammar by B. Gildersleeve and G. Lodge. Please bring these books to class on the first day, Thursday, January 18, 2018. If you have any questions about the content or aims of the course, feel free to email me: mamcgowan@fordham.edu.

 

CLAS 72100 Late Latin Verse

 

Instructor: Marco Formisano
Thursday, 2:30-5:00 PM, 3 credits
Fordham, LC Lowenstein

In this course we will read some of the most famous late Latin poems (Ausonius, Mosella; Claudian, De raptu Proserpinae; Rutilius Namatianus, De reditu suo), as well a selection of passages from other poetry by Ausonius, Proba, and Prudentius. While these texts are most often read intertextually with an almost exclusive focus on their relationships with models such as Vergil, Ovid, Horace and Statius, our aim is to study these poems on their own terms, namely as the products of late antique literary culture. Particular attention will be devoted to the concept of allegory, both as an ancient and late-antique textual phenomenon and as a topic of theorizing in twentieth-century literary criticism (e.g. Walter Benjamin, Paul De Man). On April 27, 2018 a workshop with external speakers on “Origins and Original Moments in Late Antique Literature” will take place at Fordham, and the participants in this class will be directly involved in the discussion.

 

CLAS 81100 Presocratic Philosophy

 

Instructor: David Sider
Monday, 6:30-8:30 PM, 3 credits
NYU, Silver Center, Room 503A

In this course we shall survey the path of Greek philosophy from its beginnings until just short of Plato. Most of this will be on natural science (such as evolution, the big bang theory, and subatomic particles, to be anachronistic) and the attempts to grapple with the concept of existence, but what little remains of early philosophical ethics will also be examined. Since all of early Greek thought is known primarily from later sources who quote (not always consistently), paraphrase (often tendentiously), and interpret (often erroneously), it must be approached in the first instance philologically fragment by fragment before being put into historical and then philosophical contexts.

 

CLAS 82100 The Latin Novel

 

Instructor: David Konstan
Tuesday, 4:15-6:15 PM, 3 credits
NYU, Silver Center, Room 503A

Ancient Rome bequeathed to literature three novels of very different character: Petronius’ Satyrica, Apuleius’ Metamorphoses (also known as The Golden Ass, thanks to a citation by Augustine), and the anonymous History of Apollonius King of Tyre.  We shall read most (if not all) of these texts in Latin, and discuss various points of Latin style, narrative technique, and social context.  A final paper is required, due at the end of the semester.

 

CLAS 74100 Archaeologies of the Maritime World

 

Instructor: Joan Breton Connolly
Monday, 4:15-6:15 PM, 3 credits
NYU, Silver Center, Room 503A

How has archaeological investigation, both underwater and terrestrial, deepened our understanding of ancient seafaring, colonization, commerce, social interaction, pilgrimage, warfare, and piracy?  How did connectivity among far-flung ports and coastal Greek cities contribute to the emergence of a Hellenic cultural identity?  This seminar examines the maritime cultural landscape of the Mediterranean with special focus on material culture.  From the Uluburun cargo to the Antikythera shipwreck, from Phoenician purple-dye production to late Roman fish tanks, we will consider the connectivity of maritime industries, economies, visual arts, and social practices.  Special topics to be examined: climate, time-cycles, technological aspects of seafaring, ship construction, navigation, innovation, insularities, “maritime small worlds”, coastscapes, opportunistic ports, cabotage, and connections with the hinterland. Readings cover ancient authors (literature, histories, geographies); historiographic works (Braudel, Horden and Purcell, Abulafia, Broodbank); period- and region-specific works (Malkin, Tartaron, Leidwanger, Foley, Bass).

 

CLAS 71800 Herodotus

 

Instructor: Danielle Kellogg
Wednesday, 4:15 PM, 3 credits
The Graduate Center, Room 3310B

This course will focus on Herodotos, one of the greatest prose authors of ancient Greece and a crucial figure in Greek intellectual and literary history. Herodotos’ Histories is one of the earliest lengthy prose narratives in Ionian Greek still extant and remains the primary (and often only) source for much information about the culture and history of numerous ancient Mediterranean cultures in the late Archaic and early Classical periods. Herodotos’ status as the ‘Father of History’ has been debated in the 2500 years since he set himself the task of recording the ‘great and wonderful deeds’ of the Greeks and Persians: while Herodotos’ investigation of these events set the precedent for all subsequent historical writings, his aims and methods have been controversial since antiquity (just witness the rather uncomplimentary opinions of his near contemporary, Thucydides). Herodotos wove a number of fabulous and entertaining anecdotes and tales into his historical narrative, leading some to refer to him the ‘Father of Lies’ and even to question his status as a historian at all. Current scholarly work, however, mostly treats Herodotos’ Histories both as a carefully crafted literary work in which the supposed ‘digressions’ link together many of Herodotos’ themes and ideas, and as a genuine attempt by the author to record and explain events of the past. The course will advance the students’ fluency in reading and translating Greek prose, while at the same time enriching their understanding of Greek civilization and history and exposing them to current trends in Herodotean scholarship.

CLAS 75200 Latin Sight Reading Workshop

 

Instructor: Dee Clayman
Wednesday, 3:00-4:00 PM, pass/fail, 1 credit
The Graduate Center, Room 6300

Learning goals: Students will be able to translate a Latin passage at sight at a level appropriate for accomplished MA and/or PhD students; students will pass the MA or PhD examinations in Latin translation at the first attempt.
Assessment: student performance in class; the MA or PhD examination in Latin translation

Who should take the course: The course is ideal for students preparing to take a Latin translation exam (MA or PhD), but is not limited to such students. A knowledge of basic Latin grammar and vocabulary is assumed. Any student enrolled in an MA or PhD program in classics or ancient history may register until the course limit is reached. Please consult the instructor if you are uncertain whether you would benefit from this course.

The class will meet for 1 hour each week. Regular attendance is required. The instructor will bring to class each week texts selected from the works of authors on and off the reading list, with an emphasis on prose. Students will be challenged to translate them on the spot in writing and discussion will follow of strategies for producing accurate and literal translations.
