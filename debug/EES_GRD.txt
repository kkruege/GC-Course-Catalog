EES 79903 [55707] Geographies of Peace and Conflict

Schedule: Tuesdays, 11:45 a.m.– 1:45 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. TBA (In-Person Only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructor: Prof. Nerve Macaspac
Enrollment: Course open to all GC Students

While geography historically served the mechanics of war and empire, recent decades have seen scholars establish peace as an essential geographic concept, keyword, and site of critical inquiry. This PhD seminar interrogates the complex challenges to peace amidst contemporary, cascading, and systemic crises. Through a close reading of core geographic frameworks and spatial methodologies, we will examine the conceptual and analytical friction and alignments between peace geographies, abolition geographies, and the broader “spatial turn” in peace and conflict studies. Students will actively engage with current debates, field-based research, and emerging topics related to peace and conflict, with the seminar serving as a collaborative forum to chart the future horizons and possibilities of peace geographies.

EES 79903 [55710] Solidarity Cities

Schedule: Tuesdays, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. TBA (In-Person only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructor: Prof. Marianna Pavlovskaya
Enrollment: Course open to all GC students.

The new course “Solidarity Cities” will examine how people in the U.S. and around the world have been building cities (and beyond) based on solidarity rather than profit and competition. In doing so, they have collectively shaped urban spaces according to their needs and dreams. Is the production of solidarity-based urban space and related struggles a marginal utopian project? Or is it a widespread yet invisible practice rooted in social reproduction, livelihoods, and mutual aid? Although racial capitalism and neoliberal forces appear to have fully defined our cities, people have long drawn on various forms of solidarity (economic, social, cultural, and political) to remake urban areas—despite all barriers—into cities of collective resistance to harm, cities of collective consumption and public goods, and cities of cooperation and mutual aid.

EES 79903 [55712] Political Ecology of Social and Environmental Justice

Schedule: Tuesdays, 4:15 p.m. – 6:15 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. TBA (In Person only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructors: Prof. Rebio Diaz Cardona
Enrollment: Course open to all GC students [Required EP]

This seminar examines classic and contemporary works on how social and environmental injustice is produced, understood, and resisted. It offers a broad overview of social theory relevant to the study of people-environment relations, tracing connections to contemporary environmental and climate justice scholarship and activism. Using a political ecology framework, we will pay attention to how the unequal distribution of environmental harms and benefits is produced at various scales and in locations across the globe, with a focus on how individuals and communities experience, understand, and respond to environmental degradation and climate change.

We will consider a range of practices and theoretical understandings from north and south to explore ethical, methodological, and epistemological debates in political ecology, as well as political ecological factors (including spatial, cultural, technological, and psychological factors) that enable and constrain the just futures that scholars, activists, and communities imagine, as they reconfigure relations to nature, cities, the earth, and themselves through their struggles for social, epistemic, environmental, and climate justice.

EES 77903 [55713] Social Reproduction

Schedule: Wednesdays, 4:15 p.m. – 615 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. TBA (In-Person Only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructor: Prof. Cindi Katz
Enrollment: Course open to all GC students

Social reproduction is a geographically and historically differentiated process encompassing the production of a stratified labor force and the cultural forms and practices that create and maintain its differences while making them common sense. It involves both the sensuous labor of everyday life and the structured practices that unfold in dialectical relation to production. These material social practices take place across geographic and temporal scales, encompassing daily and generational relations that are as much intimate as global in their effects and charge. Social reproduction also involves the reproduction of the material grounds and conditions of production, including the conservation of environmental resources and the concerns of environmental justice. The work of social reproduction is racialized, gendered and class specific, and is accomplished within a contested and shifting amalgam that includes the family, household, state, workplace, and institutions of civil society. The critical inquiry of these concerns and the materials social practices associated with social reproduction will be our focus in this seminar.

EES 79903 [55714] Environmental Justice Policies and Practices

Schedule: Wednesdays, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. TBA (In-Person Only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructor: Prof. Tammy Lewis
Enrollment: Course open to all GC students

This course focuses on the distributional dimensions of environmental degradation and environmental protection globally, domestically and in New York City. We will explore the social processes that generate synergistic race and class stratification, and how they impact the distribution of ecological costs and benefits. Substantive areas of focus will include the class and racial segregation of urban neighborhoods, the siting of hazardous facilities in communities of color, unequal protection of environmental health, unequal distribution of urban environmental amenities, and climate injustice. The course will also examine the social origins and impacts of a distinct environmental justice movement that emerged from BIPOC and working-class communities that are most at risk from climate change. Both successes and ongoing struggles in the EJ movement will be explored, with emphasis on NYC.

EES 79903 [55716] Uneven Futures: Speculative Fictional Strategies for Collective Survival

Schedule: Tuesdays, 11:45 a.m. – 1:45 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. TBA (In Person only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructor: Prof. Ashley Dawson
Enrollment: Course open only to EES students

Science fiction author William Gibson once remarked that the future is already here – it just isn’t evenly distributed. It doesn’t take much to extrapolate Gibson’s comment about the unequally distribution of futurity to a global geographical plane characterized by intensifying combined and uneven development. But are the core capitalist countries – the so-called West – still the domicile of futurity? How might the implied temporal, spatial, and developmental hierarchies in Gibson’s quip by inverted? How can temporality be decolonized, and what role can fiction play in this effort to imagine alternative radical futures?

This seminar examines works of speculative fiction that engage in what Kodwo Eshun argues is the “chronopolitical act” of intervening in the production and distribution of futurity. To dismantle Western narratives of progress and modernity that are premised on the historical subjugation of people of color is also to confound the Western dichotomy between the real and unreal, the scientific and the supernatural. The seminar will, therefore, be filled with spirits, ghosts, and ancestral intelligence (AI) that circulates in the present and imparts lessons about the future. There will also, of course, be lots of cyborgs. We will attend to the long legacy of genres like Afrofuturism while also investigating Africanfuturism and speculative fictions set in other parts of the Global South. Of key interest will be recent works of postcolonial cli-fi that attempt to think through strategies of collective survival in the face of the climate emergency, which is unfolding with greatest ferocity in the Global South. Foundational to all our discussions will be investigation of the way in which aesthetic genres warp, morph, and are reborn under the pressure of the quest to forge new futures.

Some of the authors and critics whose work we are likely to discuss include Octavia Butler, T.J. Demos, Cherie Dimaline, Amal El-Mohtar, Kodwo Eshun, Mark Fisher, Amitav Ghosh, Megha Majumdar, Masande Ntshanga, M.E. O’Brien and Eman Abdelhadi, Nnedi Okorafor, Rebecca Roanhorse, Rosaura Sánchez and Beatrice Pita, Boualem Sansal, Tade Thompson!

EES 79903 [55719] The US Imperial Crisis and the Horizons of Emancipation

Schedule: Thursdays, 11:45 a.m. – 1:45 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. 6300 (In Person only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructor: Prof. Jeff Maskovsky
Enrollment: Course open to EES students only. Instructor permission required. Students should email one paragraph explaining how the course fits into their studies to Jeffmaskovsky@gc.cuny.edu

What kind of critical work on the United States should scholars produce to defend precarious lives amidst an ongoing US imperial crisis? How might we advance political stances that illuminate the horizons of emancipation and contribute to the end of oppression, violence, and exploitation in all their forms? This course seeks to address these questions by foregrounding recent contributions from American studies, Black diasporic studies, critical ethnic studies, Native studies, literary studies, anthropology, and history on the relations of capitalism and colonialism through which the United States is produced and reproduced as a concrete social formation. Working to unsettle the methodological nationalism and American exceptionalism that are hallmarks of conventional American accounts, we seek instead to reconsider "the United States" by treating it as a problematic, contradiction-laden space concealing global operations of colonial and imperial power. Extending the interventions by scholars working on racial capitalism, the Atlantic and Pacific worlds, gendered racial terror, settler colonialism, the violence of liberal humanism, and related reassessment and reorientation concerning liberalism, humanity, land, enclosure, containment, labor, property, territory, empire, dispossession, freedom, sovereignty, modernity, and other key words, we will work together to uncover the death-dealing logics and practices that define the contemporary imperial crisis. And we will explore the substance and limits of abolitionist and other counter-hegemonic political projects, asking what they demand of us as engaged scholars and how their emancipatory horizons must change to address the shifting dynamics of imperial decline.

EES 70900 [53280]: Geographic Thought and Theory

Schedule: Wednesdays, 11:45 a.m. – 1:45 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. 4419 (In-Person only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructor: Prof. Alex Moulton
Enrollment: Course open to EES students only. Other programs register with Instructor Permission. [Required for 1st year Geography].

The geographer’s task is to identify and explain co-constitutive interdependencies that write the world at all scales. As such, Geographic Thought and Theory examines the contemporary practice of human geography across a broad range of methodologies. This course considers ideas and debates about the nature of the geographical imagination and with a particular focus on epistemological challenges which question who is imagined as a geographical theorist and what are considered the sources of spatial theory. Environment, climate, gender, race, class, territoriality, citizenship, region, scale, and capitalism are key categories of analysis for the course.

This course explores the philosophy and practice of research through the interdisciplinary lenses of Environmental and Geological Sciences, Geography, and Environmental Psychology. A central goal is to bridge the gap between the Natural and Social Sciences by fostering a culture of communication and collaboration, drawing on the collective expertise and scholarship of EES faculty, students, and alumni.

Beyond approaches to research, the course serves as a dedicated support system for incoming students. We provide a roadmap for navigating the doctoral journey, from tackling key PhD milestones to cultivating healthy advising relationships and effective teaching practices. Each session is framed as a collaborative discussion, including analysis of EES Colloquium presentations. Students are encouraged to step back from their immediate research and bring their unique backgrounds, interests, and curiosity to the table to build a more inclusive, cross-disciplinary community.

EES 79903 [53351]: Law, Place, and Justice

Schedule: Mondays, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. 3306 (In-Person only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructor: Prof. Jean Carmalt
Enrollment: Course open to all GC students.

How does law create inequality through its production of place and space? Conversely, how might the spatial dimension of injustice be incorporated into law that flattens or ignores the socio-economic construction of place? This course explores these questions through the lens of critical legal geography, socio-legal studies, and critical legal theory. It takes an explicitly global approach, drawing from scholarship that is both from and about diverse local contexts alongside scholarship focused on the international dimensions of law and social change. Students will be asked to lead discussion and submit a final paper that reflects their research interests.

EES 79903 [53348]: Nature Based Solutions

Schedule: Tuesdays, 4:15 p.m. – 6:15 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. 3309 (In Person only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructors: Dr. Kyle McKay & Prof. Brett Branco
Enrollment: Course open to GC students.

This seminar-based course will explore the growing use of nature-based solutions for coastal management, which are often proposed in the context of simultaneously managing multiple crises related to biodiversity loss, climate change, aging infrastructure, and inequity of natural hazards. Students will explore these topics through reading peer-reviewed literature, agency and project reports, and other resources on the subject. The instructors will guide the class through a critical examination of NBS and their efficacy for accomplishing societal goals, challenges in project execution, and scientific uncertainties. When possible, discussions will include practitioners working in this space, who can provide insights on career trajectories, personal experiences, and disciplinary expertise. This interdisciplinary-oriented course is intended for students from multiple disciplines including environmental sciences, physical sciences, ecology, environmental design, natural resources, marine sciences, anthropology, economics, engineering, and other relevant and related fields. Upon completion of this course, students will be able to:

Define and describe nature-based solutions for addressing coastal challenges.
Understand the role of both ecological and built systems water management.
Critically analyze the complex interactions between natural systems and built infrastructure within the context of water resource management.
Foster an interdisciplinary approach (and dialog) by integrating knowledge from diverse fields to enhance natural infrastructure practices.

EES 79903 [53350]: Energy and Democracy

Schedule: Wednesdays, 4:15 p.m. – 6:15 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. 6494 (In Person only)

EES 79903 [53346]: Inhabiting Digital Worlds

Schedule: Thursdays, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m.
Location: At the GC, Rm. 7314 (In Person Only)
Credits: 3 credits
Instructor: Prof. Setha Low
Enrollment: Course open to GC students.

This seminar explores the ways that a range of digital technologies, AI developments, and virtual reality are restructuring our social lives, psychological life worlds, and everyday lived spaces. The readings are organized around the various scales of embodied experience, beginning with the self, body, and home, and then expanding to encompass the community and neighborhood, city and region, and finally the globe and universe. Graduate students in the social sciences and humanities will be challenged to rethink the physical versus the virtual and instead begin the process of theorizing a hybrid concept of reality. Important entry points into the analysis include theories of embodiment, space and place, and social justice, as well as critical examinations of the financialization and marketization of these digital worlds based on the harvesting of embodied and behavioral traces. Readings will be drawn from a wide range of sources that require in-depth reading of ethnographic accounts, journalist reporting, novels, as well as political critiques by major figures in the field. Please contact Professor Low for more information. A syllabus will be available a week before classes begin.

EES 71700 [50795] Earth Systems II 
At the GC: T, 4:15 p.m. – 6:15 p.m., Rm. 3309, 3 credits, Profs. Branco/Cherrier, Course open to EES Students only [Required EGS]

This seminar-style course is divided into two parts: (1) The Hydrosphere led by Prof. Branco; and (2) Earth’s Biosphere and Ecosystem Dynamics led by Prof. Cherrier. The objectives of this semester of the Earth Systems sequence are to 1) continue to develop a strong foundational understanding of Earth’s systems, with a focus on the fundamental concepts governing Earth’s hydrosphere and the biosphere, to 2) understand the dynamic interconnected between Earth’s spheres and their processes (geosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere), 3) understand the interplay between natural variability and human activities and the negative effects of these activities on Earth’s systems and what practices can be employed to offset these effects.

Specifically, Students will:

Understand the exchange between important dynamics within different components of the hydrosphere, including atmospheric water, groundwater, rivers and lakes, and oceans.
Understand how human activities can alter the exchange between important dynamics within components of the hydrosphere.
Understand the basic structure and circulation of oceans.
Understand how the hydrosphere interacts with the lithosphere, atmosphere and biosphere.
Understand the concept of ecosystems and how they are structured and function (i.e., energy and material flow)
Understand the ecological roles played by different types of organisms in ecosystems (food web dynamics)
Understand the global distributions ecosystems and biomes and the factors that define their structure and distribution
Understand how current human impacts to Earth’s climate and environment threatens our health and well-being and what sustainable practices individuals and society as a whole can employ to offset these impacts

EES 79903 [55980] The Commons
At the GC: M, 11:45 a.m. – 1:45 pm, 3 cr., Rm. 5383, Prof. Dawson, Course Open to EES students only

From Chiapas to Occupy, from the Gezi Park uprising to disaster communism during the pandemic, acts of commoning have been central to new political imaginaries and formations over the last decades. This seminar will explore contemporary processes of – and resistance to - capitalist and neocolonialist enclosure. Our conversations will be oriented around three key theoretical and political interventions. The first is the assertion that enclosure and extraction pertain not just to material things like land and minerals but also to relatively immaterial social resources such as information, culture, and even affect. The corollary of this, and the second key theoretical hypothesis of the seminar, is the idea that the commons is not solely a thing but a social practice. The commons, in other words, is the space of social relation created in and through acts of mutual aid and solidarity. Lastly, we will explore the extent to which commoning presents political possibilities beyond the stale opposition between the vampiric free market and top-down state power.

Among the authors, activists, and theorists whose work we are likely to discuss are the following: Chris Abani, Sarah Brouillette, Octavia Butler, Pierre Dardot & Christian Laval, Bernadine Evaristo, Silvia Federici, Amitav Ghosh, Guerrilla Media Collective, Jennifer Haigh, Garrett Hardin, Fredric Jameson, Michael Hardt & Toni Negri, Peter Linebaugh, Sandro Mezzadra & Brett Neilson, Timothy Mitchell, Fred Moten & Stefano Harney, José Esteban Muñoz, Elinor Ostrom, Arundhati Roy, Raja Shehadeh, Olivia Sudjic, Latife Tekin, Eyal Weizman.

EES 79903 [52520] Participatory Democracy and Social Movements
At the GC: W, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m., Rm. TBA, 3 credits, Prof. Su, Course open to GC students

This seminar takes a look at what ordinary citizens do to shape public policies and engage in politics — in ways other than voting. We explore the notion that popular participation can make democratic governance more legitimate, fair, and effective. We examine theories and existing evidence on the promises and challenges of participatory democracy — alternatively called bottom-up participation, maximal democracy, or direct democracy. Specifically, we will examine forms and functions of civil society from a comparative perspective by looking at specific examples of (1) participatory institutions (neighborhood councils, urban budgeting, school governance, etc.), (2) participation in non-governmental organizations and development projects, and (3) social movements around the world (potential cases include landless people’s movements, transnational networks, mothers of political dissidents who have “disappeared,” AIDS protest groups, etc.). How much should ordinary citizens participate in policymaking, and how? Under what circumstances? EES 79903 [52522] Beach Politics: Social, Racial, and Environmental Injustice
At the GC: R, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m., Rm. TBA, 3 credits, Prof. Low, Course open to GC students

This seminar explores the beach—the liminal space between land and water--as a site of racial exclusion, environmental and cultural vulnerability, and the privatization. Beaches, that in the US and most of the world, are considered a public resource for everything from recreation to a workplace, are increasingly being closed off. In the US historically racial exclusion made it difficult, if impossible, for POC to use their traditional spaces and Black beaches were taken by eminent domain and developed for solely white use. Today, exclusions include anyone who might want to fish, sunbath, walk, or rest on public land that has private owners nearby who use illiberal means to restrict access. Planning and design of towns with beaches has further these exclusionary practices.

Equally important, beaches are fragile ecological spaces and increasingly being misused and misappropriated, closing them off to people who need them to a living. Issues of sexuality, sexual orientation, body practices, religion, and many other kinds of exclusions continue to plague strategies has accelerated the inability to access places that were once foundational for those who lived there.

The seminar will highlight at least 5 issues, and their understanding constitute our learning goals:

Race, class, gender and ability exclusion and inclusion
Environmental fragility, sustainability and climate change
Bodies, sexuality and social control of coastal public space
Historical practices, cultural resources and contemporary use conflicts
Privatization, urban development and residential governance strategies

Course requirements:

Read and discuss readings in class
Lead a section of the class focused on their interest area

EES 79903 [53769] Black Marxism
At the GC: R, 11:45 a.m.– 1:45 p.m., Rm. 4419, 3 credits, Prof. Wilder, Course open to EES Students only

This course will examine a transnational lineage of distinguished Black Marxists who were notable as both thinkers and political actors. It unfolds from a cluster of questions that will inform our discussions through the semester.

Is there something distinctive about the kind of Marxism – questions, topics, concepts, methods --- elaborated by this lineage of Black thinkers who identified as Marxists? Insofar as we identify a coherent tradition of Black Marxism, how does it relate to other currents of Marxism that were contemporaneous to it? How does it relate to other contemporaneous currents of non-Marxist Black radicalism? What is the relationship between Black Marxism and various Communist Parties or the Comintern? How should we understand Black thinkers whose work was deeply inspired and inflected by Marxism, but who did not identify as Marxists? How do these thinkers help us to understand a recent tendency to treat Marxism as a Eurocentric form of thought that is not relevant to the lived experiences or freedom struggles of racialized, colonized, or non-Western people? EES 79903 [48298] The Idiocy of Urban Life: Urban Geographies of Alienation and Class Formation
GC: T, 11:45 a.m. – 1:45 p.m., In Person, Rm. 4419, 3 credits, Prof. Attoh, Course open to EES students only

Writing in the mid-19th century and amidst the growth of the first industrial cities, Marx and Engels predicted the collapse of industrial capitalism at the hands of an emergent global proletariat. In their schema, cities played an important role. To the extent that industrial capitalism had given rise to ever more “enormous cities,” such cities, they argued in the Communist Manifesto, were invariably poised to “rescue a considerable part of the population from the idiocy of rural life.” As many have argued -- from Eric Hobsbawm to Hal Draper, and John Bellamy Foster -- Marx and Engels were not suggesting that rural life was stupid, but rather that rural life was inherently isolating and inimical to collective politics. Borrowing from the ancient Greek word “idiotes,” Marx and Engels were using idiocy in its classic sense, as a synonym for “privatized isolation.” In the Athenian city, to be an idiotes was not only to be a “private person withdrawn from public concerns,” but to be alienated from the wider public and the democratic process itself. In contrast to the “privatized isolation” of rural life, the industrial city promised precisely the opposite -- it promised “revolutionary combination” and the conditions for class consciousness.

As the course title suggests, this class begins by turning Marx and Engels' conception of rural idiocy on its head. Rather than rural idiocy, our focus in the class will be on developing a theory of “urban idiocy” and exploring the urban geography of both alienation and class formation. Drawing on the work of geographers, sociologists, anthropologists, political scientists, and historians, this course will seek to develop and theorize the idiocy of urban life as an idea and framework within the subdiscipline of urban geography.

EES 79903 [53527] History and Theory of Socially Engaged Art
At the GC:  M, 2:00 p.m. – 5:00 p.m., Rm. 6417, 3 credits, Prof. Sholette, Course open to all GC students

An increasing number of artists, curators, and critics have recently turned their energies toward a new type of participatory socially engaged art making. What had previously been marginalized is now gaining more mainstream attention, with a new prominence in museums, biennials, but also on the streets and other public spaces. Even the New York Times has hailed the emergence of this tendency as “social practice art.”  The aim of this seminar is to survey, critique and historicize the theory and practice of social practice art as well as activist, interventionist, public, participatory and community based art operating within and across fields such as performance, urban studies, environmental science and other socially engaged disciplines. The class will focus on such questions as: Why is it useful, even necessary, to understand the history and theory of social practice art? Where should we look to find the historical roots of social practice art? Are these within the history of art, or external to it, or crossing a line between two spheres of cultural interpretation and understanding? And what is the “social”? In an increasingly privatized society how do we define and operate within a concept of the public sphere? And how are both mainstream and alternative types of cultural institutions responding to the increasing interest in socially engaged art by emerging artists? Through lectures, readings, discussions and student research presentations we will seek to position socially-engaged visual culture and the shifting role of the artist within an historical, ideological, and critical framework. If possible, guest speakers and offsite visits will also be added as available.

EES 79903 [53532] Participatory Democracy and Social Movements
At the GC:  T, 11:45 a.m. – 1:45 p.m., Rm. 8203, 3 credits, Prof. Su, Course open to EES Students only

This seminar takes a look at what ordinary citizens do to shape public policies and engage in politics— in ways other than voting. We explore the notion that popular participation can make democratic governance more legitimate, fair, and effective. We examine theories and existing evidence on the promises and challenges of participatory democracy— alternatively called bottom-up participation, maximal democracy, or direct democracy. Specifically, we will examine forms and functions of civil society from a comparative perspective by looking at specific examples of (1) participatory institutions (neighborhood councils, urban budgeting, school governance, etc.), (2) participation in non-governmental organizations and development projects, and (3) social movements around the world (potential cases include landless people’s movements, transnational networks, mothers of political dissidents who have “disappeared,” AIDS protest groups, etc.). Sometimes, these three categories blur into one another. We will try to focus on case studies in “Global South” middle-income countries alongside domestic and European ones. How much should ordinary citizens participate in policymaking, and how? Under what circumstances? We pay particular attention to crucial tensions in on-going struggles, such as questions of authority and epistemic justice, contradictions in relationships between civil society and the state, and co-optation and non-reformist reforms.

EES 79903 [53534] Tech and the City:  Space, Power and Resistance
At the GC:  R, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m., Room 3212, 3 credits, Profs. Wolf-Powers/Zukin, Course Open to all GC students

Urban "tech” is a complex and contested arena of economic, political, and social change. Just as important, it is a potent spatial and social imaginary. In this course, we critically examine the discourse, organizations, and geography that are shaping digital technology in New York and other cities around the globe and try to imagine better practices.

New York offers an important site to understand urban tech because the city is not only the second-largest “startup ecosystem” in the world, after the San Francisco Bay Area, it presents a challenging array of groups and interests in constant contention for resources, influence, and power.  The course provides an overview of urban tech’s emplacement in New York by examining the formation of the tech ecosystem and its workforce, the impact of the platform economy through Airbnb, Uber, and Yelp, and the control of land, neighborhoods, and people through zoning, surveillance, and artificial intelligence. We will also explore how technology offers contexts for resistance to dominant political and cultural narratives and relationships and pushback against the hyper-accumulation of wealth.

All class sessions are seminar-type discussions. Researchers who have published on specific topics will be invited to visit those class sessions to discuss their work with students. Each week, based on the reading assignments, students will submit two significant questions for discussion in the next class session.  Through the semester, students will develop individual or collaborative research from “seed” projects to final “pitches” showing an interdisciplinary understanding of the field, culminating in a final, short, written research project on a single current issue in the field.

This course is cross-listed in geography/EES and sociology; students from all disciplines and programs are welcome.

Learning Goals/Outcomes: Students will learn to identify actors, interests, and processes that shape the urban context of the contemporary digital economy, to analyze their interactions, and to critique their shortcomings. They will also develop organizational skills to create and collaborate on research projects and to communicate research questions and findings in a publicly accessible way.

Assessment:  Students are expected to contribute two significant questions for discussion in each class session, to make substantive contributions to each class discussion, to create and present one short “seed” project by the midpoint of the course, and to create, present, and write up one final, cumulative research project that may be developed from the earlier “seed” project.

EES 79903 [54682] Beach Politics:  Racial, Social, and Environmental Injustice on the Coastline
At the GC:  R, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m., Rm. TBA, 3 credits, Prof. Low, Course open to EES Students only

This seminar explores the beach—the liminal space between land and water--as a site of racial exclusion, environmental and cultural vulnerability and the privatization.  Beaches, that in the US and most of the world, are considered a public resource for everything from recreation to a workplace, are increasingly being closed off.  In the US historically racial exclusion made it difficult, if impossible, for POC to use their traditional spaces and Black beaches were taken by eminent domain and developed for solely white use.  Today, exclusions include anyone who might want to fish, sunbath, walk, or rest on public land that has private owners nearby who use illiberal means to restrict access.  Planning and design of towns with beaches has further these exclusionary practices.

Equally important, beaches are fragile ecological spaces and increasingly being misused and misappropriated, closing them off to people who need them to a living.  Issues of sexuality, sexual orientation, body practices, religion, and many other kinds of exclusions continue to plague strategies has accelerated the inability to access places that were once foundational for those who lived there.

The seminar will highlight at least 5 issues including: (1) Race, class, gender and ability exclusion and inclusion (2) Environmental fragility, sustainability and climate change (3) Bodies, sexuality and social control of coastal public space (4) Historical practices, cultural resources and contemporary use conflicts (5) Privatization, urban development and residential governance strategies.

EES 79903 [54687] Participatory Democracy and Social Movements
At the GC:  T, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m., Rm. TBA, 3 credits, Prof. Su, Course open to EES Students only

This seminar takes a look at what ordinary citizens do to shape public policies and engage in politics— in ways other than voting. We explore the notion that popular participation can make democratic governance more legitimate, fair, and effective. We examine theories and existing evidence on the promises and challenges of participatory democracy— alternatively called bottom-up participation, maximal democracy, or direct democracy. Specifically, we will examine forms and functions of civil society from a comparative perspective by looking at specific examples of (1) participatory institutions (neighborhood councils, urban budgeting, school governance, etc.), (2) participation in non-governmental organizations and development projects, and (3) social movements around the world (potential cases include landless people’s movements, transnational networks, mothers of political dissidents who have “disappeared,” AIDS protest groups, etc.). Sometimes, these three categories blur into one another. We will try to focus on case studies in “Global South” middle-income countries like Brazil, Argentina, India, and South Africa, though we also include domestic cases as a point of reference. How much should ordinary citizens participate in policymaking, and how? Under what circumstances?

EES 79903 [57052] The Right to the City:  origins, theory debates
At The School of Professional Studies:  T, 6:15 pm – 8:45 pm, Rm. TBA, 3 credits, In Person, Prof. Attoh, Course open to EES Students only

This course aims to introduce students to the concept of the right to the city. Over the course of the semester students will explore the concept’s intellectual origins, its leading proponents, and its mobilization in current debates over justice and equity in cities. Coined in the 1960s by the French philosopher Henri Lefebvre, the concept of the right to the city has enjoyed something of a renaissance. Increasingly, it has found its way into literature in urban studies on everything from participatory planning to issues of public space and transportation. The goal of this class is not only to explore the roots of the concept, but also to assess its utility and to engage its critics. This will mean exploring what adherents of the right to the city concept mean by rights, how they understand the city, as well as how they understand justice. While the first half of the class will focus on sketching out the intellectual roots of the concept, the last half will explore what the concept looks like on the ground. This will mean engaging organizers and activists who have drawn on the concept in organizing efforts around affordable housing, utilities, and transportation.

EES 79903 [55086] Phase II Environmental Site Assessments
At City College:  T, 6:00 p.m. – 8:30 p.m., Marshak Bldg., Rm., 829, 3 credits, Prof. Lampousis, Course open to EES students only

The purpose of this course is to introduce students to good commercial and customary practices in the United States of America for conducting Phase II environmental site assessments (ESA). A Phase II ESA is an evaluation process for confirming and quantifying the presence of hazardous substances or petroleum products in environmental media (i.e., soil, rock, groundwater, surface water, air, soil gas, sediment) throughout a contaminated site. A Phase II ESA typically includes a determination through field screening and chemical testing of the geological, hydrogeological, hydrological, and engineered aspects of the site that influence the presence of hazardous substances or petroleum products (e.g., migration pathways, exposure points) and the existence of receptors and mechanisms of exposure. Students are automatically enrolled in the 40-hour OSHA HAZWOPER (Hazardous Waste Operations and Emergency Response Standard) certification program which applies to employees who are engaged in clean-up operations that are conducted at uncontrolled hazardous waste sites.

EES 79903 – [52419] Social Reproduction 
GC:     W, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m., In Person, 3 credits, Prof. Katz, Course Open to EES students only

Social reproduction is a geographically and historically differentiated process encompassing the production of a stratified labor force and the cultural forms and practices that create and maintain its differences while making them common sense. It involves both the sensuous labor of everyday life and the structured practices that unfold in dialectical relation to production. These material social practices take place across geographic and temporal scales, encompassing daily and generational relations that are as much intimate as global in their effects and charge. Social reproduction also involves the reproduction of the material grounds and conditions of production, including the conservation of environmental resources and the concerns of environmental justice.  The work of social reproduction is racialized, gendered and class specific, and is accomplished within a contested and shifting amalgam that includes the family, household, state, workplace, and institutions of civil society. The critical inquiry of these concerns and the materials social practices associated with social reproduction will be our focus in this seminar.

EES 79903 – [52413]  Security and Surveillance:  The Production of Fear and Anxiety
GC:   R, 11:45 a.m. – 1:45 p.m., Hybrid [Virtual classes for Security and Surveillance on 9/8, 10/6, 10/20, 11/3 and probably 11/17 (speaker is not confirmed)], Rm. TBA, 3 credits, Prof. Low, Course open to EES Students only

Introduction:  This graduate seminar examines the efforts of anthropologists and geographers to attend to the spaces and politics of security. Today, security is one of the most prominent topics on the public’s mind midst a global pandemic and increasing concerns about the biopolitics of everyday as people search for safety and security in a war-torn world.  The current crisis draws upon a long history of security concerns that are as diverse in their objects (terrorism, crime and criminals, immigration, war and violence) as the socio-political moments that produce them. A growing ethnographic literature explores security’s various dimensions, from studies of military action and everyday violence to specific security infrastructures (e.g. Lutz 2002; Low 2003; Masco 2006). Some anthropologists are also exploring the contemporary problematization of security by exploring assemblages and novel experiments (e.g. Lakoff and Collier 2009; Maguire 2014; Samimian-Darash and Stalcup 2016). Of course, the breath of this ethnographic literature presents a significant challenge to integrate and critique and this is the primary objective of our readings and discussion.

After a review of the foundational work of Foucault (1977-1978), Beck (1986), Deleuze (1992), Brown (2010), Jackson (2008) and Daniel and Berwick (2020) , the course explores how specific securitization processes are promoted, enhanced and reproduced by 1) infrastructures of exclusion and inclusion; 2) increased visibility through borderization, stereotyping and racial profiling; 3) spatial governance through enclosure and surveillance; and 4) discursive practices that obscure the violence and destruction potential of securitization practices.  Students are asked to offer their own ethnographic research or research proposal that considers various contexts and historical periods to build a theoretical framework that emphasizes the spatial and temporal as well as the political in the study of security.  Through our discussions we should better understand this elusive but dangerous concept that produces a reality that is changing the life chances of millions of people

Course requirements:  Ethnographic or historical project of some aspect of security and/or the politics, infrastructures or technologies that contribute to security claims and the creation of affective configurations.  Read and discuss readings in class.  Lead a section of the class focused on their interest area.

Course learning goals: To understand and be able to critique the concept of security. To consider the variable consequences of security when used as a social, political, biological and economic intervention.  To identify the physical and affective infrastructures that underlie the production of security and security-based thinking. To research the bases and formulation of security claims To develop a keen appreciation of how security needs inform and structure racial, gender, class and gender inequality.

EES 79903 – [52458] Isotope Geochemistry 
ASRC:     T, 12:30 p.m. – 3:00 p.m., ASRC, Rm. 5.210 In Person, 3 credits, Prof. Wang , Course Open to EES Students only, EES-sponsored course for doctoral students taught at the ASRC

Since its establishment more than a century ago, isotope geochemistry became one of the pivotal foundations of geological sciences. Every year, there are thousands of papers published involving some kinds of isotope systems, and isotope geochemistry is a very useful tool to study solid-earth geochemistry, lunar and planetary sciences, environmental sciences, atmosphere sciences, biology, ecology, and archeology. It can provide quantitative constraints on the time of geological events, tracing fluxes in hydrological, rock, and biological cycles. It can measure temperature, pH and oxygen fugacity of geological systems to reconstruct the initial state of Earth in the geological past. In recent years, it has also been applied to forensic sciences. As ASRC (CUNY) will install its first set of Isotope Mass-spectrometer in 2018, it is time for students to learn some basic knowledge about this field and its applications to take advantage of these wonderful facility.  This class will provide an overview on fundamental principles of isotope geochemistry and their applications to specific geologic problems. The course will be for senior undergraduate students and first year graduate students. The course includes two parts: 1) Stable isotope geochemistry: an introduction to the physics and chemistry of light stable isotopes, a broad overview of the principles and conceptual techniques used in light stable isotopes, a broad overview of the principles and conceptual techniques used in light stable isotope geochemistry (C, O, S, H, N, Mg) and applications to earth science; 2) Radiogenic isotope geochemistry: this part of the course will cover basic principles and applications of radiogenic isotope geochemistry. The goal is to familiarize students with how various isotopic systems can be applied in the earth sciences, and to illustrate the important conclusions that have been drawn from them. This course is a QR class, i.e., answers to 80% of homework, mid-term and final exams require quantitative calculation and reasoning.

Text book: Isotopes: Principles and Applications (by Gunter Faure);

Class policy and grades: Attendance to the class

At City College:  W, 6:00 p.m. – 8:30 p.m., Marshak Bldg., Rm. 107, 3 credits, Prof. Lampousis, Course open to EES students only

The purpose of this course is to introduce students to good commercial and customary practices in the United States of America for conducting Phase II environmental site assessments (ESA). A Phase II ESA is an evaluation process for confirming and quantifying the presence of hazardous substances or petroleum products in environmental media (i.e., soil, rock, groundwater, surface water, air, soil gas, sediment) throughout a contaminated site. A Phase II ESA typically includes a determination through field screening and chemical testing of the geological, hydrogeological, hydrological, and engineered aspects of the site that influence the presence of hazardous substances or petroleum products (e.g., migration pathways, exposure points) and the existence of receptors and mechanisms of exposure.

Graduate students receive extensive training on mainstream quality review and assessment methods of completed Phase I ESAs in preparation to enter the workforce in upper level management positions in the environmental engineering consulting industry.

Students are automatically enrolled in the 40-hour OSHA HAZWOPER (Hazardous Waste Operations and Emergency Response Standard) certification program which applies to employees who are engaged in clean-up operations that are conducted at uncontrolled hazardous waste sites.

“Aesthetics and Nature” takes up two main clusters of questions: First, what constitutes appropriate aesthetic appreciation of nature? Is it importantly different from appropriate art appreciation?  Second, to what extent are aesthetic values important for environmentalism? That is, are aesthetic values too weak, too ‘scenery-obsessed,’ too elitist, or generally, too anthropocentric to outweigh human-welfare based reasons to exploit nature as a resource? Given the alarming effects and acceleration of anthropogenic climate change, these guiding questions feel especially urgent today.
 
To investigate these questions, we’ll start with some historical treatments of the three main aesthetic categories to emerge in 18th c. European aesthetics (predominantly but not exclusively with respect to nature): Edmund Burke’s A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and the Beautiful (1757), Immanuel Kant’s The Critique of the Power of Judgement (1790), and Uvedale Price’s “Essay on the Picturesque as Compared with the Sublime and Beautiful” (1794). Then we’ll consider the increasing turn toward aesthetics as philosophy of art in the 19th c. Course Description and Objectives:
This seminar-style course is divided into two parts: (1) Dynamics and composition of Earth’s Interior, led by Prof. Black; and (2) Atmosphere and climate dynamics, led by Prof. Booth.
Each week, the group will be required to read 2–4 scientific journal articles on pre-determined topics (see tentative list on last page). In class, subgroups will discuss given questions related to the articles, and a designated group member(s) will then summarize the subgroup findings to the entire class and lead the subsequent open discussion. A central objective of this course is the production of a term paper on a topic of the group members’ choice, hopefully related to their PhD research. In addition to learning about the geosphere and atmosphere, by the end of this course you will also have improved your skills at:
• Reading, understanding, synthesizing and evaluating scientific literature
• Oral presentation and discussion of scientific results
• Writing scientific papers
• Collaborating with your peers

Student Learning Outcomes
By the end of this course, you will be able to:

EES 79903 – [63263] Rethinking Place and Space
At the GC:     R, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m., Rm. TBA, 3 credits, Prof. Kabachnik, Course open to EES Students only

Place and space are key concepts not only in geography and social theory, but in our everyday lives. The meanings of these terms may seem apparent. However, upon closer examination, their use, or misuse, is a major problem in academic writing--whether by offering inconsistent definitions or simply failing to define them. Rethinking place and space strengthens the clarity of our theoretical frameworks, research designs, and pedagogy. Therefore, the course will provide a genealogy of these concepts (and related terms like choros, spatiality, topos, chronotope), focusing on the philosophical debates surrounding them. We will sift through these readings and debates to identify (ontological, methodological, and epistemological) discontinuities and patterns, and the implications of invoking one term over another.​

EES 79903 – [63278] Env Assessment 2
At City College:        S, 10:00 a.m. – 12:30 p.m., Online, 3 credits, Prof. TBA
 
The purpose of this course is to introduce students to good commercial and customary practices in the United States of America for conducting Phase II environmental site assessments (ESA). A Phase II ESA is an evaluation process for confirming and quantifying the presence of hazardous substances or petroleum products in environmental media (i.e., soil, rock, groundwater, surface water, air, soil gas, sediment) throughout a contaminated site. A Phase II ESA typically includes a determination through field screening and chemical testing of the geological, hydrogeological, hydrological, and engineered aspects of the site that influence the presence of hazardous substances or petroleum products (e.g., migration pathways, exposure points) and the existence of receptors and mechanisms of exposure.

Graduate students receive extensive training on mainstream quality review and assessment methods of completed Phase I ESAs in preparation to enter the workforce in upper level management positions in the environmental engineering consulting industry.

Students are automatically enrolled in the 40-hour OSHA HAZWOPER (Hazardous Waste Operations and Emergency Response Standard) certification program which applies to employees who are engaged in clean-up operations that are conducted at uncontrolled hazardous waste sites. Prerequisite: EAS B3300 or permission of instructor. 3 hr./ wk.

From Chiapas to Occupy, from the Gezi Park uprising to disaster communism during the pandemic, acts of commoning have been central to new political imaginaries and formations over the last decades. Capitalism was born, Marx famously argued, when peasants were forcibly torn from their means of subsistence and hurled onto urban labor markets as free and “unattached” proletarians. As Marx evocatively put it, “the history of this expropriation is written in the annals of mankind in letters of blood and fire.” Recent theorists of capitalism have asserted that the process of violent dispossession not only has continued unabated for the last five centuries but has been intensifying during the neoliberal age. Indeed, for many, today’s enclosures are the leading edge of contemporary capitalism. We live in a period of violent land grabbing and resource extraction that is pushing planetary systems towards terminal breakdown.

This seminar will explore contemporary processes of – and resistance to - capitalist and neocolonialist enclosure. Our conversations will be oriented around three key theoretical and political interventions. The first is the assertion that enclosure and extraction pertain not just to material things like land and minerals but also to relatively immaterial social resources such as information, culture, and even affect. The commons is thus a social form that is constantly created and recreated. The corollary of this, and the second key theoretical hypothesis of the seminar, is the idea that the commons is not solely a thing but a social practice. The commons, in other words, is the space of social relation created in and through acts of mutual aid and solidarity.
