ENGL 75000. American Renaissance.

David Reynolds. Tuesday 2:00 pm – 4:00 pm. 2/3/4 credits. Online.

The American Renaissance (1835-65) is widely seen as the peak of American cultural expression. This period featured landmark writings race and slavery by Frederick Douglass, Harriet Jacobs, and Harriet Beecher Stowe; innovations in philosophy, ecological awareness, and style by Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau; the metaphysical depth and cultural variety exhibited through the fiction of Herman Melville and Nathaniel Hawthorne; the poetic experiments of Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson; and the psychological exploration and groundbreaking aesthetics of Edgar Allan Poe. Women’s rights were forcibly promoted by Margaret Fuller and other feminists. Abolitionist activism ranged from the rhetorical militancy of William Lloyd Garrison to the armed action of John Brown. Lincoln’s speeches embodied the nation’s enduring political themes. This era also saw a surge in popular culture, exemplified by penny newspapers, pulp novels, graphic reform writings, and nation-touring singing families. Alongside studying key works of the period, we examine current approaches to cultural history, American Studies, and the study of race and gender. The course also investigates how online archives and AI can help us gain new insights that lead to original scholarship and potentially publishable writing.

ENGL 75500. After Afrofuturism: Futures of Black Speculation.

Jonathan Gray. Thursday 4:15 - 6:15 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

Afrofuturism attempts to articulate a speculative counter politics centered in cultural production organized around the radical fact that, as the artist Alisha B Wormsley so bluntly states, “there are Black people in the future.” This class investigates what comes after Afrofuturism, what kinds of speculation obtain once Wormsley’s challenge is taken as a given. We will accomplish this by engaging in recent cultural production that involves a (re)turn to what I’m calling Black archival speculation and what others describe as critical fabulation, afro-fabulation, and the Black Archival turn. These overlapping terms describe a desire to make legible a past that may not have existed as such through the creation of speculative texts that link unaccounted for resistance then to the full emancipation of Black life now. These fantastical imaginings—think Jesmyn Ward’s Sing Unburied Sing; Ryan Coogler’s Sinners; and Nickel Boys, both the Pulitzer Prize winning novel and the Academy Award nominated film—alongside critical interventions by Richard Iton, Saidiya Hartman, Tavia Nyong’o Brigette Fielder, and others transcend the generic expectations of sci-fi through its exploration of a (previously unknowable?) speculative past with all that this implies about both the Black critical imagination and the Black radical tradition.

ENGL 79010. Urban Literacies.

Todd Craig. Thursday 4:15 pm – 6:15 pm. 3 credits. Hybrid synchronous.

(Cross-listed with Urban Education)

This course asks students to investigate how literacies shape and guide community members in urban locations. If we envision literacy as a way that people read, write and interact with the world, how might the aspects of Urban Literacies influence the ways students in metropolitan environments learn, and consequently impact their local communities and a larger world? We will explore theories of Urban Literacies and how those theories directly impact our contemporary landscape. This will require us to interrogate how the acts of reading, writing, critically thinking and communicating shift based on various locations and urban epicenters over time. This course will also explore how technological advancements have changed the ways we interface with ideas of literacy.

ENGL 80400. Archiving Queerness.

Mark McBeth. Thursday 6:30 - 8:30 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

Collecting, Finding, and Interpreting Artifacts of the Intersectional and the Marginalized In the boxes and folders of archives, objects appear inanimate and inert. Yet, as Bruno Latour claims, these types of quasi-objects or "actants" have an agency in human lives. They have often unacknowledged forces in how the world (i.e. ideologies) and the word (i.e., discourses) get shaped and conserved (or reformed and revolutionized). Archival artifacts offer a particular approach to Latour's actor network theory because, rather than just one snapshot of a specified moment in history, they allow a more expansive view over geographic areas and an extended longitudinal understanding over multiple generations. In this course, will collectively and collaboratively do four things (maybe a few other side tasks as well):

revisit some of the 20th-century collectors of artifacts who made research possible for archival researchers today.

critically examine the archives (the physical spaces), the archivization (the curation of chosen materials), and the archiving (the organization of materials once deposited). We will consider who and what gets into collections and then how their archival organization affects their usage.

visit some New York archives to explore what memory treasures they hold and how researchers might use them for divergent means and investigations.

spend some time choosing, viewing and analyzing primary archival artifacts to rehearse how to inscribe, interpret and integrate their meaning into our scholarship.

While we will focus upon literacy objects that have impacted 20th-century Queer and other marginalized communities for the purposes of shared analytic exercises together, students will have the freedom to choose and curate their own "exhibit" of objects to study, unpack, and write about.

ENGL 80600. Barthes/Foucault/Kristeva: Texts at a Revolution.

Marc Dolan. Thursday 11:45 am - 1:45 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

This course will focus on three of the most salient participants in the fecund intellectual cauldron of Paris during the 1960s and 1970s: Roland Barthes, Michel Foucault, and Julia Kristeva. They were, respectively, a philologist, a philosopher, and a psychoanalyst by training, but in an intellectual moment that was beginning to fumble beyond existentialism and structuralism toward a better understanding of an increasingly globalist world, these three scholars’ works overlapped and sometimes directly commented on each other. For us in the twenty-first century, postmodernism is a ground-level assumption about the world in which we live, but for these three thinkers “postmodernism” was a “discovery”—in precisely the same way that many before them might have said that Christopher Columbus “discovered” “America.” To put it baldly, they saw things that already existed and gave them newly recognizable names. In terms that Foucault would eventually coin, they identified a moment of epistemological emergence.

In this course, we will read works by all three scholars in the order in which they were originally published, in an attempt to track the exact movements in their thought through which that thought emerged. With the inevitable skeptical wisdom of hindsight, we will also question whether any of their intellectual movements might in retrospect appear to have been wrong turnings. Among the works that we may read in common are: Writing Degree Zero, Mythologies, A History of Madness, The Order of Things, and Discipline and Punish, as well as essays in both Image/Music/Text and The Kristeva Reader. We will pay central attention to three groundbreaking works that were published within a year of each other—S/Z, The Archeology of Knowledge, and Desire in Language—all of which arrived in the immediate wake of 1968, a year of very palpable revolutionary barricades on the streets of Paris just outside these three scholars’ classrooms and studies. We will also probably read A Lover’s Discourse, The History of Sexuality, and Tales of Love against one another, as well as Barthes’ and Kristeva’s self-conscious engagements with exoticism in Empire of Signs and About Chinese Women.

ENGL 80600. The Freedom Party: Liberation Aesthetics.

Eric Lott. Wednesday 6:30 - 8:30 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

(Cross-listed with American Studies Certificate Program)

An inquiry into the long history of thinking about the aesthetic as a modality of liberation. The aesthetic is often under erasure (Hal Foster’s The Anti-Aesthetic, Terry Eagleton’s The Ideology of the Aesthetic), only to find reclamation as an emancipatory category (Tina Campt, Rita Felski). We’ll take up the question of art’s relation to revolution as raised in many traditions and from various perspectives, from Friedrich Schiller to Carrie Mae Weems. Can the aesthetic be social movements’ advance-guard or at least sidekick, or is it always their graveyard? What inclines aesthetic practice in the direction of political change? And is that change necessarily for the good (any number of MAGA poets running around these days)? Art (and debates about whatever that is) as inherently revolutionary (cf. Herbert Marcuse’s The Aesthetic Dimension) as opposed to art as quietist and escapist, or a bourgeois ruse (Eagleton); protest versus immanence, movement art over against movement understandings of all art. Questions certain to arise include “post-critique” celebrations of textual pleasure or surface reading; the (eternal) return of close reading (Johanna Winant, Jonathan Kramnick); the aesthetic as a space for the critique of sovereignty (Fred Moten, José Muñoz’s uses of Ernst Bloch); political embraces of form (Caroline Levine, Anna Kornbluh); the encounter of the literary and infrastructures like the postwar creative writing program and Amazon (Mark McGurl, Dan Sinykin); museum studies and the politics of installation; and whole new refunctionings of form as political intervention (J Dilla’s math, Taylor Swift re-recording her own catalogue, Arthur Jafa’s arrythmia, Kevin Everson’s dreadpan, Kara Walker’s anarchitecture). And there’s always still Adorno’s Aesthetic Theory to contend with. Other matters likely: the relation of parties to The Party (is there a DSA aesthetic worth defending?), the aesthetics of play, the question of ideology, psychoanalytic understandings of the creative act, the political unconscious, the role of art in emergent revolutionary formations such as the Paris Commune and conversely the revolutionary potential of apparently anodyne formations such as the dance floor. Other possible readings: Sigmund Freud, Leon Trotsky, Sergei Eisenstein, Walter Benjamin, D.W. Winnicott, Roland Barthes, John Berger, Susan Sontag, Fredric Jameson, Stanley Cavell, Greil Marcus, Sianne Ngai, Lauren Berlant, Kobena Mercer, Douglas Crimp, Wayne Koestenbaum, Claire Bishop, Nathaniel Mackey, Amber Musser, among others, with plenty of textual sites and examples.

ENGL 80600. Critical Disability Studies.

Talia Schaffer. Wednesday 11:45 – 1:45 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

(Cross-listed with Women’s Studies Certificate Program - WSCP 81000)

Disability studies is an emerging, flourishing field, an exciting new way of thinking about bodyminds in the world. This course aims to give students a strong grounding in the fundamental principles and foundational texts of disability studies, including writings on the normate by Lennard Davis and Rosemarie Garland Thomson, core readings by Christine Miserandino and Tobin Siebers, along with care ethics by Eva Feder Kittay and Nel Noddings. We’ll build on this to address the theoretical interests that dominate the field today, including crip time, disability-gain, neurodiversity, queer-crip alignments, disability and race, accommodation theory, reading writers including Alison Kafer, H. Dirksen-Baumann, Margaret Price, Eli Clare, Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samrasinha, Elizabeth Freeman, Sami Schalk. One of the issues we’ll be thinking about is the extent to which academia reinforces a narrow sense of acceptable bodyminds, and how we might creatively expand that in our own work, including our teaching. Another key component of this course will be a deep dive into the historical construction of disability, with particular attention to the way alternative understandings of disability in the past might provide useful templates for us today (readings may include Susan Schweik, Paul Longmore, Jay Dolmage, Sarah Rose, Essaka Joshua, Michael Brown, and others). Because the modern idea of disability emerged in the nineteenth century, three short case studies will help us notice when it coheres and when it remains surprisingly fluid: Austen’s Mansfield Park, Dickens’s A Christmas Carol, and Oliphant’s The Library Window. Students will be encouraged to explore minds and bodies in the texts or cultural issues they care about most. The course will accommodate multiple forms of accountability in different media: weekly summaries, presentations, nontraditional or traditional final papers. I am excited to share this revelatory, reparative, expansive theoretical attunement with students, and to see what you make of it in your own fields.

ENGL 80700. Heated Chivalry: Romance, Medieval and Beyond.

Steve Kruger. Wednesday 2:00 – 4:00 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

(Cross-listed with Medieval Studies Certificate Program – MSCP 80500)

This course will consider the genre of romance, from its earliest medieval instantiations through contemporary popular romances like Heated Rivalry.

The first two-thirds of the course will focus on medieval romance, examining intensively a series of (mostly poetic) texts, stretching from the romances and lais of Chretien de Troyes and Marie de France (in the 12th century) to Thomas Malory’s Morte Darthur (in the 15th). Along the way, we will consider romances about Charlemagne, Alexander, the Crusades, Arthur, the Grail, ancient Greece and Rome, “modern” England and France. Although we begin with texts written in French, we will treat mainly English-language texts, and we will read these in the original Middle English.

In the next third of the course, we will consider where romance travels after the 15th century. The syllabus here will partly depend upon student interest. (So, if we have a number of early modern students or Americanists, we will focus attention toward their fields of expertise.) We might read Philip Sidney or Mary Wroth; Shakespeare’s The Winter’s Tale; Sir Walter Scott or Nathaniel Hawthorne; Virginia Woolf’s Orlando, with an eye toward considering how these take up the mantle of the earlier romance genre while transforming it. And we will end by thinking about (queer?) romance in our current moment through the lens of Heated Rivalry—considering (again, partly based on students’ interests) either (or both) of Rachel Reid’s novels, Heated Rivalry and The Long Game, the Jacob Tierney television series, and some of the now voluminous fan fiction.

One central goal of the course will be to reflect on what we mean by the genre of romance, and to consider how we might approach the question of genre theoretically. Alongside the romance texts, we will therefore take up a wide range of approaches to theorizing genre: formalist arguments like Todorov’s; feminist readings like Radway’s; reception theory like Jauss’s; Marxist/materialist formulations like Lukács’s and Jameson’s; cultural studies projects like Modleski’s.

For non-medievalists, projects on later cultural materials are encouraged. For medievalists, interdisciplinary approaches (e.g., thinking about Crusades-related romances in relation to the historiography of the Crusades; considering works across different linguistic/national traditions; thinking comparatively about the representation of something like “courtly love” or “chivalry” in both literary works and non-literary modes like the visual arts) are welcome.

ENGL 80700. The Art of Late Antique and Medieval Letters.

Brian Sowers. Monday 4:15 pm – 6:15 pm. 3 credits. In person.

(Cross-listed with Classics – CLAS 72800; Medieval Studies Certificate Program - MSCP 70100; Liberal Studies - MALS 74400)

This course introduces students to ancient, late antique, and early medieval letters and epistolography (the art of writing, circulating, and publishing letters). This course focuses on a single literary genre (letters) in a way that introduces students to wider research and reading methods relevant to premodern studies (ancient, late antique, medieval). Course readings will be in English; students with mastery of additional ancient/modern languages will be expected to incorporate these language skills in their research papers.

ENGL 81100. Actors, Bodies, and Performance in Early Modern England.

Tanya Pollard. Monday 4:15 - 6:15 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

This course will explore ways that actors, both individually and collectively, shaped the construction of plays in early modern England. How did the members and power dynamics of repertory companies inspire playwrights’ development of characters and plots? What can we learn from accounts of actors in plays and other documents, and how did recognizable bodies interact with prosthetics such as wigs, cosmetics, blackface, and physical deformities? How might factors such as height, build, beards, voices, previous acting parts, and reputations have affected roles, and how did authors and audiences play with conventional practices of crossing lines of gender, race, class, and age? How did economics and company status affect casting and roles?

ENGL 84200. Romantic Concepts of Nature.

Alexander Schlutz. Monday 2:00 – 4:00 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

Since the rise of ecocriticism and Green Romanticism in the 1990s it has become commonplace to present Romantic writers as anticipating contemporary environmental concerns and to (re)mobilize for current ecological debates the Romantic critique of nascent processes of industrialization and a Cartesian, mechanical, view of the natural world. At the same time, ecologists and environmental writers perceive the “romanticization” of nature—the projection of imaginary, aesthetic and cultural constructs onto a material world fundamentally alien to them—as one of the main obstacles to a fruitful understanding of our relationship to the environment. More recently, ecocritics and ecopoets have come to suggest that, given the unraveling of the nature/culture binary and any stable conception of “Nature” in the disastrous predicament of our Anthropocene present, investigations of Romantic-era literature and culture are no longer applicable to the urgent needs of the present moment. Hence, in the contemporary discussion, one can see the Romantics being lauded for writing against the objectification of nature, being critiqued for neglecting the difference between the products of the writer’s consciousness and affect and the material Other they confront, and being dismissed as irrelevant for current ecological thought.

To position ourselves with respect to such conflicting assessments, we will investigate what a variety of Romantic-period concepts of nature—a plurality rather than a single position—looked like concretely. We will examine two of the central philosophical positions on the relationship of human consciousness to the natural world Romantic-era writers could draw on, those of Baruch Spinoza and Immanuel Kant, and discuss the writings and philosophical positions of Mary Wollstonecraft, William and Dorothy Wordsworth, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, and John Clare. Throughout, our goal will be to ascertain the answers the texts of these writers can offer to questions about the place of human beings in the natural world, the relationship of mind and matter, and of human and natural history, central philosophical questions they indeed share with contemporary environmental thinkers.

ENGL 85400. Negative Aesthetics in U.S. Latinx Literatures.

Richard Perez. Wednesday 4:15 - 6:15 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

For the last two centuries, Latinx authors in the U.S. have forged a literary tradition predicated on complex rearticulations of American sensibilities through generative forms of negativity. In this course we will consider, using Adorno as a theoretical springboard, how Latinx writers employ the negative to split and recombine languages, creolize racial identities, reimagine borders, and queer exile; and in so doing alter our notion of what constitutes aesthetic production, experience, and judgment. This literary sensibility runs counter, for instance, to Whitmanian flourishes of optimism or the ideological insistence on positivity that often veils the violence of colonial and market practices. Thus, we will examine how the formation of U.S. Latinx literary aesthetics complicates what Marcuse calls given realities through a restless and critical imaginary attuned to the potential of the negative, a potential found, paradoxically, in privation, rage, failure, confusion and conflict. In addition, we will explore the ways in which this negative potential aspires towards something revolutionary and utopic calling for sentient shifts both in our aesthetics and in our politics. Primary authors will include: Piri Thomas, Reyna Grande, Ernesto Mestre-Reed, Junot Diaz, and Iris Gomez. We will also read secondary literature by Theodore Adorno, Emmanuel Levinas, Frantz Fanon, Edward Said, Judith Butler, Michael Taussig, Mary Pat Brady, Fred Moten, Lyn Di Iorio Sandin, Patricia Gherovici, Jose Munoz, Siane Ngai, Sandra Ruiz, Cathy Park Hong, and Nicole Guidotti-Hernandez.

ENGL 85500. Afropsychedelic: Black Experimental Forms in the 1970s.

Shelly Eversley. Tuesday 4:15 - 6:15 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

What would it mean to know the world without repeating the structures that make antiblackness thinkable? This seminar works through Afropsychedelic, a framework for reading Black experimental forms as a practice of freedom that breaks open perception, narrative time, and genre so Black freedom dreams become imaginable on their own terms. We will read, among others, Carlene Hatcher Polite’s obscure novel Sister X and the Victims of Foul Play (1975), Toni Cade Bambara’s anthology The Black Woman (1970), poetry and manifestos from the Black Aesthetic Movement, and Melvin Van Peebles’s film Sweet Sweetback’s Baadasssss Song (1971). We will also consider the Caribbean Artists Movement, founded in London in 1966, where C. L. R. James joined early debates, and to its journal Savacou, launched in Jamaica in 1970, where Sylvia Wynter published “Novel and History, Plot and Plantation” (1971). Alongside Wynter and James, Margo Crawford, Carter Mathes, Nathaniel Mackey, Phillip Brian Harper, Carole Boyce Davies, and Jayna Brown will help us think about the human, abstraction, and other ways of knowing.

ENGL 86600. Figures of Anti-Imperialism: Palestine, Islam, Marxism.

Siraj Ahmed. Monday 11:45 am - 1:45 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

This course begins from the national liberation movements that oppose U.S. imperialism today, both in Palestine and around it. The premise of the course is that we won’t appreciate the logic of these movements until we understand what is specific to the imperial formation they oppose and, by extension, its difference from prevailing concepts of empire. To that end, we will consider Marxist theories of imperialism, homing in especially on the presumption—contested by Third-World Marxists (e.g., Mariátegui, Mao, Fanon, Che, Cabral, Amin)—that the industrial working classes must become the revolutionary subjects of history (e.g., Lenin, Trotsky, Gramsci, Luxemburg, Lukács). We will study, pace that presumption, the implication in recent scholarship that US empire depends less on colonized labor than on ‘accumulation by waste’ (Ali Kadri, Michael Denning, Ruth Wilson Gilmore). Such scholarship details how the dominant forms of capital (i.e., military industrial, technology and surveillance, finance) profit from surplus and disposable populations by testing the technology of genocide on them (Zygmunt Bauman, William Robinson). In both Palestine and the broader region, we will study the history by which Marxist liberation movements were largely replaced by Islamic ones and consider how the tactics, politics, and metaphysics of both these phases of anti-imperial struggle respond to the mode of accumulation they confront. We might read Ghassan Kanafani, Arwa Saleh, or Mahdi Amel on the Marxist side and Sayyid Qutb, Zaynab Al-Ghazali, Malcolm X, or Ali Shariati on the Islamic side.

As it explores these theoretical contexts, the course will have two other tasks. We will examine the adequacy of received anti-imperial scholarship (e.g., Jameson, Said, Spivak, Davis, Butler, Mbembe, Khalidi, Kelley, Moten, Coulthard) to the material actualities of empire and reflect on this scholarship’s curious reticence toward the revolutionary movements fighting empire now. And we will study how, in thinking with such movements, literature from Palestine and the region expands the field of Western aesthetic perception and how it does not (e.g., Tayeb Salih, Emile Habibi, Kanafani, Sahar Khalifeh, Radwa Ashour, Susan Abulhawa, Wisam Rafeedie).

Prior knowledge of these topics is neither expected nor desired. The point of the course is to build understanding from the ground up. Despite the breadth of topics, weekly readings will be selective and manageable.

ENGL 86800. Uneven Futures: Speculative Fictional Strategies for Collective Survival.

Ashley Dawson. Tuesday 11:45 am - 1:45 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

(Cross-listed with Earth and Environmental Sciences - EES 79903)

Science fiction author William Gibson once remarked that the future is already here – it just isn’t evenly distributed. It doesn’t take much to extrapolate Gibson’s comment about the unequally distribution of futurity to a global geographical plane characterized by intensifying combined and uneven development. But are the core capitalist countries – the so-called West – still the domicile of futurity? How might the implied temporal, spatial, and developmental hierarchies in Gibson’s quip by inverted? How can temporality be decolonized, and what role can fiction play in this effort to imagine alternative radical futures?

This seminar examines works of speculative fiction that engage in what Kodwo Eshun argues is the “chronopolitical act” of intervening in the production and distribution of futurity. To dismantle Western narratives of progress and modernity that are premised on the historical subjugation of people of color is also to confound the Western dichotomy between the real and unreal, the scientific and the supernatural. The seminar will, therefore, be filled with spirits, ghosts, and ancestral intelligence (AI) that circulates in the present and imparts lessons about the future. There will also, of course, be lots of cyborgs. We will attend to the long legacy of genres like Afrofuturism while also investigating Africanfuturism and speculative fictions set in other parts of the Global South. Of key interest will be recent works of postcolonial cli-fi that attempt to think through strategies of collective survival in the face of the climate emergency, which is unfolding with greatest ferocity in the Global South. Foundational to all our discussions will be investigation of the way in which aesthetic genres warp, morph, and are reborn under the pressure of the quest to forge new futures.

Some of the authors and critics whose work we are likely to discuss include Octavia Butler, T.J. Demos, Cherie Dimaline, Amal El-Mohtar, Kodwo Eshun, Mark Fisher, Amitav Ghosh, Megha Majumdar, Masande Ntshanga, M.E. O’Brien and Eman Abdelhadi, Nnedi Okorafor, Rebecca Roanhorse, Rosaura Sánchez and Beatrice Pita, Boualem Sansal, Tade Thompson!

ENGL 87300. Improvisation

Wayne Koestenbaum. Tuesday 2:00 pm – 4:00 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person.

(Cross-listed with Biography & Memoir – BAM 70500-3)

From Yvonne Rainer’s autobiography: “Ruth Barnes, who studied with me between 1969 and 1971, recently described the first dance class she attended in my studio on Greene Street: ‘When I came in, Yvonne was lying on the floor. When everyone was assembled she went into a closet and came out with a huge garbage bag, which she emptied. After fifty green tennis balls [I remember them as red rubber balls!] tumbled out, she said, ‘Get to work.’ And that was the class. I’ve never seen adults so intensely involved in such serious uncompetitive play.”

In this seminar, we will think and write together about how to improvise and about why one might be motivated to embark upon an improvisatory practice, whether in words, music, movement, or images. Key texts will be Fred Moten’s In the Break: The Aesthetics of the Black Radical Tradition, Yvonne Rainer’s Feelings Are Facts: A Life, Jack Whitten’s Notes from the Woodshed, John Cage’s Silence, Bernadette Mayer’s Midwinter Day, and Gertrude Stein’s A Novel of Thank You. We may also read selections from Ann Cooper Albright and David Gere’s Taken by Surprise: A Dance Improvisation Reader, Danielle Goldman’s I Want to be Ready: Improvised Dance as a Practice of Freedom, Derek Bailey’s Improvisation: Its Nature and Practice in Music, George Lewis’s A Power Stronger Than Itself: The AACM and American Experimental Music, as well as John Cassavetes’s Opening Night (starring Gena Rowlands), and texts and interviews by Pauline Oliveros, Park McArthur, Will Rawls, David Antin, and others.

ENGL 87300. Late Medieval and Early Modern Theatre and Performance.

Erika Lin. Thursday 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m. 3 credits. In person.

(Cross-listed with Theater – THEA 86000; Global Early Modern Studies Certificate Program – GEMS 83100)

This course offers an interdisciplinary introduction to theatre and performance in England and Europe from the 1400s through 1600s. Combining historical and theoretical approaches from a variety of disciplines, we will examine a range of dramatic genres, such as biblical theatre, saints plays, moralities, farces, revenge tragedy, commedia dell’arte, and court masques. We will also consider performance practices that were unscripted, improvised, or spectacular in nature, such as clowning, jigs, ballads, puppetry, rope-dancing, mountebank shows, bearbaiting, morris dancing, and civic pageantry. These various kinds of theatre and performance will be situated in relation to broader cultural discourses from the era, including those related to gender, sexuality, class, race, and religion, among others.

In addition to historicist concerns, we will also address theoretical and methodological matters, including both practical strategies for dealing with primary sources from this period and analytical reflection on epistemological questions raised by their idiosyncratic forms. Discussion may encompass the portability of critical tools developed to grapple with obsolete performance genres, unfamiliar documentary conventions, fragmentary sources, and gaps in evidence. In doing so, the course aims to invite a reevaluation of paradigms taken for granted in contemporary theatre research as well as self-reflexive examination of the theoretical stakes of that work.

Primary readings will include both dramatic literature and other extant texts related to performance, such as inventories of costumes and props. Secondary readings will be drawn from a variety of fields, including theatre and performance, cultural history, literature, art history, and religious studies. Non-English material will be provided in translation. No experience with paleography is required. Evaluation: active class participation, short weekly response papers, in-class presentation, research proposal with annotated bibliography, and a final paper.

ENGL 87400. Cinema of the Apocalypse.

Peter Hitchcock. Wednesday 2:00 pm – 4:00 pm. 3 credits. In person.

(Cross-listed with Film Studies – FSCP 81000)

Obviously, while the recent global pandemic has spurred new interest in the genre of viral calamity--films like Outbreak, Contagion, 28 Days (Years) Later, etc--the subject and sense of apocalypse has been integral to the history of cinema itself, as if its apparatus and ways of seeing open up the art before the otherwise inscrutable and abstract concepts of doom and end of days. To some extent this can be measured in the formal compulsions of visual narrative, and it is for this reason that film is often interpellated to address the immediacy and affective intimacy of catastrophe (an apocalypse “nowness” if you will). This course is intended to examine both the genealogies of representing apocalypse in film, a longue durée, say, from Verdens Undergang (1916) to Don’t Look Up (2021), but also to explore the situatedness of individual narratives that struggle to express both the desire and disavowal of imagining the end of the species and that of the world. With the sensitive dependence to historical conditions, for instance, Cold War catastrophe could be linked to anxiety about nuclear destruction (films like Fail-Safe [1964] or Dr. Strangelove [1964]) or sci-fi doppelgangers around Soviet invasion (The Thing from Another World [1951], The Day the Earth Stood Still [1951], Them! [1954]). We will look to complicate the conditions of spectatorship between, for instance, The Night of the Living Dead (1968), the perhaps radiation-tainted reanimation of “ghouls” and zombies, and Zombieland: Double Tap (2019), where even the banalization of annihilation is banalized. Indeed, the latter connects to a whole sub-genre of laughing in the face of the cannibalistic undead (Shaun of the Dead [2004]) or murderous aliens, (Mars Attacks! [1996], Attack the Block [2011]). The demise of the human is often imagined as a consequence of overpopulation or the end of reproduction, from Soylent Green (1973) and Avengers: Infinity War (2018) to Children of Men (2006), Mad Max: Fury Road (2015) and The Girl with All the Gifts (2016). Here we will consider why apocalypse is often linked to normative assumptions about gender and sexuality and the transformations of social reproduction as such. The racialization of future dread is also a significant focus of cinema, and where racial anxiety is both exposed and critiqued (Alien Nation [1988], District 9 [2009], Us [2019]. Indeed, apocalypse as a theme can be used to cross-reference and interrogate the codes of conscious and unconscious identification and alienation throughout the genre. Apocalypse from this perspective might offer discourses of a great leveling, and yet it is just as clear that film attempts to negotiate the gnawing persistence of inequalities on a world scale (the reproduction of class hierarchy in, for instance, Elysium [2013] and Snowpiercer [2013]). While cinematic apocalypse is never an unalloyed barometer of social anxiety, the effulgence of films of environmental catastrophe is significant in its own right. Often such narratives offer an imaginary resolution of a real contradiction: The Day after Tomorrow (2004), for instance, seems to place its bets on a father and son reuniting, while The Wandering Earth (2019) envisages the entire planet being dragged to safety. This suggests that fighting for change may not have the visual allure of suffering the consequences of quiescence. Nevertheless, in general the cinema of the apocalypse is the scene of conflicted allegories over and above the sublation of causes and effects, and the readings for the course will move between theory and fiction to elaborate this tension (a list might include Cameron, Chaudhary, Gurr, Knight, Kristeva, Martin et al, McCarthy, Butler, Missari, Seed, Sharrett, Shaviro, Sontag, Stone, Subramanian, and Vandemeer).

ENGL 76000. The Wake.

Nico Israel. Thursday 11:45 am-1:45 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3212.

James Joyce said it took him seventeen years to write his final novel, Finnegans Wake (1940), so he didn’t see why the book shouldn’t take seventeen years to read. In this seminar we will try to read his wildly inventive, multilingual, hilariously gargantuan “book of the night” in a mere fourteen weeks. We will explore a few of the dozens of source texts—Irish legends, biblical stories, the Egyptian Book of the Dead, American popular songs, philosophical writings, and Joyce’s own Ulysses—that give the novel shape, and we will encounter both the history of Joyce (and modernist) studies and some salient contemporary examples of Joyce criticism. The book was completed just as the second world war began, but Joyce portrays both history and family life as one battle after another in an endless war--and it still manages to be a very funny novel. Of special interest throughout the seminar are questions of gender, coloniality, globality, ecology, indigeneity, planetarity, shame and the purgation of shame as envisioned throughout the novel. A “wake” is a track that breaks the flow of water and a funereal rite. In its command form, it is a call to awaken from sleep. Finnegans Wake, Joyce’s final novel, is concerned above all with finality and the possibility of beginning, again.

ENGL 76000. Aestheticism, Decadence, Modernism, 1880-1930.

Richard Kaye. Thursday 6:30 pm-8:30 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3212.

This class explores the relation between the aestheticist and decadent movements as well as their determination of modernist aesthetics. We will begin with late-nineteenth-century British, American, and French works by Hardy, Wilde, James, and Huysmans. The fin de siècle was a time of pervasive fears and fantasies dominated by such figures as the New Woman, the urban detective, the homosexual bachelor, the Anarchist, the Oriental, the overreaching colonialist, the self-preening aesthete, the vampire, and the femme fatale, the latter reaching an apotheosis in Wilde’s Salome. In the diverse writings of Pater, Olive Schreiner, Vernon Lee, and Wilde, aestheticism emerged as a theoretically coherent and varied movement absorbed in exquisite surfaces, tantalizing ambiguities, and useless artifice. For decadent writers and artists, scientific theories of “degeneration” could be recalibrated as erotically charged, non-teleological experiments, while Freud drew on “decadent” scenarios for his proto-modernist narratives dealing with hysteria and sexual disorder, preeminently in Dora: Fragment of a Case of Hysteria, Women writers, meanwhile, struggled to find a place within the male-defined coteries of aestheticism and decadence, a theme dramatized in Henry James’ tale “The Author of Beltraffio,,” narrated by a decadent acolyte, in which the aestheticist project must be sequestered from female readers, who can only misconstrue its aims as immoral. Yet there were also alliances between male decadents and feminist writers (Wilde promoted Schreiner’s novel The Story of an African Farm because of its bold challenge to realist conventions and depiction of colonialist malaise.) In France, the woman writer Rachilde publishes Monsieur Venus (1884), a symbolist/decadent novella concerning a dominatrix noblewoman, Raoule, who gradually transforms her working-class lover Jacques into her mistress by emptying him of all his “masculine” traits.

In the class’s second part we will explore how the fin outlasted the siècle, maintaining an intense afterlife in the Anglo-American modernist writing of Yeats, James, Eliot, Conrad, Joyce, Lawrence, and Djuna Barnes. The morbidity, subjectivism, sexual experimentalism, and excesses of literary technique characteristic of 1890s sensibility foment modernist revisions that seek to repress their origins in decadent poetics. Wilde's Picture of Dorian Gray, with its hero who cannot "develop," inspires modernist counter-bildungsromanae. We consider Joyce’s Stephen Hero, an early version of Portrait of the Artist as Young Man, arguably a satire of aestheticism as well as a novel with an explicitly Paterian protagonist. The keenly observing, detached bachelor familiar from James also narrates Rilke’s lyrical The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge (arguably the first modernist novel) and that helps to form the paralyzed solitary consciousness of Eliot’s “Prufrock.” Arguably the narrator of Conrad’s Heart of Darkness adopts decadent techniques for his coiled narrative of colonial tragedy. We will consider, too, Eliot’s absorption in the figure of the Jew as an emblem of a malevolent decadent cosmopolitanism versus Djuna Barnes’ depiction of the decadent Jew in her novel Nightwood as a more positively transformative cultural agent. We will consider how the Aestheticst and Decadent movements shaped the writing of the Harlem Renaissance. Our class concludes with James’ The Golden Bowl, a novel of twinned adulteries that is one of James’ most topical, aesthetically difficult, and decadent works of fiction. Among the works we will read: Hardy, Jude the Obscure, Huysmans, Against Nature; Wilde, The Picture of Dorian Gray, Salome; Schreiner, Story of an African Farm; Freud, Dora: Analysis of a Fragment of a Case of Hysteria; Conrad, Heart of Darkness, Joyce, Stephen Hero, Yeats, Selected Poems; Lawrence, Selected Short Fiction; Eliot, Selected Poetry; James, The Golden Bowl; Barnes, Nightwood; Showalter, Elaine, ed., Daughters of Decadence. We will read relevant critical and theoretical texts, including Walter Pater, “Conclusion” to The Renaissance; Arthur Symons, “The Decadent Movement in Literature”; Mario Praz, from The Romantic Agony; George Bataille, from Literature and Evil; Richard Ellmann, “The Uses of Decadence”; Richard Gilman, from Decadence: The Strange Life of an Epithet; Linda Dowling, “The Decadent and The New Woman”; Michael Riffaterre, “Decadent Paradoxes,” Leo Bersani, from The Culture of Redemption; Regenia Gagnier, “Individualism, Decadence, and Globalization,” Vincent Sherrry, from Decadence and the Reinvention of Modernism”; Matthew Potolsky, from The Decadent Republic of Letters. There will be a mid-term paper as well as a final paper.

ENGL 79020. Reading Ecologies.

Jessica Yood. Thursday 4:15-6:15 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3309.

How do we read now? Why does it matter?

This course takes an expansive look at the role of reading in our lives, in the academy, and in American culture from the 1920s to the present. Our focus will be on the novels, short stories, memoirs, critical theories, rhetorical concepts, cognitive insights, curricular manifestations, technological influences, and pedagogies that shape what we mean by reading and what we do when we read.

Authors whose work we will explore include: Brandt, Iser, Derrida, Barthes, Akbar, Lu, Ong, Z. Smith, Grief, Felski, Christian, Guillory, Anzaldúa, Rothman, Rule, Shipka, Carillo. In addition to talking and writing about reading, we will also experiment with reading activities and platforms, both during class and online.

The questions that open this course echo the questions that prompted Amy Wan’s Spring 2026 course, “Writing Ecologies” (ENGL 79020). It is absolutely not necessary to have taken that class in order to take this one. Yet we will also draw on the concept of “ecologies” and I will take as a starting point the complex relationship between reading and writing. Students from all disciplines and areas of study welcome.

ENGL 80200. The Appetite of Thought: Exercises in Eco-Techno Criticism & Reading.

Joan Richardson. Thursday 2:00-4:00 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3308.

What is the difference between “thinking” and being “inhabited by thought”? What are the “steps to an ecology of mind,” that can lead us to understand mind as continuous/one with the processes of nature? What do the Turing patterns common to the fertilization of eggs by sperm and to the rivers of galaxies observed in the universe have to do with what we perceive as beauty? What does satisfying our hunger for beauty have to do with thinking? We will explore these questions and others in our discussions this term. The phrases in quotation marks above are borrowed from Ralph Waldo Emerson and William Bateson, the anthropologist/semiotician/cyberneticist who already in the 1950s gave us the term “double bind,” describing the kind of messaging inducive of schizophrenia. This kind of “doublespeak” (a combination of “doublethink” and “Newspeak” from George Orwell’s 1984) is typical of what Zadie Smith has recently called the “colonization of attention” by digital manipulation. Our readings will include selections from the writers mentioned here as well as from others concerned with the complex interrelations of human thinking/imagining/“natural” processing both in themselves and under the pressures of ever-increasing industrialization and, consequently, our not having time to think but being fed sound bites and memes instead by social media algorithms.

While our perception today is threatened—or at least confused, thwarted, vexed—by some of the latest developments in technology (i.e., GPT-5 and its variants...), there are at the same time other developments—like the James Webb Telescope, the Simonyi Survey Telescope at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, investigations of the deep sea vents on the ocean floor—that wonderfully expand our understanding about and into “the exquisite environment of fact” we inhabit, concomitantly extending our “family” connections, realizing our “bond to all that dust.” This extension—remembering that the Greek root oikos meaning “family” gives us the eco- of “ecology” and “economy”—now includes recognizing ourselves to be the habitations, hosts to hosts in our microbiomes, of descendants of some bacteria that were the first life forms on earth (as Lynn Margulis in Symbiotic Planet prepared us to learn).

In addition to selections from the writers already noted, we will also consider as exercises to think with extracts from William James’ “The Stream of Thought”; Henry David Thoreau’s Walden; Stanley Cavell’s The Senses of Walden; Norbert Wiener’s The Human Use of Human Beings; Wilson Harris on “quantum imagination”; Jean-Francois Lyotard’s The Inhuman; Emanuele Coccia’s The Life of Plants; Peter Godfrey Smith’s Metazoa: Animal Life and the Birth of the Mind; Bruce Clarke’s Gaian Systems…Neocybernetics, and the End of the Anthropocene. We will conclude the term reading Samantha Harvey’s Orbital (winner of the 2024 Booker Prize), thinking and feeling about, for example, what happens to mind/minding/imagining when experiencing 16 sunrises and sunsets in “1” day, as do the crews of the International Space Station, a most successful technology.

“It can never be satisfied, the mind, never”: It matters what you pay attention to.

ENGL 80600. Close Reading: Then and Now.

Stephanie Hershinow. Tuesday 11:45 am-1:45 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3212.

Recent work by John Guillory, Jonathan Kramnick, Dan Sinykin, and Johanna Winant has revived the practice of close reading as a method worthy of sustained critical analysis. Of course, close reading has never gone away; for many of us it has remained central to our pedagogical practice even as it became theoretically unfashionable. This course will introduce students to the history of close reading, from its British (I.A. Richards, Empson) and American (Wimsatt, Brooks, etc.) origins, master practitioners (Auerbach, Miller, Sedgwick, and more) through later critiques (especially of the racial politics of the Agrarian New Critics), and finally to its recent reemergence as a site of potential for the future of the literary humanities. We will consider this disciplinary history alongside questions about our own critical practice and pedagogical commitments. Students from all subfields welcome.

ENGL 80600. Fascism and Form.

Eric Lott. Wednesday 6:30 pm-8:30 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 4419.

In a well known book, Fredric Jameson proposed we study Marxism and Form; this course explores its meiotic obverse, fascism and form (informed by Jameson’s subsequent study of Wyndham Lewis, Fables of Aggression). Spurred as well by Stuart Hall's writings on Thatcherism, Ernesto Laclau’s work on Peronism, and the Black Radical Tradition’s confrontations with supremacist ideologies that gave rise to Hitler and Mussolini, we will pursue a disciplined inquiry into theoretical and textual dimensions of fascist discourse and infrastructure down to our own moment of crisis. We’ll try to gauge the way fascist impulses make their way into cultural productions seemingly distant from them as well as avowedly devoted to them, and locate traditions of anti-fascist study and cultural practice that might help fight global fascism’s advance in the present. The course will range across such familiar topics as the Italian Futurists, certain U.S. modernists, Louis-Ferdinand Céline, Walter Benjamin's search for anti-fascist flashpoints, Ezra Pound's pamphlet Jefferson and/or Mussolini, Kenneth Burke’s reading of Mein Kampf, and cultural responses to Franco’s Spain; we’ll also take up Nazi jurist and political theorist Carl Schmitt's devotion to Herman Melville, Trump mentor (and Angels in America central character) Roy Cohn’s tutelage by Mussolini devotee Generoso Pope, Philip K. Dick’s The Man in the High Castle, Paul Gilroy's reading of Garveyism as fascism, and Susan Sontag on Hans-Jurgen Syberberg and Leni Riefenstahl. Other matters that may well come to the fore are current developments like J.D. Vance’s Hillbilly Elegy progeny from Curtis Yarvin to Groypermania; MAGA-rally spectacle, nexus of Von Dutch cap and Il Duce; the new-Right literary firmament; the weaponization of anti-antisemitism; music from Morgan Wallen to Elon Musk’s ex-wife Grimes; the not-so-secret monumentalism of films like The Brutalist and the Confederate monument deconstruction of Kara Walker; the popularization of siege-guerilla madcap in Eddington no less than One Battle After Another and Sinners; and the barricade as will and idea, from Trump’s border fetish to anti-fascist barricades from Ferguson to Minneapolis. Theorists apart from the above certain to include Simone Weil, Hannah Arendt on “totalitarianism,” the Felix Guattari of “Everybody Wants to Be a Fascist,” Angela Y. Davis, Michel Foucault, Klaus Theweleit’s Male Fantasies, Giorgio Agamben, Kristin Ross’s Communal Luxury, Alberto Toscano’s Late Fascism, Achille Mbembe’s Brutalism, Sophie Lewis on abolishing the family, and Andrea Long Chu on trans unfreedoms.

ENGL 80700. Premodern Families: Sex, Celibacy, Marriage, and Community in the Middle Ages.

Glenn Burger. Wednesday 4:15-6:15 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 8203.

(Cross-listed with Medieval Studies Certificate Program – MSCP 80500)

This course examines the wide variety of “families” structuring medieval social and cultural formations: notably extended families (organized around kinship, bloodline, and the exchange of property); spiritual families (monastic and other religious communities, spiritual marriages, the cult of the dead); and nuclear families (organized around consensual sacramental marriage and the bourgeois/gentry household).

We will begin by considering how a medieval privileging of virginity and celibacy as the highest manifestations of human nature might foster alternative conceptions of family in the various communities established to foster such a spiritual life. We will examine monastic community (Aelred of Rievaulx’s Spiritual Friendship), lay spiritual communities such as anchorites and beguines (the Ancrene Wisse), and spiritual marriage (The Book of Margery Kempe and Chaucer’s Second Nun’s Tale). We will also consider the threat of deformation to such spiritual family life posed by inter-religious marriage (represented in the popular romance, The King of Tars). And we will examine the complex interpenetration of living and dead family members that the development of Purgatory in late medieval culture demanded (with reference to the Middle English romance, Apollonius of Tyre).
From the twelfth to the sixteenth century the married estate underwent a profound revaluation as an important sacrament of the Church. In addition, a developing market economy and wide-scale urbanization also foregrounded the growing value of the bourgeois nuclear family and household. We will examine the impact of this sacramentalization of marriage on representations of the Holy Family in cycle dramas and the aristocratic family in romance (Chretien de Troyes’ Eric et Enide) and conduct literature (The Book of the Knight of La Tour Landry). And we will consider the reorientation of gender and sexuality in the bourgeois and gentry married household in such texts as The Good Wife’s Guide [Le Menagier de Paris] (a conduct book written by an older merchant for his fifteen year old bride), middle class English romances such as the Tale of Florence and Chaucer’s Franklin’s and Wife of Bath’s Tale, the journees chretiennes (guides for living a Christian life written for lay men and women), as well as Jan van Eyck’s famous depiction of a bourgeois married couple in his 1436 painting, The Arnolfini Portrait. 

ENGL 82100. Early Modern Sexuality in a Global Context.

Mario DiGangi. Tuesday 6:30-8:30 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3309.

(Cross-listed with Global Early Modern Studies Certificate Program – GEMS 72100; Comparative Literature – CL 80100; Liberal Studies – MALS 74600)

This course examines the histories and discourses of early modern sexuality through colonial, mercantile, interreligious, and imaginative encounters. We will explore sexual cultures, ideologies, and communities across Europe (England, Spain, Florence, Rome, Germany), the Ottoman Empire, Africa, India, and the Far East, as well as the role of sexuality in the Atlantic slave trade. We will address indigenous understandings of sexuality and colonial persecution of sexual crimes in Nahua societies, colonial New Spain, Brazil, and Mexico. The course will also focus on some exceptional historical figures in Europe and the Americas: Pocahontas, Malintzin, and Catalina de Erauso, the latter of whom is the subject of Juan Ruiz de Alarcón’s play, La Famosa Comedia de la Monja Alférez, which we will read in a new English translation. Attention will be given to archival records, visual culture, poetry, travel narrative, and drama.

ENGL 83500. Life Writing, 1600-1800: Personal and Political Histories.

Carrie Hintz. Tuesday 6:30-8:30 pm. 2/3/4 credits. Hyflex synchronous: Room 6494.

We will explore life writing produced from 1600-1800: auto/biography, diaries and letters, narratives of enslavement, conversion narratives, criminal biographies, and travel writing. What do these narratives teach us about the political and social formations of the period, including colonialization, enslavement, the role of women, ideas of sovereignty, and the human impact on the natural world? We will also consider theories of authorship, narrative, and life writing’s relationship to the early novel, with authors including (but not limited to) Richard Baxter, John Bunyan, Agnes Beaumont, Charlotte Charke, John Jacques Rousseau, Helen Maria Williams, Mary Wollstonecraft, and Mary Prince. An extended unit on The Interesting Narrative of Olaudah Equiano will allow us to delve into its complex textual, critical, and political history. The course will be useful for seventeenth- and eighteenth-century specialists and students working on life writing in any period.

ENGL 85400. Latinx NYC.

Melissa Garsow-Castillo. Friday 11:45 am-1:45 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3308.

While there is debate about when the “first” Latinx writings came about in New York City, what is clear is that by the early 19th century, our city became the epicenter of Latin American intellectual expression. New York City’s literary history includes 200 years of Latinx contributions spanning from dissidents and revolutionaries of the Spanish crown like Cubans Jose Heredia and Jose Martí, to those affected by US imperialism past and present such as Puerto Rican Luisa Capetillo or Dominican Angie Cruz, to people who migrate for educational or economic opportunities, or increasingly to escape violence and climate change. Whatever their diverse backgrounds, New York City has supported a diverse Latinx community whose writings are a testament to an influential writing community that has led the way in literary experimentation and innovation in a city that is over 27.5 percent Latinx.

This course will focus on Latinx writers from New York City past and present as a way to examine major literary moments and movements in Latinx Literature as well as interlocking issues including race, immigration, gentrification, gender, and sexuality. Drawing on urban studies and spatial theory, we will consider various genres of Latinx writing including treatices, journalism, poetry, theater, fiction, and memoir to understand the development of Latinx New York, a diverse set of communities living in the global center of neoliberal culture and consumption.

ENGL 86600. The Political Economy of Decolonial Forms.

Peter Hitchcock. Wednesday 2:00-4:00 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3207.

The disarticulation of colonialism and coloniality takes many forms across the social, the political, the economic, etc. but how might one specify decolonizing the mind in terms of cultural expression and, particularly, literary form? Is the latter merely an effect of, for instance, political/economic demands or does it have a more active presence in transforming the foundations of a colonizing world system as such? This course will examine the antinomies and entanglements of literariness and literary form with economic and political structures. One aim throughout will be to understand what is being decolonized and how an articulation of form might contribute to decolonial processes (of modernity, of the state, of thinking). Avowedly interdisciplinary, we will explore the interanimation of the aesthetic and the economic, the idea and the ideological. Moving among and within fiction, drama, poetry, and non-fiction we will consider several major examples of anti-, de-, and post-colonial writing as both forms of dissent and as dissent in form. Such counter-critique will also introduce us to key examples of theory in this regard. Together the literature and theory are not offered as a formalism but as a way to decolonize political economy itself.

ENGL 86700. Palestine is the Compass: Propaganda / Deep Politics / Poetics.

Ammiel Alcalay. Monday 6:30-8:30 pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3309.

(Cross-listed with Middle Eastern Studies – MES 76900; Comparative Literature – CL 80100)

The US population remains impervious to the outsized pressure it exerts on the rest of the world. The impact of the live-streamed genocide in Gaza/Palestine has moved more people to call into question basic assumptions about their governments, institutions, values, priorities, allegiances, and cultural practices. Academia and the arts have seen severe restrictions, curtailment of free speech, self-censorship, and an even more drastic reduction of imaginative horizons.

How did we get here? While Palestinian texts and visual materials will serve as focal points, background in and reference to US propaganda techniques and key moments—from Bernays through WWII, the Cold War, the Korean War, and the wars in Southeast Asia, to 9/11, the war on terror, and the wars on Afghanistan and Iraq—will be referred to, along with some background as to how Ancient Near Eastern polities and cultures have been interpreted and represented.

We will draw examples from the poetics and practices of a wide range of writers, artists, filmmakers, thinkers, political prisoners, and revolutionaries whose works resist and enact liberation from military, economic, and ideological domination.

Examples will be drawn from Palestine, Iraq, Egypt, Lebanon, Syria, Algeria and other places, but also by seeing and trying to displace ourselves through encounters with US culture, the hegemonic and antithetical uses of American English, and forms of representation in the visual arts, film, photography, the press, and digital platforms. Throughout, we will consider historical, ideological, political, formal, methodological, and archival questions.

ENGL 89000. Mining the Archives.

David Reynolds. Tuesday 2:00-4:00 pm. 2/3/4 credits. Online.

(Cross-listed with American Studies Certificate Program – ASCP 82000; Biography and Memoir)

During the past two decades, a revolution has occurred in scholarship: troves of archival materials that were once very hard to access and search have been digitized and put online. ENGL 75000. The American Renaissance.

David Reynolds. Tuesday, 2:00-4:00 pm. Online synchronous.

(Cross-listed with History – HIST 74900, American Studies Certificate Program – ASCP 81500, Biography and Memoir – BAM 70400-05, and MALS - elective)

The American Renaissance (1835-65) is widely acknowledged as the height of American cultural expression. This era featured significant writings on race and slavery by Frederick Douglass, Harriet Jacobs, Harriet Beecher Stowe, and others, whose arguments, condemned by slavery’s supporters, initiated the cultural battles that continue to this day, as seen in the recent debates over The 1619 Project during the Trump era. The American Renaissance also saw advances in ecological awareness and philosophy through Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau; the metaphysical depth and cultural diversity expressed in the fiction of Herman Melville and Nathaniel Hawthorne; the innovative poetry of Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson; and the psychological exploration and groundbreaking aesthetics of Edgar Allan Poe. Margaret Fuller and other feminists strongly advocated for women’s rights. Abolitionist efforts ranged from William Lloyd Garrison’s rhetorical militancy to John Brown’s armed actions. Lincoln’s speeches embodied the nation’s persistent political themes. This period also experienced a rise in other cultural areas, exemplified by Hudson River School paintings, illustrated magazines, and the early forms of popular music. When examining key works from this time, we look at approaches to cultural history and American Studies. The course also explores how online archives and AI can help generate new insights that lead to original scholarship and publishable writing. Class members will be encouraged to pursue their own interests within this broad interdisciplinary framework.

ENGL 80200. The Appetite of Thought: Exercises in Eco-Techno Criticism & Reading.

Joan Richardson. Thursday, 2:00-4:00 pm. In person: 4419.

What is the difference between “thinking” and being “inhabited by thought”? What are the “steps to an ecology of mind,” that can lead us to understand mind as continuous/one with the processes of nature? What do the Turing patterns common to the fertilization of eggs by sperm and to the rivers of galaxies observed in the universe have to do with what we perceive as beauty? What does satisfying our hunger for beauty have to do with thinking? We will explore these questions and others in our discussions this term. The phrases in quotation marks above are borrowed from Ralph Waldo Emerson and William Bateson, the anthropologist/semiotician/cyberneticist who already in the 1950s gave us the term “double bind,” describing the kind of messaging inducive of schizophrenia. Our readings will include selections from the writers mentioned here as well as from others concerned with the complex interrelations of human thinking/imagining/“natural” processing both in themselves and under the pressures of ever-increasing industrialization and, consequently, our not having time to think but being fed sound bites and memes instead by social media algorithms.

While our perception today is threatened—or at least confused, thwarted, vexed—by some of the latest developments in technology (i.e., GPT-5 and its variants...), there are at the same time other developments—like the James Webb Telescope, the Simonyi Survey Telescope at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, investigations of the deep sea vents on the ocean floor—that wonderfully expand our understanding about and into “the exquisite environment of fact” we inhabit, concomitantly extending our “family” connections, realizing our “bond to all that dust.” This extension— remembering that the Greek root oikos meaning “family” gives us the eco- of “ecology” and “economy”—now includes recognizing ourselves to be the habitations, hosts to hosts in our microbiomes, of descendants of some bacteria that were the first life forms on earth (as Lynn Margulis in Symbiotic Planet prepared us to learn).

We will conclude the term reading Samantha Harvey’s Orbital (winner of the 2024 Booker Prize), thinking and feeling about, for example, what happens to mind/minding/imagining when experiencing 16 sunrises and sunsets in “1” day, as do the crews of the International Space Station, a most successful technology.

“It can never be satisfied, the mind, never”: It matters what you pay attention to.

ENGL 70800. Medieval Nature.

Karl Steel. Mondays 2:00-4:00pm. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3309.

We will necessarily engage with some classics and some contemporary work in ecocriticism too, ranging, for example, from Adorno and Horkheimer and Raymond Williams to up-to-the-minute work in indigeneity. This class will be my first ecocriticism class: get in on the ground floor!

Although reading work in the original languages will be encouraged, all texts will be available in modern English translation. Course work will include two written presentations and, for those taking it for 3 or 4 credits, a seminar paper.

ENGL 80600. Anthropocene Investigations.

Alexander Schlutz. Mondays 2:00 p.m. - 4:00 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person: 3207.

The term “Anthropocene,” first introduced by the chemist and Nobel Laureate Paul Crutzen and the biologist Eugene Stoermer over twenty years ago, has by now become the most widely used designation for the current period of global, human-induced environmental catastrophe in both scholarly and public discourse. The appropriateness of the term (though of course not the global crisis it seeks to highlight) has, however, been subject to vigorous critique in the social sciences and the humanities, mainly due to its problematic naturalization of the human and its erasure of crucial questions of human difference and responsibility. From the perspective of the humanities in particular, a return to a species narrative, with an undifferentiated anthropos writ large as the protagonist, can seem to erase in one fell swoop decades of scholarly work in critique of essentialist conceptions of “the human.” A range of alternatives, from Capitalocene to Chtulucene, have been proposed in an effort to alter the narrative parameters in order to call anthropocene grand narratives into question.

At the same time, a growing number of scholars in the humanities take seriously the challenge of the “Anthropocene” to rethink what viable narratives about and representations of the relationships of human beings to their environments might look like at a moment of global crisis where human and natural history can no longer be disentangled. Such attempts include a newly framed engagement with literature and art more broadly as modes of representation that might allow us to bring the contemporary human predicament into view in different ways than scientific data and public policy debates.

To address these overlapping discussions, this seminar will offer a two-fold investigation. On the one hand, we will attempt to take stock of the disciplinary discussion surrounding the “Anthropocene” and examine a range of critical perspectives and proposed alternatives in naming and timeline. At the same time, we will also turn our attention to emergent transdisciplinary approaches in the environmental humanities, as well as to the creative practice in literature and the arts, in order to investigate what a poetics for the “Anthropocene” might look like. Our theoretical interlocutors will include Bruno Latour, Donna Haraway, Kathryn Yussof, Robin Wall Kimmerer, and T.J. Demos, among others.

ENGL 86600: Decolonizing the State in Theory and Culture.

Peter Hitchcock. Wednesdays 4:15 p.m. to 6:15 p.m. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3305.

The defeat of imperial, colonial, and settler-colonial forces has historical dates that often hinge on declarations of autonomy and independence, yet any “rendezvous of victory” (Césaire) in this regard acknowledges decolonization as a complex process of culture, politics, and economics with a longue durée extending into the present. In decolonization, the form of autonomy is never less than vexed and often pitches revolutionary desire against the inertia of colonialism by other means in key fields of concern. One of these, of course, is the function and form of the state as both a celebration of delinking and as a field of contradiction for postcolonialism tout court. Theories of state have long been a battleground in Marxism and radical thought of various kinds. In anti-colonialism and anti-racism, many nuanced critiques, like Fanon’s in “The Pitfalls of National Consciousness,” offer a window onto the limits of state (as nation, as territory, as dependency/commonwealth, as “départements et régions d’outre-mer,” as “client”, etc.). This course will examine how different postcolonial and decolonial theories address the contradictory structures and meanings of the state for decolonized futures. While state phobia can be a useful heuristic in challenging the norms of colonial sovereignty and law (a feature postcolonialism sometimes shares with both poststructuralism and anarchism), there is also a genuine fear of statelessness and symptoms of social instability that might provide an opening for regional incursion and modes of neocolonialism. We will consider the state as a basic nostrum of liberal modernization, yet in tension with a postcolonial prerogative, where modernization is a response to colonial underdevelopment, not just in terms of economic stimuli, but with regard to building an institutional infrastructure honed to distribute social well-being beyond imperial prescriptions. There is no magic formula for such delinking, in part because the world order overdetermines just how autonomous a postcolonial state can be. Perhaps one can trade locally and regionally with other postcolonial states but if broader aid is required there is appropriate suspicion of the available options, including Global North-inspired institutions like the World Bank or the IMF. If the path to statehood features nation building, this has often come with dependency and crippling indebtedness. Can one decolonize the state without squandering the liberation achieved by anti-colonial struggle? Is this where postcolonial culture makes its mark, as an imaginary resolution of real [state] contradictions? Or is culture a vital arena of elaboration (elabore, working out) where the concepts of decolonization and the state are demystified, rethought, or otherwise transformed? Rather than take the position decolonization will inexorably dissolve the state into a form of civil society more worthy of the term (both civil and social beyond discrimination based on race, gender, and class, for instance) we will explore a set of material case studies in which decolonization struggles with the state as neither the apotheosis nor nadir of its greater aims. The hope is that by taking seriously the state in its form and logic we will be better equipped to unravel the terms of its decolonization as a methodology, as a reading practice. Theoretical genealogies will be drawn from Marx, Lenin, Fanon, Spivak, J.Butler, Luxemburg, Niang, Davidson, Chandhoke, Chatterjee, Stoler, Anderson, Scott, and Hall, among others. The cultural case studies will develop out of reading, for example, Farah, Mukasonga, Roy, Condé, Glissant, Kanafani, El Saadawi, Ngũgĩ, and O.Butler. Coursework requirements will be decided on the basis of credits and instructor consultation.

ENGL 80700: Conversion and Identity in the Middle Ages.

ENGL 89000: Mining the Archives, Reinterpreting the Past.

David Reynolds. Tuesdays 2:00 p.m. to 4:00 p.m. 2/3/4 credits. Online. Cross listed as ASCP 82000 Section 02.

During the past two decades, a revolution has occurred in scholarship: troves of archival materials that were once very hard to access and search have been digitized and put online. Rare books and manuscripts; entire runs of newspapers; obscure pamphlets; letters; visual and audio files—these are some of the rich resources that are now universally available and instantly searchable. The implications for the study of literature, history, biography, popular culture, and are immense. ENGL 86500: Reading Edward Said (and His Critics).

Anthony Alessandrini. Thursdays 6:30 p.m. - 8:30 p.m. 2/3/4 credits. In person: Room 3207.

Edward Said—as a literary and cultural critic, as a political analyst and activist, as a public intellectual, and as an eloquent voice for Palestinian liberation—remains, twenty years after his untimely death, one of the preeminent radical intellectuals of our time. His work sits at the center of Middle Eastern studies, even as his most influential book, Orientalism, undoes the very narrative that suggests that our round globe can be neatly divided into “West” and “East.” Said’s work has also influenced debates in literary and cultural studies, anthropology, gender studies, and political theory, among other fields, while also providing a political vocabulary for a generation of activists engaged in anti-war, anti-occupation, and anti-racist organizing. Orientalism marks one of the distinctive aspects of Said’s career that we will examine: on the one hand, you don’t even have to know who Edward Said is to know what is meant when someone refers to an idea, a political policy, or a television series as “Orientalist”; on the other hand, the book itself is largely the work of a literary critic, with close readings of writers and scholars whose work is hardly familiar to many modern readers.

In this course, we will make our way through the remarkable body of work that Said produced, across a variety of disciplines, styles, and genres, throughout his career. While the course is grounded in Said’s engagement with and influence on Middle Eastern studies, our close reading of Said will also bring us into conversation with the political and intellectual methods and theoretical approaches that frame his work—including Marxism, poststructuralism, and psychoanalysis, along with methods drawn from the Frankfurt School and from postcolonial studies, a field that Said both helped to found and also found reason to criticize—in major works such as Orientalism, The World, the Text, and the Critic, and Culture and Imperialism. We will also chart Said’s participation in, and writing about, Palestinian liberation, from his foundational 1979 book The Question of Palestine, to his engagements with Palestinian culture and politics in After the Last Sky, The Politics of Dispossession, and Out of Place, to his sharp critiques of the Oslo Accords and their aftermath, in Peace and Its Discontents and The End of the Peace Process. We’ll conclude with Said’s remarkable late work and his lovingly agonistic relationship with humanism in works like Freud and the Non-European, Humanism and Democratic Criticism, and On Late Style. Along the way, we’ll place Said’s work in conversation with figures who influenced him (including Talal Asad, Ibrahim Abu-Lughod, Antonio Gramsci, Raymond Williams, and Theodor Adorno) and those who he in turn has influenced (including Lisa Lowe, Lila Abu-Lughod, Barbara Harlow, Anjuli Raza Kolb, Sophia Azeb, and Wael Hallaq). And since Said was an unapologetic polemicist who was not afraid to make enemies, both intellectual and political, we’ll spend some time with his critics, from both the left and right.

ENGL 80800. women, rape culture, and masculinity in the Cantebury Tales.

Glenn Burger. Fridays 11:45 a.m. - 1:45 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person: Room 3310A.

Since its discovery in 1873 by the prominent medieval scholar, F. J. Furnival, the record of Cecily Champaigne’s release of Chaucer for “all manner of actions related to my raptus [de raptu meo] or any other thing or cause” has raised the troubling spectre of “Chaucer the rapist.” New documents recently discovered by Sebastian Sobecki and Euen Roger suggest that Chaucer and Chaumpaigne, rather than rivals in a rape charge, may instead have been codefendants against a third party, Thomas Staundon. If these recent discoveries shift our focus on “Chaucer the man” away from his sex life, they don’t undo over a century of (largely male) enthusiasm for a poet whose “bawdiness” in the Canterbury Tales manifested a viral heterosexual manliness “we” all could enjoy. Nor do they erase the extent to which masculinities constructed on the exploitation of and violence to women’s bodies are front and center in so much of the Canterbury Tales. This seminar thus takes up Samantha Seal’s call to disentangle Chaucer’s poetry from his sex life and reimagine the connection between poet and reader, poet and critic, in ways that no longer exploit the imaginary flesh of female bodies: “If there is a debt owed to Cecily Chaumpaigne, to all the women (and men) who had to come to Chaucer through the hazy literary mist of his critics’ casual lust and not-so-genial fantasies of male likeness, it is to find this paradigm, to find a way of reading—and of reading Chaucer—that no longer makes modern masculinity the lens through which we seize upon the past.”

In this seminar we will consider Chaucer’s— and Chaucerians’—vexed role in the enduring structures of rape culture, which Anastasia Powell and Nicola Henry define “as a constellation of widespread attitudes, practices, and myths in which sexual violence is tolerated, accepted, eroticised, mini¬mised and trivialized.” We will begin with a brief consideration of the archival evidence for Chaucer’s relationship with Cecily Champaigne, late medieval English legal understanding and use of raptus, as well as a patriarchal critical tradition that constructs Chaucer the man and poet as truly “universal” and “one of us” because he manifests a virile heterosexual masculinity. We will then turn to the relationship between representations of “rough love” and artisanal masculinities in the fabliaux of the Tales (notably the Miller’s, Reeve’s, Cook’s, Merchant’s, and Shipman’s Tales). We will also consider the extent to which “gentle” masculinities—in the Knight’s, Man of Law’s, Clerk’s, and Franklin’s Tales—truly distinguish themselves from such modes or instead simply re-present the same traffic in women and sexual economy in different clothing. To what extent are these masculinist sexual economies laid open to view by Chaucer? And to what extent are we thus asked to think critically about the libidinal excitement they arouse and to question their ability to construct an erotics for the Tales? If the matter of woman looms large in these tales, are there other places in the Tales that might encourage an alternative asexual/queer erotics to emerge?

ENGL 86000. Literature of the Great War: Modernism, Memory, and the Poetics of History.

Richard Kaye. Mondays 6:30 p.m. - 8:30 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In person: Room 5382.

Today most literary critics and cultural historians understand the First World War as crucially determining twentieth-century literature as well as “modernity.” This course explores creative and intellectual responses to the Great War (1914-1918) by focusing on the changes that wartime experience fostered in national identity, gender and racial relations, sexual attitudes, psychoanalysis, prevailing conceptions of historical progress, and the aesthetic strategies of writers. In an exploration of fiction, poetry, memoir, film, music, painting and sculpture, we will consider the close relation between personal trauma and historical catastrophe, as major and lesser-known writers turn to the war. Readings will begin with Thomas Hardy’s ironic elegies that reflect a shift from Victorian to modernist poetics as well as E. M. Forster’s Edwardian-era Howards End (1910), with its nostalgia for an undestroyed, rural England and its sense of future precarity. With Virginia Woolf’s short story “The Mark on the Wall” (1917), the war becomes the occasion for Woolf’s first attempt at modernist fiction, one that reflected her skepticism about the (to her, indistinguishable) realms of government propaganda and the daily newspaper. In her novel Jacob’s Room (1922), we discover an unsentimental, retrospective depiction of Britain on the edge of war and then, through the death in combat of the novel’s titular protagonist, as it is engulfed by it. In addition to the writings of soldier-combatants such as Robert Graves, Siegfried Sassoon, Rupert Brooke, Wilfred Owen, Julian Grenfell, and Isaac Rosenberg we will consider the different responses of women writers and artists such as Radclyffe Hall, Käthe Kollwitz, Virginia Woolf, Gertrude Stein, Vera Brittain, and Rebecca West, whose novel The Return of the Soldier (1918) was the first fictional treatment of “male hysteria” (shell shock), a psychological condition once understood as a wholly “female malady.” In a consideration of several pivotal works of fiction— the French writer Henri Barbusse’s best-selling Under Fire (1916), which Ernest Hemingway described as “the only good” book to come out of the First World War, Woolf’s Mrs. Dalloway (1925), with its shell-shocked soldier Septimus Smith, and Ford Madox Ford’s experiment in historical impressionism, Parade’s End (1924-27)—we will examine how the new, subjectivist techniques of modernism, once critiqued by contemporary critics as occluding historical actualities, indirectly register wartime experience. Often Great War writing is emphatically political about gender and racial disparities. In Gwendolyn B. Bennett’s short story, “Wedding Day” (1926), a Black American prize fighter and soldier discovers a fragile empowerment and then total disillusionment in post-war Paris. Woolf’s experience of the war helped to generate some of her most avowedly feminist and pacifist writing. Meanwhile, the homo-social environment of the trenches allowed for the emergence of new male-male sexualities everywhere evident in First World War poetry. Similarly, the entry of women into the work force allowed for new identities and sexualities, as in Hall’s story “Miss Ogilvy Finds Herself” (1934).

The politically divergent Primitivist, Futurist, and Dadaist art movements took their inspiration from widespread militarism and institutional breakdown across Europe, while Freud’s “talking cure” became instrumental in the treatment of shell shock. Meanwhile, Freud’s psychoanalytic theory of the "death drive" and grim assessment of so-called “civilization” gained intellectual sustenance from mass slaughter. We will view two films, Abel Gance’s silent movie “J’Accuse” (1919), some of whose actors were doomed soldiers on leave, and Stanley Kubrick’s excoriation of top military brass, “Paths of Glory” (1957). Because of the international literary scope of First World War, the course will take up Anglo-American (often short fictional texts) by D. H. Lawrence. Rudyard Kipling, Joseph Conrad, Woolf, Gertrude Stein, Edith Wharton, Hemingway, and William Faulkner as well as French and German works. We will consider David Jones’ In Parentheses (1937) for its innovative upending of lyric conventions, T.S. Eliot The Waste Land (1922) for its evocation of a shattered post-war landscape, and W. H. Auden’s (neglected) modernist long poem The Orators: An English Study (1932) in terms of its animus against the British establishment as well as for its depiction of a suicidal, sexually conflicted British airman. Not all wartime literary works assumed an anti-war stance, however. Ernst Jünger’s wartime memoir Storm of Steel (1920) envisioned military combat as an ultimate (and apolitical) test of masculine prowess, while Edmund Blunden’s autobiographical account of his combat experience in France, Undertones of War (1928), sought to present a radically unsentimental, non-judgmental depiction of combat. We will consider one of the enduring, central myths of the First World War—namely, that it ushered in “modernity.” Although Marcel Proust imagined that the war had destroyed the old social hierarchies, arguably it was the Second Empire that had done so, while recent historians have noted how the First World War witnessed a rise in spiritualism (as grieving families sought to communicate with the dead), a return to racial polarities (as Black soldiers returned from service to experience persistent racism), and a submergence of proto-feminist aspirations (as women largely relinquished the Suffragette cause for the war effort). That “modernity” is at least partially a myth may also be true for some artistic “breakthroughs.” While Benjamin Britten’s War Requiem (1962) explicitly drew on the war poetry of Wilfred Owen, Britten himself claimed that musically he was indebted to Verdi. Contemporary authors have accessed the First World War for other purposes, among them Pat Barker in her dramatization of the fate of Siegfried Sassoon and that of the “ordinary” soldier in Regeneration (1991) and Geoff Dyer in his genre-defying, elegiac The Missing of the Somme (1994). Among the literary and cultural critics as well as historians we will consider: James Joll, Paul Fussell, Elaine Showalter, Samuel Hynes, Susan Gubar and Sandra Gilbert, Jay Winter, Adrian Caesar, Paul Lerner, and Joanna Bourke. Requirements: A mid-term, a ten-minute class presentation, and a final paper that may be adapted from the mid-term paper.

ENGL 86800. The Politics of Liberation.

Siraj Ahmed. Mondays 6:30 PM - 8:30 PM. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person: Room 4422.

From its late fifteenth-century origins forward, the process of modern state formation has created, with much fanfare, populations who claim to be sovereign and free by virtue of their national belonging. Over and over again, both inside and outside the borders of the state system, this process has also created, which much less fanfare, generally racialized peoples whom it attempts to deny political agency and to treat as effectively non-sovereign. These peoples live in circumstances of ‘organized state abandonment,’ in Ruth Wilson Gilmore’s incisive phrase. They are exposed, differentially, to unnatural death in its various forms: not just to the sovereign power to take life but also to the sovereign prerogative to let die, to slow violence, to social death, et cetera. We might consider this dynamic—the creation of sovereign populations whose economic and political security appears to depend on the inverse creation of non-sovereign peoples—a paradigmatically colonial logic. Yet, long after the supposed end of colonial rule, this logic has only become more world-encompassing, systemic, and intractable than ever. And if we were to consider even longer times frames, we might find that practically every socio-political form—not just secular states but also, for example, the sacred communities that preceded them—creates both sovereign and non-sovereign people, those whom it protects and those whom it does not.

If so, the antithesis of coloniality would not be any past or future society that intellectuals or revolutionaries could reconstruct or imagine. It would lie instead in the insistent and concrete demand of non-sovereign communities for liberation from the particular necropolitical conditions in which they live. This class will reflect on the question of how we can practice a critical method that keeps faith with the demand for liberation in this form. It proposes that such a method would no longer turn written texts, cultural traditions, and historical periods into the reified objects of scholarly knowledge, into things scholars may claim to know. As we shall see, that transformation is a distinctive feature of colonial modernity. We will try instead to read texts in terms of a constitutive, always ongoing, and irresolvable conflict between sovereign and non-sovereign forms of being—between the varied systems that expose certain communities to death and the diverse practices that reject those systems—as it has manifested itself across recent history. Our aim will be to re-activate a liberatory energy that, refusing its own objectification, escapes the colonial/academic will to knowledge.

Theoretical readings may include Walter Benjamin on divine violence and messianic justice; Hannah Arendt on liberation and freedom; Frantz Fanon on revolutionary violence and national consciousness; Michel Foucault on political spirituality; Antonio Negri and Michael Hardt on constituent power; Giorgio Agamben on destituent potential; as well as excerpts from Talal Asad’s Genealogies of Religion: Discipline and Reasons of Power in Christianity and Islam and Formations of the Secular: Christianity, Islam, Modernity; Robin D.G. Kelley’s Freedom Dreams: The Black Radical Imagination; Saba Mahmood’s Politics of Piety: The Islamic Revival and the Feminist Subject; Banu Bargu’s Starve and Immolate: The Politics of Human Weapons; Adom Getachew’s Worldmaking after Empire: The Rise and Fall of Self-Determination, and more.

We will also spend extended time with a few literary texts and their historical contexts: e.g., decolonization in North Africa and ‘the Arab world’ via Tayeb Salih’s A Season of Migration to the North; apartheid regimes in South Africa and Palestine via J.M. Coetzee’s The Life and Times of Michael K, Emile Habibi’s The Secret Life of Saeed, the Pessoptimist, and/or Ghassan Kanafani’s Return to Haifa; and proxy wars in Central America and Sri Lanka via Michael Ondaatje’s Anil’s Ghost.

ENGL 89600. Knowledge Infrastructures.

Matthew Gold. Mondays 4:15 PM - 6:15 PM. 2/3/4 Credits. Hybrid: Room 3310A.

Infrastructure is all around us, rarely remarked upon. Indeed, the latent state of infrastructure is part of what marks it as such; as Susan Leigh Starr has noted, infrastructure studies involves the study of "boring things."

This class will explore the emerging nexus of critical infrastructure studies and critical university studies, focusing on how they can be combined with digital humanities approaches to explore the infrastructure of scholarly knowledge. From our libraries to our journals to our conferences to our operating systems to our use of social media, scholars communicate through an entanglement of corporate and commercial interests. Beyond the obviously problematic commercial infrastructures built by predatory publishers and corporate conglomerates such as Elsevier, scholars routinely depend on for-profit publication venues and commercial journals to disseminate their work, and often use enterprise online platforms to teach their classes.

As a set of alternatives to the commercialized infrastructure of knowledge dissemination in the academy, the course will consider open access publication models, free software development, and university press publishing. Even as we explore such alternatives, we will critique them, considering the ways that such alternatives themselves depend upon commercial technical stacks, and considering whether these alternatives are equally available and accessible across the globe.

Topics to be explored include: introductions to critical infrastructure studies and critical university studies; the environmental impact of the cloud; the free software movement; academic publishing models; constructing open platforms. Students in the class will explore publishing platforms collaboratively created by CUNY and other partners, including the CUNY Academic Commons and Manifold, as well as others such as Humanities Commons and Zotero. The goal of the class, in the end, is to ask students to consider how and where their own scholarly knowledge is distributed, by whom, and under what terms.

ENGL 76200.  Capitalism, Colonialism, and Critique.

Peter Hitchcock. Wednesday 2:00 PM - 4:00 PM. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person: Room 3207.

The archive on the vexed relations of capitalism and colonialism is immense but here we will focus on the politics of postcolonial theory and culture. The course has three major aims. First, while colonialism’s economic project is not exactly a secret, we will suggest that part, at least, of its undoing rests on the ability to read capital, to understand the complex processes that produce and reproduce an economic logic as a hegemonic relation. Second, to delve further into capital as relation within colonialism reveals constitutive dimensions of domination that are themselves purposeful in reading practices. We will explore, for instance, racial capitalism as paradigmatic in colonialism. Social reproduction, or gendered divisions of labor in capitalism’s colonial desire, is a similarly vital component of colonial order and resistance to the same. And the ecological implications of capitalism’s colonial adventures offer crucial evidence of the ways in which nature can be made in the image of barbarous exploitation. The third aim is not simply to combine the elements of the first two, but is instead to suggest that reading and writing the postcolonial is hardly adjunct to understanding the solemn and troubled consanguinity of capitalism and colonialism, but sees the literary as a mode of praxis both in the consolidation of colonial culture and in its dissemblance and deracination. Rather than encapsulate the archive of capitalism and colonialism, the course will emphasize what is productively symptomatic within it. On one level, our readings will serve as an introduction to an interdisciplinary politics of postcolonial reading; on another level, the course material will hope to provoke an engagement with your own politics of reading and the archives to which it is directed. As Walter Rodney once noted, colonial education was always hostile to the principle of praxis. Postcolonial critique enjoins the (class) struggle to create praxis anew. Readings will be drawn from among Ngũgĩ, Spivak, Fanon, Robinson, Mbembe, Césaire, Marx, Apostol, Jameson, Gilmore, Katz, Harvey, Balibar, Roy, DeLoughrey, Wynter, Bofane, Mohamed, and Condé. Coursework requirements will be decided on the basis of credits and instructor consultation.

ENGL 80700. Dreaming in the Middle Ages, and Beyond.

Steven Kruger. Wednesdays 11:45 AM - 1:45 PM. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person: Room 3307.

From Genesis to the Wizard of Oz, from Revelation to Prince’s “1999,” dream experience has been central to Western literary and cultural traditions – whether as inspiration for the literary text or as the object of representation and exploration. Dream visions – texts that frame themselves as dreams – emerge as a particularly noteworthy genre in the Middle Ages, with the Dream of the Rood, visions of heaven and hell, Guillaume de Lorris and Jean de Meun’s Roman de la Rose, Boccaccio’s Corbaccio, Chaucer’s four dream poems, Langland’s Piers Plowman, the anonymous Pearl, and poems by most of the major English poets of the fifteenth century (Lydgate, Douglas, Dunbar, Henryson, James I) all exploiting dream experience as the basis for narrative and lyric expression.

The course will center on medieval dream texts such as these, but we will also consider the Biblical and classical traditions that underlie medieval dream narratives, and we will reach beyond the Middle Ages – to early modern texts, to the Romantics, and to the twentieth century – to consider some of the afterlives of medieval dream vision. (The post-medieval texts taken up will depend in part of the interests of students registered for the course.) We will also read widely in the classical, late-antique, and medieval theories of sleep and dreams (from Aristotle to Macrobius and Augustine to Albertus Magnus), and in modern dream theory (from Freud and Jung to Foucault to contemporary neuroscientific explorations of the dream state).

Students taking the course for 2 credits are expected to do the reading, participate in discussions, and do the oral presentations. Students taking the course for 3 or 4 credits also will take on a semester-long project related to the material of the course (with the required length of the final paper varying for the receipt of 3 or 4 credits).

ENGL 89000. Mining the Archives, Reinterpreting the Past.

David Reynolds. Tuesdays 2:00 PM - 4:00 PM. 2/3/4 Credits. Online.

During the past two decades, a revolution has occurred in scholarship: troves of archival materials that were once very hard to access and search have been digitized and put online. Rare books and manuscripts; entire runs of newspapers; obscure pamphlets; letters; visual and audio files—these are some of the rich resources that are now universally available and instantly searchable. The implications for the study of literature, popular culture, history, and biography are immense. With the help of now-available archives, famous figures or writings of the past can be placed in fresh contexts, and new ones can be unearthed. How do we move from the raw material of the archive to the publishable article or book? This course addresses such issues. Students from any field or period concentration will have the opportunity to explore online and physical archives that are especially interesting to them and relevant to their work. Class readings include articles or book chapters about archival research. Students will periodically report to the class about their progress in the archives and will write a term paper based on their research. ENGL 82100 Introduction to Global Early Modern Studies.

Erec R Koch. Tuesdays, 6:30pm-8:30pm. 2/3/4 Credits. In Person: Room 3307. (Cross-listed with GEMS).

This course spans writings from the late sixteenth century through the early eighteenth century, writings that focus on French and British contact with indigenous peoples of North America and the Caribbean.  The fascination for literature of travel and discovery is manifested by the enduring popularity of the widely published works of Vespucci and Jean de Léry on their travels, of accounts of early British colonies in North America, of missionary histories of the Caribbean and New France (Canada) contained in the voluminous annual Jesuit Relations, as well of representations in literature (Montaigne’s Essais) and drama (Shakespeare’s The Tempest, for example, according to some scholars). We will focus on the ways in which writings about encounters with those peoples both held them against European culture as radically “other” and were instrumental in defining European culture, social organization, and ideologies. We will also examine how late seventeenth century and Enlightenment notions of progress and history and the beginnings of anthropology helped to reshape that construction and frame it within ideas of lineal descent and progressive change. In essence, those efforts resulted in recuperating those cultures as a distant and superseded “past” of European cultures, often nuanced by primitive purity and innocence. Readings will include Montaigne, travel memoirs, contemporary accounts of founding of North American colonies by French and English, The Jesuit Relations, Hobbes, Adam Smith, Kant, Diderot, Montesquieu, and Rousseau. ENGL 80600. Asia as study, method, and figure.

Kandice Chuh. Tuesdays 2:00 p.m. - 4:00 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person. 

Taking flight from Kuan-Hsing Chen and Laura Kang's respective uses of "Asia as method," this course occasions consideration of the questions and knowledge made available by attending to the figuration, mobilization, and circulation of "Asia" in a variety of arenas. Rather than seeking to authenticate Asia, we will ask after the historical, cultural, political economic, and aesthetic potency of the definitions it comes to bear as it circulates in largely (though not only) U.S.-based academic discourses. When, how, and why does it function as a racial category? When, how, and why, an index of racial capitalism and geopolitics? as a sign of anti-imperial and anti-racist politics? explicitly or tacitly as a technology and/or sign of gender, sexuality, and class? as a cultural trope or theme? Our engagement with such questions as these will unfold with persistent attention to the relationality of Asia and Asianness to other variably racialized and/or geographic signifiers like Middle Eastern, Black, North/South, Native American, and brown. This reading-intensive, discussion-driven course will include work by such scholars as Zoltan Biedermann, Srinivas Aravamudan, Chi-Ming Yang, Lisa Lowe, Aren Aizura, Takashi Fujitani, Lisa Yoneyama, Chris Patterson, Moon-Ho Jung, Iyko Day, Lisa Rofel, Rey Chow, and Gayatri Spivak, along with Chen and Kang and a range of primary texts. Students should read Kang's _Traffic in Asian Women_ in preparation for the first day of seminar. Students may enroll for 2, 3, or 4 credits; course assignments will vary accordingly. No auditors.

English 86800. Decolonial Ideologies.

Ashley Dawson and Ángeles Donoso Macaya. Wednesdays 11:45 a.m. - 1:45 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. IDS.

As the climate emergency rages, Indigenous people, frontline communities, and Earth defenders are increasingly in the crosshairs of extreme extractivism. The stakes could not be higher, and a survey of the efforts of land and water defenders is critical to understanding and overcoming the present planetary crisis. What political tactics are most effective in resisting extreme extractivism, and how do various forms of culture help to exacerbate or challenge contemporary ecocide? What, in other words, are the lineaments of decolonial ecologies today?

In this course, we will survey contemporary literary, filmic, and visual texts that explore extractive practices and inscribe decolonial ecologies. How do such forms of re-presentation figure the popular uprisings against dispossession and ecological devastation taking place in both the Global South and among frontline communities in the Global North? In addition, the course will explore how various texts account for those forms of resistance that do not just include overt political opposition to extraction but also alternative ways of being and thinking in relation to the environment.

The course centers the alternative ontologies of non-European peoples and social movements challenging empire, racism, sexism, and classism. Materials for the class will straddle genres such as contemporary visual art, performance art, film, and literature oriented around climate justice. Examples of some of the works we may consider include The Otolith Group’s experimental film Infinity Minus Infinity (2019); the photographs of Nigerian photographer George Osodi; Amitav Ghosh’s transnational Cli-Fi novel Gun Island (2019); the performance Tierra [Earth] (2013) by Guatemalan performance artist Regina José Galindo, the film Mencer: Ñi Pewma (2011) by Mapuche filmmaker Francisco Huichaqueo Pérez; works by Colombian artist Carolina Caycedo, such as Water Portraits (2015-2016) and Genealogy of a Struggle (2017); and Estratos [Strata](2019)by Chilean artist Alejandra Prieto.

English 89500. Introduction to Media Studies.

Marc Dolan. Thursdays 11:45 a.m. - 1:45 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person.

This course is an introduction to media studies for those who have mostly concentrated in English. We will spend less time on close readings of media artifacts and more time working in what media scholars call Industrial Studies.

Film studios, comic book companies, recording labels, broadcast and streaming services, even traditional book publishers will all be considered in both their “privileged” and “independent” forms. While global conglomerates will occupy some of our time, we will also pay special attention to how national, regional, and subcultural structures produce their own unique variations of pervasive media forms, considering perhaps not only how a British TV serial emerges from a different media system from a US one, but also how the system that generates Mexican TV serials is overtly unique from the one that produces Brazil’s. Moreover, we will devote several weeks, not just to audience studies, but to the idea that the final labor on the creative “assembly line” is performed by the audience, who for the last two centuries have frequently shaped the final cultural positioning of enduring media artifacts much more than their creators might want to admit.

Although the course’ first third will cover key concepts and theorists, much of the latter two-thirds will be guided by student interest. Students will make one brief presentation reviewing the literature in a specific subfield early on and then present a final project (i.e., talk, paper, syllabus, etc.) that best suits their specific academic needs. Active participation in all discussions is expected.

ENGL 85100. Reckoning with C19: Unexpected Places & Neglected Flashpoints.

Duncan Faherty. Tuesdays 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. Hybrid. (Cross-listed with ASCP 82000). 

Since its earliest configurations, American literary history has framed the middle of the nineteenth century as the zenith of U.S. cultural production. To put it another way, the 1850s have routinely been marked as the pivotal acceleration point for a progressive vision of American development. While this logic was pervasive for much of the field’s early history, twenty-first century Americanist scholarship has decentered the overarching primacy of the “American Renaissance” even as it has reinvigorated study of the long C19. Arguably, cotemporary formations of the nineteenth century, as represented in current scholarship and conferences, would almost be unrecognizable to twentieth century scholars. This course will explore the new terrains framed by these emergent counter formations. As such, the course seeks to consider the more complex canons, traditions, generic classifications, routes (instead of roots), geographies (of region, of nation, of globe), scales, and temporalities animating the emergent texts and contexts animating recent C19 Americanist scholarship. Part of this work will be thinking about how the contours of the nineteenth century (once imagined as the American literary century) have been reoriented by early Americanist and C20th/21st Americanist conceptions of a long C19 (pulling its origins earlier on the one hand, and extending it on the other). By considering how the digitization of archival materials has expanded our access to the print public sphere, we will survey the impact of several important digital humanities recovery projects. Working with such open-access projects as the Colored Conventions Project, the Early Caribbean Digital Archive, Chronicling America, and the Viral Texts Project, we will trace how an increased attention to seriality, to periodical cultural, and to archival work has reshaped the field’s objects of study. In part, this class will serve as a laboratory for students to experiment with positioning themselves (and their work) within these new schemas, as well as offering a chance to undertake some exploratory configurations of emergent or alternative C19s. The final selection of our objects of study will be shaped in consultation with participants, but possibilities include thinking past the primacy of the Civil War by grounding our sense of C19 as framed by (for example) the Haitian Revolution, Seneca Falls, the Mexican-American War, the passage of the Fugitive Slave Act, or the General Strike of 1877. Possible readings include the speculative fiction of Charlotte Perkins Gilman, Edgar Allan Poe, and Ignatius Donnelly; theorizations of new forms of belonging by Sojourner Truth, David Walker, María Amparo Ruiz de Burton, Frederick Douglass, John Rollin Ridge, Pauline Hopkins, and Sarah Winnemucca; and queer short fiction by Louise May Alcott, Charles Chesnutt, Herman Melville, Sui Sin Far, and Kate Chopin.

ENGL 76000.  Global Modernisms.

Nico Israel. Tuesdays 2:00 p.m. - 4:00 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person.

This seminar approaches twentieth and twenty-first century literature through the lens of the Nobel Prize for Literature. First awarded in 1901, the prize was one of five created by Alfred Nobel, the inventor of dynamite, a major supplier of armaments throughout the second half of the 19th century, and, somewhat later, a supporter of the arts, sciences, and peace movement. Nobel's would-be philanthropic support for global literature and his stated criteria for prize-worthiness raise a number of important questions for the study of modernism: Given the violent history of the twentieth century—colonialism and imperialism; nationalism, internationalism and transnationalism; genocide; liberation movements, Hot and Cold War (themselves undergirded by weaponry)—is literature to be perceived as an “ideal” antidote to violence, as Nobel seems to have imagined, or itself a reflection of and response to that violence? Ought the prize reward formal innovation and discovery, in the way that the Nobel Prize in physics rewards "outstanding contributions" to the physical sciences? To what extent is the very conception of world literature a residue of would-be universal humanism and how is it imbricated in ideas of universal human rights?  

The seminar explores some of the writing of Nobel winners across the twentieth century—Rudyard Kipling (UK), Rabindranath Tagore (India), Henri Bergson (France), Knut Hamsun (Norway), W.B. Yeats (Ireland), Thomas Mann (Germany), Pearl S. Buck (USA), Andre Gide (France), Jean Paul Sartre (France), Samuel Beckett (Ireland/France), Wole Soyinka (Nigeria), Toni Morrison (USA)—and some brief examples from the writing of its “losers” (those modernists who are most frequently viewed as emblematic and are still taught –Pound, Proust, Kafka, Joyce, Woolf, Breton, Césaire, etc.)  We will end the semester with a glance at the work of a few 21st century winners—J.M. Coetzee, Elfriede Jelinek, Orhan Pamuk, Bob Dylan, and/or Abdulrazak Gurnah.  Critical reading will include works by Immanuel Kant, Johann W. von Goethe, Georg Lukács, Walter Benjamin, Theodor Adorno, Hannah Arendt, Pierre Bourdieu, Michel Foucault, James English, John Guillory, Alain Badiou, Joseph Slaughter and others. 

ENGL 85410. Formations of U.S. Cultural Studies.

Eric Lott. Tuesdays 6:30 p.m. - 8:30 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person. (Cross-listed with ASCP 81500)

Formations of U.S. Cultural Studies This course leans hard on the “formations” of its title: it examines key cultural and social formations in the greater United States in tandem with notable constellations of cultural studies/American Studies scholarship, so serving as an advanced inquiry into both. As a modern practice, U.S. cultural study was the offspring of Progressive and then Popular Front political commitments, including such critics as Van Wyck Brooks, Constance Rourke, Ralph Ellison, and F.O. Matthiessen, whom we will look at alongside some of their chosen subjects, very often literary and cultural engagements with Jacksonian capitalist take-off (viz. the “flowering of New England,” “American humor,” “American Renaissance”). Cold War or myth-and-symbol American Studies offered both a counter to the hegemony of the New Criticism and an array of exceptionalist national fantasies generated out of 19th-century romance. New Left versions of U.S. cultural studies saw in American atrocities in Southeast Asia a legacy of regeneration through violence that extended back to Puritan New England while opening up archives of study that extended to popular, subaltern, and abjected domains. We will explore reigning paradigms in U.S. cultural studies by looking at the critics themselves (among many other writers, possibilities include Clyde Woods, Greil Marcus, Lauren Berlant, Amy Kaplan, David Harvey, José Muñoz, and Kandice Chuh); their intellectual genealogies or backstories (Karl Marx and W.E.B. Du Bois, Louis Althusser and Stuart Hall, Ernst Bloch and Sylvia Wynter); and the cultural tendencies and scenes they favored in producing their work (from Hawthorne to punk to sex work to carceral capitalism). Periodization as a mode of speculation will focus our conversations around such key nodal points as 1848, 1898, 1914, 1945, 1989, 2001, and beyond. No longer bound to reflexive allegiances to the nation-state, that 18th-century technology of compulsory homogeneity, U.S. cultural studies takes borders—of the nation, of community, of cultural production, of subjectivity (race, ethnicity, gender, sexuality, et al.)—as both preeminently porous and continually policed. To put it simply, we’ll examine cultural and artistic shapes and forms of many kinds (literature, performance, cinema, television, music, and more) in the context of scholarship from several disciplines across several decades that together produce horizons of U.S. cultural study—forms and formations, bases and superstructures.

ENGL 80600. 1987 or thereabouts: A Year in Black Feminist Criticism.

Amber Musser. Tuesdays 11:45 a.m. - 1:45 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person. (Cross listed with AFCP and WSCP).

Seminal texts published in 1987: Hortense Spillers’ “Mama’s Baby, Papa’s Maybe,” Barbara Christian’s Race for Theory, Toni Morrison’s Beloved, and Harriet Jacobs’s Incidents in the Life of the Slave Girl. Each of these texts has set off waves of engagement and criticism, which continue to ripple through black studies, sexuality studies, and black feminism. ENGL 80300. Sense and Sensuality: Queer of Color Critique.

Amber Musser. Wednesdays 11:45 a.m. - 1:45 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person. (Cross listed with AFCP and WSCP).

That sensation orders knowledge is one of the primary arenas of exploration within queer of color critique. This course will explore different sensational arenas, the different possible critiques that they produce, and what this means for thinking about sexuality, gender, and queer theory. Throughout the course of the semester, we will explore sensation in multiple ways: 1) as a diagnostic tool for understanding some of the different ways that race, gender, and sexuality intersect 2) as a way to trouble the dichotomy between interiority and exteriority to understand the ways in which orders of knowledge become imprinted on the body 3) as a mode of producing alternate forms of knowledge about gender, race, and sexuality. In addition to reading about different sensations and their relationships to politics and sexuality, this course will ask students to examine sexuality and sensation as collections of embodied and politicized experiences and to think about sensuality as a method in queer theory. Students will also be required to participate in an end of year symposium on queer theory. Readings may include: Aberrations in Black, Rod Ferguson; Atmospheres of Violence: Structuring Antagonism and the Trans/Queer Ungovernable, Eric Stanley; Sense of Brown, José Muñoz; Ricanness: Enduring Time in Anticolonial Performance, Sandra Ruiz;  The Other Side of Terror: Black Women and the Culture of US Empire, Erica Edwards; After the Party: A Manifesto for Queer of Color Life, Joshua Chambers-Letson; Black Performance on the Outskirts of the Left, Malik Gaines; Minor China: Method, Materials, and the Aesthetic, Hentyle Yapp; and A Dirty South Manifesto: Sexual Resistance and Imagination in the New South, LaMonda Horton-Stallings

ENGL 81500. Plague and theater in early modern England.

Tanya Pollard. Thursdays 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person. 

How did plague affect the imaginations of playwrights, actors, and audiences in early modern England? What is the relationship between catastrophe and creativity? How does contagion change art forms rooted in physical collaboration and live engagement, and how might the vulnerability of bodies in theaters affect audience responses and writerly strategies? How does disaster reshape genres such as tragedy and comedy? This course will explore these and other questions about the impact of plague on bodies, minds, domestic households, public spaces, economic stability, and foreigners. Reflections on the impact of epidemiological crises on creativity in other periods, including our own, will be encouraged. Readings will include Shakespeare, King Lear and Winter’s Tale; Shakespeare and Middleton, Timon of Athens; Jonson, Volpone and The Alchemist; Middleton, The Revenger’s Tragedy; and Dekker, The Wonderful Year; approaches will include history of medicine, science, bodies, affect, emotion, and performance.  

ENGL 78100. Mad Women.

Caroline Reitz. Fridays 11:45 a.m. - 1:45 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person. (Cross listed with WSCP).

Feminist anger is having a moment, but the double meaning of “mad” as angry and crazy has shaped the representation of women in popular crime fiction since Lady Audley burned down the house over 150 years ago. This course puts sleuths, villains, spies, and superheroes in conversation with the politics of the representation of female emotion. When is rage, as Brittney Cooper suggests, a “superpower” and when is it incapacitating? When is it justice and when is it revenge?

We will read both popular and academic feminist treatments of anger, such as works by Soraya Chemaly, Myesha Cherry, Brittany Cooper, Audre Lorde, Sianne Ngai, Lauren Berlant, and Rebecca Traister, as well as scholarship in Mad Studies, Trauma Theory, and Affect Theory, as part of an interdisciplinary exploration of crime fiction, comics, and television. This class will begin in the mid-19th century and go through 2021. We will also think about how we make transhistorical arguments about cultural figures and how that shapes projects like dissertations or syllabi. While most of our works will be drawn from the anglosphere, we will ground our explorations of the genre in an understanding that crime fiction is world literature.

Writing assignments for the class will be steered toward both scholarly practice (a conference paper for the Research Society for Victorian Periodicals or the Popular Culture Association, for example, or an article submission to a journal on crime fiction) and non-academic publication, such as for the online newsletter, CrimeReads. Any questions please reach out, creitz@jjay.cuny.edu.

ENGL 88100. Imagining Domestic Labor in the British Nineteenth Century.

Talia Schaffer. Wednesdays 11:45 a.m. - 1:45 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person. (Cross listed with WSCP).

The course surveys the emergence of domestic labor, service, and caregiving in the British 19th century, drawing on feminist economics, sociology, and ethics of care. Service work was the largest category of employment for women in the nineteenth century. What did it mean to have intimate household relations outsourced to paid strangers, and how did employers feel about these transactions? How might repeated acts of caregiving have shaped people’s subjectivity in ways that the realist novel does not adequately apprehend? How did women’s service work relate to the emergence of male professionalism? How did people regulate the personal relationality in the years before and during the emergence of institutional medical care? We will look at fiction that imagines the servant-master relation as a source of stress or a fantasized form of friendship: Cranford, Little Dorrit. We’ll read Villette, a novel that imagines the mentality of a global migrant caregiver, and Agnes Grey, a novel about the governess’s labor. We’ll read the diaries of servant Hannah Cullwick, and look at household manuals. Readings will include Arlie Russell Hochchild’s theory of emotional labor, work on global care chains, theories of maternalism, and we’ll also think about the histories of service work, from medieval and early modern through the eighteenth century. Seminar paper, presentation, keyword for 4 credits; all but seminar paper for 3 credits.

ENGL 84200. Writing of a Revolution: P.B. Shelley, Mary Wollstonecraft, William Godwin, Mary Shelley.

Alexander Schlutz. Mondays 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person.

In 1819, the year England arguably came closest to a revolution, P.B. Shelley wrote The Mask of Anarchy, an openly political poem that calls for non-violent resistance and civil disobedience in response to the infamous Peterloo massacre; The Cenci, a play in which extra-legal violence remains the only recourse against a brutally oppressive patriarchal theocracy, and where all attempts to escape the violent dynamic of oppressor and oppressed seem doomed to failure; and he completed the first three acts of Prometheus Unbound, a lyrical drama in which poetic language emerges as the only means to create a utopian opening in the “empty time” of history and its unending cycle of violent subjugation. Using these different poetic positionalities as entryways, this seminar will investigate P.B. Shelley’s poetry, prose, and drama as a series of attempts to envision the relationship between poetry and history in the wake of the failed French Revolution and the catastrophe of the Napoleonic wars. To guide our investigation, we will also consider the political prose and fiction of Shelley’s literary and philosophical heroes, William Godwin and Mary Wollstonecraft, written in response to the French Revolution in the 1790s, and his wife Mary Shelley’s revolutionary novel Frankenstein, first published in 1818. The course will include an opportunity to visit the Carl H. Pforzheimer Library of Shelley and his Circle at the New York Public Library, one of the foremost Shelley archives in the world, only a few stones’ throws away from the GC.

Course requirements:

4 short position papers, including a “conference abstract”; 15-minute conference presentation to be delivered at the in-class, end-of-semester course conference; final 15-20-page research paper.

(Given Shelley’s claim in A Defence of Poetry that poets are “the mirrors of the gigantic shadows which futurity casts upon the present,” final projects that consider the intertextual and intercultural connections between Shelley’s work and that of writers in Shelley’s past, present, or future will be welcome.)

Course readings:

Shelley, Percy Bysshe. The Major Works. Oxford World’s Classics. Ed. Zachary Leader and Michael O’Neill. Oxford UP, 2009.

Wollstonecraft, Mary. The Vindications. The Rights of Men. The Rights of Woman. Ed. D.L. Macdonald and Kathleen Scherf. Broadview, 1997.

Mary Wollstonecraft Mary and Maria. Mary Shelley Matilda. Ed. Janet Todd. Penguin, 1992.

Godwin, William. Caleb Williams. Ed. Gary Handwerk and A.A. Markley. Broadview, 2000.

Shelley, Mary. Frankenstein. The original 1818 Text. Ed. D.L. Macdonald and Kathleen Scherf. Broadview, 2012.

Additional primary and secondary readings will be available via the course e-reserve page.

ENGL 80700. Problems in Posthumanism.

Karl Steel. Wednesdays 2:00 p.m. - 4:00 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. In-person.

This seminar will aim to think the categories of the human and animal as cultural and historical problems. The human as an abstract, universal category has tended to carry with it claims to the exclusive possession of rationality, free will, and political autonomy. In practice, however, this category tends to be reserved for only a small subset of humans. We will therefore attend to how the differential recognition of humanity and animality interacts with other hierarchized categories of human differences: gender, dis/ability, race, religion, and mental health. Because I am a medievalist, many of our primary texts will be largely drawn from the Middle Ages, and will include works (in translation!) like The Book of Margery Kempe, The Life of Christina the Astonishing, ibn Tufayl’s Hayy ibn Yaqzan, and Marguerite Porete’s Mirror of Simple Souls. Primary text readings will be flexible, however, open to modification depending on the interests of course registrants: please reach out to me in advance. Critical theory will range across the relevant classics and recent good work in critical animal theory, ecocriticism, posthumanism, and biopolitics, especially from work that thinks ably and nimbly about racism and gender.

ENGL 89010. Teaching with the world on fire.

Amy Wan. Mondays 2:00 p.m. - 4:00 p.m. 2/3/4 Credits. Hybrid.

What does it mean to teach with the world on fire? We’ll use this course as an opportunity to think about teaching while the world burns around us, both literally and figuratively. This is a class about the state of higher education as a failing institution among many failing institutions, the realities of the job market and teaching gigs, the history of how teachers have marginalized students and their languages, and how classrooms are practiced as spaces of surveillance and discipline. This is also a class about hope–the hope that continues to be put into teaching, despite it all, and the possibilities for change.

Students will be asked to consider current best pedagogical practices, approaches, strategies, and theories in the context of their own specializations/fields and current issues in higher education. For this course, students will choose what they will compose over the course of the semester from a menu of practical and theoretical assignments around teaching (e.g. lesson plans, sample course policies, syllabi/reading lists, class presentations, annotated bibliographies, teaching philosophies, teaching websites, response papers, teaching journals, literacy narratives). Topics include but are not limited to: linguistic justice, abolitionist university studies, ungrading, classroom surveillance, anti-racist teaching practices, corporatization of educational technology, translingualism, adjunct labor, and accessible higher education. Among a variety of articles, books, and podcasts, we will also be reading the bell hooks classic Teaching to Transgress. Veteran teachers, those new to teaching and those anticipating teaching are all welcome.

Class will meet mostly in-person with 5 classes meetings on Zoom. Dates for online meetings TBD.

ENGL 80600. Entanglements of being, history, aesthetics.

ENGL 86800. The Global South as Politics and Aesthetics.

Peter Hitchcock. Wednesdays 6:30 PM - 8:30 PM. 2/3/4 Credits.
Of course, the Global South is not a geographic referent, and yet reading the South globally is a vital heuristic in postcolonial and decolonial critique. Rather than ask “south of what?” or “global where?” this course is designed to interrogate the critical grounds for thinking the Global South as a decolonial “worldliness.” Eschewing the lure of cartographic consciousness, we will instead explore the parameters of the Global South as forms of political and aesthetic transnationalism and postnationalism (in theoretical as well as literary expression). Globalization itself is often rendered as a scene of combined and uneven development, one in which vibrant struggles against colonialism and coloniality simultaneously confront the systemic demands of capitalism as a world system. While it is true certain versions of world literature as subject and archive cleave quite closely to a logic of commodification and circulation, we will examine how the Global South can be thought of as, whatever else it is, an injunction about the terms of a global literary (“global Anglophone,” “global novel,” “postcolonial exotic,” etc.). How might this shed light on the Global South’s primary identity as a political genealogy, one inflected by the Bandung Conference (1955), the Non-Aligned Movement, Pan-Africanism, Tri-continentalism, and Third World solidarity? Literature does not simply express the new political grammar of these events and processes, but reading the world is a way of writing it otherwise and this can be understood as very much a postcolonial problematic (on political identities of race, gender, sexuality, and class for instance, but also around abstractions of space and time, territory and temporality). The conceptual framework developed is avowedly interdisciplinary and comparativist and the course in general can be considered both an introduction to the global study of literature and a specific investigation of what we might call postcolonial prerogatives in that endeavor, a politics and aesthetics for which the term “Global South,” while easy to misconstrue, seethes with hermeneutical possibility. Texts will address how the Global South is theorized at the intersection of anti-colonial practices and literary imaginaries (Spivak, Prashad, Mbembe, Simone, Mishra). Rather than being an effect of postcolonial and decolonial autonomy, literature will be thought of as active in that process, with the Global South refusing the map in favor of mentality, a discrepant “worldmentality” to borrow from Diawara on Glissant (other examples may include Mohamed, Hamid, Apostol, Aw). ENGL 76000. Aestheticism, Decadence, Modernism, 1880-1930.

Richard Kaye. Thursdays 6:30 PM - 8:30 PM. 2/3/4 Credits.

This class explores the relation between the aestheticist and decadent movements as well as their determination of modernist aesthetics. We will begin with late-nineteenth-century British, American, and French works by Hardy, Wilde, James, and Huysmans. The fin de siècle was a time of pervasive fears and fantasies dominated by such figures as the New Woman, the urban detective, the homosexual bachelor, the Anarchist, the Oriental, the overreaching colonialist, the self-preening aesthete, the vampire, and the femme fatale, the latter reaching an apotheosis in Wilde’s Salome. In the diverse writings of Pater, Olive Schreiner, Vernon Lee, and Wilde, aestheticism emerged as a theoretically coherent and varied movement absorbed in exquisite surfaces, tantalizing ambiguities, and useless artifice. For decadent writers and artists, scientific theories of “degeneration” could be recalibrated as erotically charged, non-teleological experiments, while Freud drew on “decadent” scenarios for his proto-modernist narratives dealing with hysteria and sexual disorder, preeminently in Dora: Fragment of a Case of Hysteria, Women writers, meanwhile, struggled to find a place within the male-defined coteries of aestheticism and decadence, a theme dramatized in Henry James’ tale “The Author of Beltraffio,,” narrated by a decadent acolyte, in which the aestheticist project must be sequestered from female readers, who can only misconstrue its aims as immoral. Yet there were also alliances between male decadents and feminist writers (Wilde promoted Schreiner’s novel The Story of an African Farm because of its bold challenge to realist conventions and depiction of colonialist malaise.)  In France, the woman writer Rachilde publishes Monsieur Venus (1884), a symbolist/decadent novella concerning a dominatrix noblewoman, Raoule, who gradually transforms her working-class lover Jacques into her mistress by emptying him of all his “masculine” traits.

In the class’s second part we will explore how the fin outlasted the siècle, maintaining an intense afterlife in the Anglo-American modernist writing of Yeats, James, Eliot, Conrad, Joyce, Lawrence, and Djuna Barnes.  The morbidity, subjectivism, sexual experimentalism, and excesses of literary technique characteristic of 1890s sensibility foment modernist revisions that seek to repress their origins in decadent poetics. Wilde's Picture of Dorian Gray, with its hero who cannot "develop," inspires modernist counter-bildungsromanae. We consider Joyce’s Stephen Hero, an early version of Portrait of the Artist as Young Man, arguably a satire of aestheticism as well as a novel with an explicitly Paterian protagonist. The keenly observing, detached bachelor familiar from James also narrates Rilke’s lyrical The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge (arguably the first modernist novel) and that helps to form the paralyzed solitary consciousness of Eliot’s “Prufrock.” Arguably the narrator of Conrad’s Heart of Darkness adopts decadent techniques for his coiled narrative of colonial tragedy.  We will consider, too, Eliot’s absorption in the figure of the Jew as an emblem of a malevolent decadent cosmopolitanism versus Djuna Barnes’ depiction of the decadent Jew in her novel Nightwood as a more positively transformative cultural agent. We will consider how the Aestheticst and Decadent movements shaped the writing of the Harlem Renaissance. Our class concludes with James’ The Golden Bowl, a novel of twinned adulteries that is one of James’ most topical, aesthetically difficult, and decadent works of fiction. Among the works we will read: Hardy, Jude the Obscure, Huysmans, Against Nature; Wilde, The Picture of Dorian Gray, Salome; Schreiner, Story of an African Farm; Freud, Dora: Analysis of a Fragment of a Case of Hysteria; Conrad, Heart of Darkness, Joyce, Stephen Hero, Yeats, Selected Poems; Lawrence, Selected Short Fiction; Eliot, Selected Poetry; James, The Golden Bowl; Barnes, Nightwood; Showalter, Elaine, ed., Daughters of Decadence.  We will read relevant critical and theoretical texts, including Walter Pater, “Conclusion” to The Renaissance; Arthur Symons, “The Decadent Movement in Literature”; Mario Praz, from The Romantic Agony; George Bataille, from Literature and Evil; Richard Ellmann, “The Uses of Decadence”; Richard Gilman, from Decadence: The Strange Life of an Epithet; Linda Dowling, “The Decadent and The New Woman”; Michael Riffaterre, “Decadent Paradoxes,” Leo Bersani, from The Culture of Redemption; Regenia Gagnier, “Individualism, Decadence, and Globalization,” Vincent Sherrry, from Decadence and the Reinvention of Modernism”; Matthew Potolsky, from The Decadent Republic of Letters.  There will be a mid-term paper as well as a final paper.

ENGL 80700. Arthurian Traditions.

Steven Kruger. Wednesdays 4:15 PM - 6:15 PM. 2/3/4 Credits. 
Arthurian stories continue, in our own moment, to shape a sense of the historical past, and particularly the Middle Ages—as we see in a recent cultural production like the movie The Green Knight. In this course, we will take a long view of the Arthurian tradition, from its elaboration in the twelfth century by such writers as Geoffrey of Monmouth, Beroul, Chretien de Troyes, and Marie de France, through its later medieval development in romances by such writers as Geoffrey Chaucer, the anonymous author of Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, and Thomas Malory, to its postmedieval incarnation in a wide variety of works. We will read, alongside the primary texts, a number of theoretical and critical treatments that interrogate how traditions, like the Arthurian, operate in relation to politics.

ENGL 80200. How To Read. What To Do: Science and/or/as Fiction.

Joan Richardson. Thursdays 2 PM - 4 PM. 2/3/4 Credits.
The verb to read comes from the Old English raedan, meaning “to advise, interpret (something difficult), interpret (something written).” Keeping this definition in mind, the concern of this seminar will be, in general, to learn how to read, interpret, what is offered under the rubrics of the various “sciences”—to sift from the mass of information available—what we need to pay attention to, consider, so to imagine what to do, what we might do, to change the habits of mind that have imperiled our relationship to our world. As Walter Benjamin observed already a century ago in One Way Street, our connection to the cosmos has been lost. Course corrections are necessary to sustain living in what has been called our “critical zone”—the habitable space of earth’s atmosphere. More particularly, the seminar will investigate the emergence and nature of mind, of consciousness—not limited to human consciousness—foregrounding (following William James) that “consciousness” is not an entity, but a function: it matters what we pay attention to, and the activity of the imagination is primary. The 19th-century Irish physicist John Tyndall—whose investigations of carbon dioxide predicted the “greenhouse effect—delivered a lecture in 1870 “On the Scientific Use of the Imagination.” For us in the 21st century it is crucial to recognize that the imagination is seeded by what is observed, including, if we pay attention to them, the facts of nature: how we interpret these facts determines the uses to which they will be directed. We need to imagine in order to recognize the inter-relatedness of the facts. Power of action depends on an informed population. We need to extend our scope, cultivate ecological imagining to be able to address our current crisis responsibly.

As the individual “sciences” gradually precipitated out of natural philosophy in the 19th century, it was the inherited schema of the Enlightenment that directed how they would be conceived and considered—a Newtonian clockwork mechanism was the model, each thing in its place. But then came the Darwinian event and the discovery that we inhabit a universe of chance—mutation, randomness, ongoing process entered to offer different ways of considering and imagining. The political implications of recognizing the continuity of human consciousness with the life forms of other animals, plants and bacteria are immense. The most recent work into the nature of cells posits “that every cell in existence is a direct descendent of a single original—a split of a split of a split, through generations”: the basis of our connection to all forms of earthly life has begun to be evidenced. The exploration leading to this point has grown out of interdisciplinarity, an ecology of different practices that is unsettling categories put in place by “imperial science” to allow for the cross-pollination of ideas, of imaginings earlier confined within disciplinary boundaries. As Henry Louis Gates Jr. has recently observed, we need to understand the facts of genetics to overturn “race theory”: “…while race is socially constructed, genetic mutations—biological records of ancestry are not, and the distinction is a crucial one….[I]f we don’t disentangle these concepts, we may miss the great promise of using genetics to push back against a very long and sad history of the misuse of science for pernicious purposes.”   

Consideration of three differently inflected kinds of texts will open discussion during the first weeks of the term: Benjamin Labatut’s When We Cease to Understand the World (2020), a weaving of fact into fiction around the imaginings and historical impacts of a handful of 20th-century scientists and mathematicians, including Erwin Schrodinger (of “Schrodinger’s cat”) and Werner Heisenberg; Amitav Ghosh’s The Great Derangement: Climate Change and the Unthinkable (2016); and Donna Haraway’s “Symbiogenesis, Sympoiesis, and Art Science Activisms for Staying with the Trouble” (in  the collection Arts of Living on a Damaged Planet [2017]). Readings for the rest of the term will be drawn from (but not limited to) a rich list of texts/authors concerned with the question, “Where do we find ourselves?”. The list will include (in no order here): an account, ‘The Haudenosaunee/Mohawk Imagination and the Ecology of the Sacred”; work by William James, Rachel Carson, Erwin Schrodinger (from What is Life?), Gregory Bateson (from Steps Toward an Ecology of Mind), Lynn Margulis, David Bohm (from Wholeness and the Implicate Order), Antonio Damasio, Emanuele Coccia, Isabelle Stengers, Stephon Alexander, Richard Feynman, Sylvia Wynter, Bruce Clarke (from Gaian Systems), Anna Tsing, Frans de Waal, Peter Godfrey-Smith (from Metazoa: Animal Life and the Birth of the Mind), and….

Requirements: participation in discussions and a term paper/project.
