MALS 70000: Seminar in Interdisciplinary Studies
Monday, 4:15-6:15 PM, Prof. Annette Saddik, In-Person, Room 7395, 3 credits

Excess, Ambiguity, and Laughter in 20th-and 21st-Century Culture

Laughter can serve as a powerful social commentary, particularly the kind of ambivalent laughter associated with grotesque, tragicomic, or black humor--what Frances K. Barasch has called "ludicrous-horror"--which breaks through imposed limitations, destabilizes fixed boundaries, and juxtaposes contradictions in order to challenge what is considered stable or acceptable.

In this course, we will be studying works that embrace a politics of excess and laughter that celebrates the irrational and the undefinable, often employing exaggeration, distortion of superficial reality, and a sense of irony. These works highlight the ambiguities and inconsistencies of living in the world--the excesses that leak out of closed systems of meaning, that seep through the cracks of the rational, the stable, the complete, and point toward the essence of the real.

Objects of study will include literature, theatre, painting, music, theoretical texts, and performance cultures such as circus aesthetics, "freak shows," burlesque, and cabaret.

 

MALS 70000: Seminar in Interdisciplinary Studies
Thursday, 4:15-6:15 PM, Prof. Miryam Segal, In-Person, Room 5417, 3 credits

Meaning and Interpretation

This course takes as its focii meaning and interpretation—and the relationship between the two. How do we humans find or create meaning, and how do we understand that process? To think through some scholarly and artistic answers, we will read theories of linguistic meaning, religious meaning, literary interpretation, legal interpretation, as well as literary works, religious scripture and law. In reading these as ways of getting at more productive ideas about meaning, we will also be interested in similarities, analogies, and the way that these theories—and assumptions—are cross-disciplinary.

Works studied include Herman Melville’s Billy Budd, Honoré de Balzac’s “Sarrasine,” films by Brian de Palma, biblical narrative and modern “mythologies”; scholarship in semiotics, narrative theory and deconstruction, such as the works of Roland Barthes, Jonathan Culler, Wolfgang Iser, Barbara Johnson, Eyal Peretz, Tzvetan Todorov, as well as theory of law, and religion and sociology by Peter Berger and Robert Cover and others.

In this introduction to Liberal Studies, we will spend time and effort on writing as a craft and tool of expression and on thinking together about the processes of editing and of writing-as-thinking that are critical for work in the humanities at the graduate level.

 

MALS 70100: Narratives of New York: Literature and the Visual Arts
Tuesday, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. Christopher Schmidt, In-person, Room 3207, 3 credits
Cross listed with ASCP 81500

The Lower East Side. Greenwich Village. Harlem. These are not just storied neighborhoods but contact zones that have witnessed waves of migration and demographic change, reform movements and political resistance. In this course, we will consider the rich histories of these NYC sites through readings in fiction and poetry, visual arts and film, history and cultural studies. Throughout we will pay special attention to the avant-guardists, activists, feminists, and queers who have been drawn to these neighborhoods, and the communities formed around them.

Topics may include:

Photography and its role in exploiting “the other half” of the Lower East Side
Greenwich Village and New York Dada
Modernist alienation and O’Neill’s The Hairy Ape
Nella Larsen and the Harlem Renaissance
The Beats, the New York School, and the LES mimeograph revolution
Harlem and the Black Arts movement
The Young Lords and the Garbage Offensive
The Five Demands, June Jordan, and the poet-teachers of CCNY’s SEEK program
Downtown deindustrialization and site-specific art
Cruising, anarchitecture, and the West Side Piers
Gran Fury, ACT UP, and AIDS activism

Course requirements will include regular discussion posts, one review, one presentation, and a final term essay or multimodal capstone. A visit to the archives at the NYPL, Interference Archives, or the LGBT Center National Archives is likely.

 

MALS 70500: Introduction to Global Ancient and Medieval Studies
Monday, 4:15-6:15 PM, Prof. Sarah Madole Lewis, In-person, Room 3207, 3 credits
Cross-listed with MALS 74500, CLAS 74300, MSCP 70100, & MES 78500

Archaeology—artifacts, ecofacts, and the built environment —can offer different perspectives on the past and provide insights into individuals often left out of the historical record. This course first introduces students to two major methods of archaeological inquiry—excavation and survey. The course then examines important sites from the ancient and Late Antique Mediterranean region. Different types of archaeological evidence are examined with reference to specific sites (including the Acropolis in Athens, Pompeii, and Dura-Europos) to understand and engage with some of the ongoing debates in archaeology, art history, and history, as well as the history of reception, and the cultural and disciplinary biases that shape our interpretation of this material. Cultural Heritage, museum practices, and the preservation of archaeological sites will also be considered. Guest lecturers will speak on different aspects of archaeological inquiry and the rule of museums in preserving the past in the twenty-first century.

 

MALS 71200: The Culture of Fashion: Theories and Practices,
Monday, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. Elizabeth Wissinger, In-Person, Room 3212, 3 credits
Cross-listed with WSCP 81000 & ASCP 81500

The Culture of Fashion and Specters of Style

This graduate seminar explores fashion as a haunted system, using Jacques Derrida’s notion of hauntology to grasp how ghosts of colonialism, race, gender, labor, waste, and technology persist within contemporary systems of style. Fashion will be approached as a key site where capitalism turns spectral, obscuring its own conditions of production behind the glamour of “Fashion” with a capital F, even as bodies, environments, and infrastructures bear its marks. We will examine how past violences and futures-that-never-were materialize in fibers, garments, and images, from plantation cotton and sweatshop labor to the oil-based plastics threading through the synthetic clothing central American fashion’s development. Sifting through the bodily dynamics of queer and unruly bodies haunting fashion’s strict dichotomies and ableist structures, we will also look at how fashion literally cuts and shapes bodies along the lines of desire, especially within the entanglements of wearables, ingestibles, and other fashion and digital technologies that function to capture bodily vitalities for profitable ends.

Readings will bring together cultural and media theory, gender and sexuality studies, theories of materiality, science and technology studies, and the sociology of embodiment, enabling students to trace how clothing is haunted by hidden labor, toxic waste, colonial extraction, and normative ideals of race, gender, and ability. Throughout, students will use hauntology as both concept and method, to analyze fashion as a system of beliefs, practices, and values that shape, and are shaped by the larger culture, culminating in research project or creative-critical blend of research and student-created object (a film, garment, piece of art, etc.). Project topics can include, but are not limited to, examining fashion through art, film, popular, and other forms of visual culture, material culture, technological cultures that conjure bodies, and the stories of students’ own wardrobes, to theorize fashion’s ghosts and unpack how challenges to linear time and fixed identities persist, haunt, and structure the very act of getting dressed.

 

MALS 72000: Thesis Writing Course
Wednesday, 4:15-6:15 PM, Prof. Libby Garland, Online synchronous, 3 credits

If you would like to sign up for the thesis writing course, permission of the department is required. To express your interest in taking this course, please fill in this form.

This three-credit research and writing course is online, synchronous, and open to all MALS students who are working on their theses. It is designed to help students navigate the thesis process: research, drafting, writing, and revising. Together, we will discuss strategies for carving out time to write, honing research questions, finding great sources, crafting arguments and throughlines, organizing evidence, telling compelling stories, and polishing drafts into strong finished prose. The class also offers a supportive writing community. Students will have the chance to get feedback from the professor as well as to exchange and give feedback on each other’s work in progress.

 

MALS 72100: Feminist Texts and Contexts
Monday, 2:00-4:00 PM, Prof. Shereen Inayatulla, Hybrid Synchronous, Room 6421, 3 credits
Cross listed with WGS 71001 & WSCP 81001

This course provides an overview of critical keywords, concepts, and discussions rooted in feminist theory. The assigned texts take up an array of interdisciplinary questions, research practices, and approaches to understanding gender. Throughout the semester, we will examine trajectories of feminist thought and consider how historical movements shape contemporary sociopolitical conditions on a local and global scale. The reading and writing assignments in this course ask students to explore effective methods for applying theory to practice. Our coursework amplifies intersectional perspectives of the global majority—BIPOC and working class/working poor communities. We will discuss how a feminist praxis can advance the struggle against interlocking systems of oppression as we experiment in producing our own written reflections and artifacts for a shared analysis within the course.

MALS 73200: American Social Institutions
Wednesday, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. Justin Rogers-Cooper, In-person, Room 3207, 3 credits
Cross listed with ASCP 81500

Social institutions could be political, cultural, or economic: a state agency, the blues, a social movement. Are racial and gendered communities not also social institutions? In this course, we must prioritize contradictions captured by “American” social institutions. We will thus open discussions into citizen, race, gender, nation, state, and corporation through histories, fiction, and music. To frame those conversations, we need key concepts from American studies:

to consider key historical junctures when the institutions burst or got born, including rebellions, uprisings, coups, insurgencies, riots, and protests (not to mention depressions and elections);
to attend to the legal, cultural, and political “technology” of how racialized and gendered citizens create themselves and are denied creation, produced through and recognized by power, and not;
to render our thinking within scholarly critiques of U.S. exceptionalism, seeking to expose the mechanics of nation-states themselves, to figure out global patterns of recognition and repression and potential.

We will read scholarship and fiction, watch documentaries and movies, and listen to music to spur our thoughts. Typical intellectuals, writers, and artists include James Baldwin, Audre Lorde, W.E.B. Du Bois, Jodi Melamed, Grace Lee Boggs, Keeanga-Yamhtta Taylor, Ruth Wilson Gilmore, Frederick Douglass, Lisa Lowe, LaRose Parris, Natalie Etoke, Fred Moten, Olúfẹ́mi O. Táíwò…Nina Simone, Exterminate All the Brutes, Billie Holiday…

Given the range of students who typically take this course, your reflections and expertise will be necessary for analyzing how American social institutions work – from above and below, from desire and from fear, how they hope and collapse, how they war, how they sound. How ordinary people make them and unmake them, how they’re ruled. These discussions should carry insights into all MALS tracks and projects, allowing you freedom to integrate, assemble, synthesize, and collect.

Unlike previous iterations I’ve taught of this course, this time we will take a few weeks to examine a sustained crisis in social institutions to apply and explore the subjects, concepts, and scholarship introduced in the course. In this case, we will carefully and respectfully investigate the spate of terror and heartbreak known as the “Atlanta child murders” from 1979-1981. Looking at history, fiction, and video, we will wrap our minds around this often forgotten and deeply troubling moment of anti-Black violence to better understand the contradictions, failures, and powers that emerge when confronting what must be faced. To pursue this unsettling but important subject in U.S. history, we will learn about southern political economy and read Baldwin’s Evidence of Things Not Seen, Tayari Jones’s Leaving Atlanta, and at least parts of Toni Cade Bambara’s These Bones Are Not My Child. We will also watch one or two documentaries along with relevant scholarship.

 

MALS 73400: Africana Studies Introduction
Thursday, 4:15-6:00 PM, Prof. Nathalie Etoke, Hybrid Asynchronous, Room 3207, 3 credits
AFCP 70100

This course provides an overview of Africana Studies as an intellectual discipline while also exploring its grassroots and political foundations. A field born out of Black people’s freedom struggle in the 1960s, its different names – Black Studies, African American Studies, Africana Studies – reveal the field’s continuous evolution. It went from being anchored in Black consciousness to becoming institutionalized. We will adopt a chronological approach that highlights the epistemic shifts at work in the field. We will also address the ongoing challenges that Africana Studies must confront: How is it connected to or disconnected from the everyday life of working-class Black people? How can the discipline resist the commodification process inherent to its institutionalization? Historically, Black people’s liberation has been the central tenet of the discipline. How do the politics of representation and the recognition of the diversity of Black identities complicate the liberation question? Ultimately, in studying the field’s intellectual history, students will also investigate how the encounter with the West, the institutionalization of racism and economic oppression/exploitation shape the human predicament that diasporic and continental Africans still face today.

Learning outcomes:

Historicize, contextualize, and analyze the grassroots origins of Africana Studies, a discipline that student protests, the civil rights movement, and the Black freedom struggle brought to academia.
Address topical areas that highlight Africana modes of self-writing (especially those exploring race, class, gender, and questions of freedom and humanness for people of African descent).
Improve research and writing skills through critical essays on assigned subjects.

Participate in class discussions, listen, and respond to others’ ideas.

 

MALS 73600: Introduction to Mass Violence in the Modern Era
Monday, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. Mark Lewis, In-person, Room 5417, 3 credits
Cross listed with HIST 70900

Designed for non-lawyers, this course investigates the profound historical, political, and ethical questions surrounding war crimes, crimes against humanity (such as systematic persecution of persons based on their religion, ethnicity, nationality, or race), and genocide (the intentional destruction of a racial, religious, national, or ethnic group, in whole or in part). The course content includes learning about some major cases of crimes against humanity and genocide in Africa, Asia, Europe, and the Middle East in the 20th and 21st centuries, analyzing forms of transitional justice and the lack of such efforts, and probing the impact of these proceedings on culture, politics, and history. Students will also learn about institutions involved in these matters, including the United Nations, the International Court of Justice, and the European Court of Human Rights. Students completing the course should be able to make policy recommendations concerning transitional justice and the prevention of mass atrocities. They should be able to identify some of the legal tests and rules used to determine whether certain actions in warfare are legal or not, such as the detention of civilians and aerial bombardment, as well as what types of actions in peace-time are permitted by state and non-state actors. They will develop the ability to sift relevant primary and secondary sources related to specific historical, political, and sociological problems, assess hypotheses and evidence skeptically, and formulate their own reasoned interpretations.

 

MALS 73900: Introduction to Translation Studies
Tuesday, 4:15-6:15 PM, Prof. Alexander Elinson, In-Person, Room 3305, 3 credits
Cross listed with CL 89400

Literature is unimaginable without translation, yet translation is often derided as a process of simplification, flattening, domestication, loss, and even treachery. Anglophone literary cultures, in particular, have often been resistant to, even suspicious of, translation, or have wielded it as a tool of colonialism and control.

This course is dedicated to the methods, theories, practice, and art of literary translation. We will read texts on translation and translation theory, and language more generally, to explore different approaches to and implications of translation.

MALS 74600: Introduction to Global Early Modern Studies
Tuesday, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. Mario DiGangi, In-Person, Room 3309, 3 credits
Cross listed with GEMS 72100, CL 80100, ENGL 82100 & FREN 72000

This course examines the histories and discourses of early modern sexuality through colonial, mercantile, interreligious, and imaginative encounters. We will explore sexual cultures, ideologies, and communities across Europe (England, Spain, Florence, Rome, Germany), the Ottoman Empire, Africa, and the Far East, as well as the role of sexuality in the Atlantic slave trade. We will address indigenous understandings of sexuality and colonial persecution of sexual crimes in Nahua societies, colonial New Spain, Brazil, and Mexico. The course will also focus on some exceptional historical figures in Europe and the Americas: Pocahontas, Malintzin, and Catalina de Erauso, the latter of whom is the subject of Juan Ruiz de Alarcón’s play, La Famosa Comedia de la Monja Alférez, which we will read in a new English translation. Attention will be given to archival records, visual culture, poetry, travel narrative, and drama.

 

MALS 74800: Introduction to Public Scholarship: Theories, Methods, and Approaches
Wednesday, 4:15-6:15, Prof. Elizabeth Macaulay, In-Person, Room 3207, 3 credits
Cross listed with HIST 79500 & PUBL 70100
 

Public Scholarship has had a long history. As Susan Smulyan (2022) has noted in Doing Public Humanities, engaged research has been a vital component of certain fields in the humanities and humanistic social sciences—from women and gender studies to archaeology. This introductory course explores critical debates around, theories about and approaches to public scholarship. We will examine public-facing projects, community-engaged projects, and public initiatives (including several at the Graduate Center), and by doing so, students will examine the audience, purpose, ethics, and challenges of developing scholarly research with a popular audience. This course will examine the interdisciplinary, collaborative, and adaptive nature of public scholarship. Concerns over funding, bias, and access will be central to our discussions. During the semester, members of the Graduate Center Faculty and other practitioners will engage with the course and their work. Case studies will be essential to our discussions, and students will research and present on existing projects. As a final project, students will develop an original public project.

 

MALS 77200: Film Histories & Histography
Thursday, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. Leah Anderst, In-Person, Room 6495, 3 credits
Cross-listed with FSCP 82000

Film History & Historiography surveys the cinematic medium from its inception to the present day with a focus on major historical, cultural, technological, and industrial developments. For our course, this will include: the growth of international silent cinema, Hollywood and the industrialization of film, the development of other sites of film production, nonfiction and nontheatrical traditions, European New Waves, Third Cinema, and independent film movements. The course will also cover different strategies and theories of historiography that reflect the research interests of the students in the class. The semester will include instruction on research methods taught in conjunction with the Mina Rees Library staff.

 

MALS 78100: Issues in Urban Education
Tuesday, 4:15-6:15 PM, Prof. Eduardo Viana, In-Person, Room 3310A, 3 credits

This course provides a foundation for research and practice in urban education by surveying the major issues, controversies, and reform movements shaping public schools in the United States. Adopting an interdisciplinary approach rooted in history, sociology, urban politics, and psychology, we will critically examine contemporary challenges through the lenses of advocacy, activism, and emancipatory possibility. Key topics include the historical development of urban spaces and their schools, the lived experiences of educators and students in historically marginalized communities, and a critical analysis of school reform efforts.

Through a combination of field experiences and scholarly reflection, we will examine the dynamic interplay between education policy and the lived realities of public schools, exploring how policymakers, educators, families, and students exercise collective, transformative agency to negotiate systemic inequities and work toward more just and inclusive schools. In doing so, we will explore activism as a form of collaborative praxis through which individuals and communities together reshape educational contexts—transforming themselves while transforming their worlds. Central to the course is a question that will guide your journey: What kind of educator or advocate are you committed to becoming, and what kind of world must we create for our children?

Specifically, this course is designed for students to:

Analyze the historical, political, and social roots of urban education using interdisciplinary perspectives.
Evaluate the impact of race, ethnicity, gender, and socioeconomic status on the structure and reform of urban education systems.
Investigate the experiences of educators, students, and communities within urban schools.
Critically assess dominant reform efforts and explore emancipatory alternatives to traditional urban schooling.
Develop focused research questions and apply appropriate theoretical and methodological tools to conduct independent inquiry in urban education.

 

MALS 78400. Introduction to Latin American Studies -- CANCELED
Monday, 4:15-6:15 PM, Prof. Víctor Sierra Matute, In-Person, 3 credits

Coloniality and the Politics of Time

This seminar approaches Latin America—a name that coexists with Abya Yala, a term used in several Indigenous traditions and contemporary activism—as an epistemic formation forged through processes of conquest and colonial governance, translation and mediation, survival and reinvention. Rather than treating the region as a stable cultural entity, we examine how its histories have been narrated—and contested—from Indigenous intellectual traditions prior to European conquest to contemporary debates on decolonization. How did colonial regimes reorganize temporal experience through extraction and racialization? How have modern and contemporary thinkers challenged these inherited chronologies?

Primary readings include the Popol Vuh; selections from Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala’s Primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno and Úrsula de Jesús’s spiritual diary; independence-era political writings; and nineteenth-century reflections on nationhood. We then move to twentieth- and twenty-first-century interventions by writers such as Alejo Carpentier, José María Arguedas, Nicolás Guillén, Lydia Cabrera, Cecilia Vicuña, Mayra Santos-Febres, Quince Duncan, Rita Indiana, Yuri Herrera, Edgar García, Alan Pelaez Lopez, and Gabriela Wiener—authors whose works unsettle colonial and neo/late-colonial memory. These texts are framed through theoretical perspectives from Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui, Antonio Quijano, Nadia Altschul, Daniel Nemser, María Lugones, Rocío Quispe-Agnoli and Marisol de la Cadena, among others.

We will anchor our discussions in major debates within Latin American studies: coloniality and decolonial thought; race and racial capitalism; sovereignty and nation formation; nature and extractivism; archives and counter-archives; migration and diaspora; gender and subalternity; and the sensory and material dimensions of power. Particular attention is given to the persistence of coloniality as a structuring condition that continues to shape cultural production beyond the formal end of empire.

By foregrounding temporality as a political terrain, the course positions Latin American studies as a site of intellectual practice and theoretical innovation. Students write weekly responses, lead seminar discussions, and produce a substantial research paper engaging primary texts and historiographical debates. Sources are available in translation and, where possible, in original languages. Discussions are conducted in English.

 

MALS 78500: Economics for Everyone
Wednesdays, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. Miles Corak, Online Synchronous
Cross listed with QMSS 75000

This may, or it may not be, your first economics course, but it can reasonably be your last. “Economics for Everyone” is specially designed to meet the needs of students in all disciplines who may have had only limited exposure to economics. You will learn the fundamental vocabulary and grammar of a subject central to many public policy debates—the big issues ranging from globalization to climate change, from inequality to unemployment—but also the smaller concerns central to everyday life, like why does my cappuccino cost so much? Upon completion you will have the skills and knowledge to be a more informed and engaged citizen. Our study of the subject moves through three themes. The first examines the method and scope of economics, introducing some fundamental principles, and by appealing to some important historical examples illustrates how the definition and methods of the subject have evolved. MALS: 70000: Seminar in Interdisciplinary Studies

Monday, 4:15 PM - 6:15 PM, In-person, Prof. Anna Akasoy

This course serves as an introduction to key concepts and concerns of select disciplines of the humanities and social sciences with a particular focus on the ways academics think about the truth embedded in their research and the ways they seek to persuade others of their truth claims.

Academic research typically reflects the ambition to identify a truth of some kind. Depending on the academic discipline, views regarding the nature of that truth, our ability to identify and describe it, as well as conventions for such descriptions can vary substantially. Sometimes, creative exercises can complement academic research, although the resulting constellations can be contentious. Historical fiction, for example, shares with the academic study of history the ambition of describing past realities, but authors apply different standards in their presentation of these realities. Likewise, views differ as to whether academic research aims at establishing a single truth to the exclusion of other descriptions or the extent to which different truth claims can coexist, even where they might be mutually exclusive.

These different attitudes to truth in academic research are reflected in different strategies of persuasion in academic publications. Academics employ a range of strategies in order persuade their readers, including documentation (e.g., citations, bibliographical references, examples), organization of information and rhetorical styles. Persuasion does not happen in a social vacuum. Just how much power differentials can impact interpersonal relations, including the disposition of some individuals to agree with the assertions and desires of others has recently received much critical attention from social movements.

In this course, we will discuss a broad selection of accessible materials, including historical philosophical views how the truth should be demonstrated, explorations of interdisciplinarity and the ability of disciplines to cooperate despite their different conventions for truth claims, the significance of power in persuasion and the recognition of truth, the use of persuasion in conflict resolution, and strategies of critical reading of academic research, including criticisms of manipulation and how to decide where we trust authors and where and when we can realistically probe their arguments. A small selection of academic articles on topics of broad interest will be used for a close analysis of sample strategies of persuasion.

Students will explore academic pursuits in a diverse selection of disciplines as well as the nature of the knowledge produced in these disciplines and the status of this knowledge in specific disciplines, interdisciplinary contexts and the public sphere. Students will have the opportunity to analyze the truth claims in sources of their disciplines of interest as well as the truth claims they will be making themselves in their own academic work. Throughout the semester, they will reflect on the conventions and expectations of academic research and demonstration in their own fields in contrast and comparison with other academic traditions.

MALS 78500: From UFOs to UAP: Disclosure and National Security

Wednesday, 4:15 PM - 6:15 PM, Fully In-person, Prof. Eli Karetny

We have entered a new era in how the American government treats UFOs, which are now referred to officially as Unidentified Anomalous Phenomenon (UAP). The 2021 Pentagon UAP Report not only acknowledged the reality of UFOs/UAP and called for their scientific study, but it also presented them as a threat to national security. This course will explore how the politics of disclosing the existence of UFOs entailed overcoming the longstanding “UFO taboo,” the reasons for changing the language from UFOs to UAP, the social implications of discovering that behind UAP there may be a non-human intelligence (NHI), and why the government “securitized” the concept by treating a scientific mystery as a national security threat. But do UAP really pose a threat, and if so, how should the government respond to that danger? Science fiction has often assumed the threat would be an external one of conquest or annihilation, which some theorists believe could lead to increased international cooperation by uniting humanity against a common enemy. We will consider that idea, along with alternative scenarios, including the worrisome possibility that the threat could lead to social collapse, as the State is unable to provide security against a technologically superior threat. We will explore how UAP expose the anthropocentric assumptions of modern state sovereignty, force societies to reimagine the concept of “security,” and challenge humanity to cooperate in new ways to deal with the political and scientific questions raised by the existence of UAP.

MALS 74700: Special Topics in Global Early Modern Studies 3 credits
Thursday, 2:00 PM - 4:00 PM, Prof. Monica Calabritto, In-person, Room 6494
Cross-listed with GEMS 83100, C L 80900

Love in Early Modern Culture and History

This seminar will explore love in its dual nature: as a physical, erotic passion and as a spiritual, noble emotion. We will also examine how love can lead to intense passions—jealousy, possessiveness, cruelty, hatred, and physical harm, among others—in literary and historical accounts from the Middle Ages to the seventeenth century. From love as a sickness to love as a blessing and a gateway to the divine, and from love as a civilizing force to love as a dehumanizing state of mind, this seminar will encourage a dialogue between different perspectives on the most obvious yet elusive emotion.

We will start with Plato’s dialogue, the Symposium, and Avicenna’s Treatise on Love. Avicenna, the Arab polymath who authored the Canon of Medicine, produced one of the most influential texts in both Islamic and European medical traditions until at least the seventeenth century. These two works highlight the ongoing tension between the body and the soul, a theme explored further in many other texts we will examine. In both, physiological and medical aspects are intertwined with philosophical ideas. This interaction is further developed and emphasized in Marsilio Ficino’s fifteenth-century commentary on Plato’s Symposium, which was widely read by philosophers such as Judah Leon Abravanel and Tullia D’Aragona, physicians like Jacque Ferrand and Girolamo Mercuriale, artists such as Michelangelo, and writers including Louise Labé, William Shakespeare, and Madame de la Fayette.

A selection of medical documents from the early sixteenth to early seventeenth centuries will further illustrate the link between philosophy, medicine, and literature in relation to the concept of early modern love. Additionally, we will examine early modern criminal cases and chronicles where love led to violent passions—jealousy, possessiveness, cruelty, hatred, and physical harm, among others. As Friar Lawrence warns Romeo in Shakespeare’s Romeo and Juliet, “These violent delights have violent ends.” (2,6) Finally, we will also explore the numerous visual representations of love depicted in various works of art that accompanied the celebration of marriages, love affairs, and the birth of children.

Several texts from Italian, French, and English sources composed from the early fourteenth to the late seventeenth century will assist in exploring several questions: How do these texts depict the tension between body and soul, as well as erotic passion and spiritual emotion? How did the fifteenth-century Neo-Platonic reinterpretation of Plato’s Symposium and Petrarch’s sonnets influence love literature of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries? In what ways do genre and gender shape the portrayal of love within these works? What can we learn about the hostile and violent emotions associated with love as presented in these literary texts?

Additionally, how can we explain the darker aspects of love—such as gender stereotypes, love as sacrifice and devotion, and the justification of abuse and murder in both fictional and historical accounts?

With its distinct claims of authenticity and relevance, literary fiction can be used to think through unresolved ethical problems (e.g., the ethical rights of AI) or spark controversies of their own, as obvious in debates about texts such as Goethe's Sorrows of Young Werther or Kristen Roupenian’s Cat Person. Each meeting is organized around an ethical issue. Literary texts are suggested, but students are welcome to bring alternative and additional texts to the class. Topics include, but are not limited to: the ability of literature to use introspection for psychological and emotional experiences of ethical challenges, the use of multiple perspectives to render interpersonal conflict, the use of literature in promoting ethical norms, the potential of historical fiction in giving prominence to experiences underrepresented in historical sources, the nature of literary characters and our ethical obligations towards them, representation and cultural authenticity, translation, authorship and ownership, and memoirs, autofiction and posthumous rights of individuals represented in them. Examples of texts for discussion include among others Hilary Mantel’s historical fiction and Saidiya Hartman’s critical fabulation, W.G. Sebald’s On the Natural History of Destruction (about German literary responses to World War II), Stefan Zweig’s Beware of Pity and Elena Ferrante’s Lost Daughter (both examples of stories about regret), Else Lasker-Schüler’s ‘Oriental’ persona (for issues of representation), the global reception of Dostoevsky’s Crime and Punishment, and the legacy of Nabokov’s Lolita. To supplement our analyses of literary texts, we will consider a small selection of accessible philosophical ethics such as the virtues in Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics.
Students with an interest in Middle Eastern studies will also be offered options for considering texts from the region for each meeting, for example Naguib Mahfouz’ Thief and the Dogs, The Case of the Animals versus Man before the King of the Jinn from the epistles of the Brethren of Purity, Ahmed Saadawi's Frankenstein in Baghdad, or the legacy of Orientalism in the translation of Arabic texts

MALS 70000: Seminar in Interdisciplinary Studies
Thursday, 4:15 pm – 6:15 pm, 3 credits, Prof. Anna Akasoy
In-Person, Room 3309
MALS Majors Only

"The Pursuit of Happiness"
Happiness is often understood to be an ultimate and universal human aim. Whatever purpose we identify for our actions and ambitions, the purpose of pursuing happiness seems to be self-explanatory. It appears that there is no need to explain that or why we want to be happy. Across social, cultural and intellectual contexts, however, the pursuit of happiness has been conceptualized in different ways. Key aspects such as happiness as ethical or spiritual improvement, happiness as a particular state of mental health or the relationship between happiness and economic prosperity have long been and are still widely discussed. Acknowledged in the Declaration of Independence, the pursuit of happiness is also subject to commodification and cultural commentary. The collective happiness of societies has been gaining attention as a way to measure their general wellbeing and flourishing. Recent developments such as the rise of social media or the impact of the Covid pandemic have raised broad concerns about the happiness and wellbeing of large segments of society, in particular younger people.

In this course, we will explore concepts of happiness from a variety of historical, cultural and intellectual contexts, using a variety of methods mostly from the humanities such as history, intellectual history, cultural history, philosophy, and literary studies. Aspects of the social sciences will be taken into account as well. We will compare conceptual approaches to happiness to a variety of forms of cultural expression such as novels (e.g., Sally Rooney's Normal People for the impact of historical events and socio-economic inequality on the happiness of a particular generation; Angie Kim's Happiness Falls for happiness and neurodiversity) or film and television (e.g., Olive Kitteridge for happiness and interpersonal ethics or for the relationship between happiness and one's environment). Students are welcome to suggest additional examples. We will also discuss recent discussions about a broader mental health crisis and its impact on a variety of social and cultural activity.

Furthermore, we will analyze recent interdisciplinary studies of happiness in order to survey a variety of academic methods and discuss what the interdisciplinary nature of such scholarship might be. The class will offer the opportunity to explore recent scholarship produced at the Graduate Center which is related to the subject of happiness and collective wellbeing. We will also discuss the extent to which happiness constitutes an aim of academic work.

MALS 74400 Special Topics in the Archaeology of the Classical, Late Antique, and Islamic Worlds
Tuesday, 4:15 pm – 6:15 pm, 3 credits, Prof. Elizabeth Macaulay
In-Person, Room 3207
Cross-listed with MSCP 80500, CLAS 74100, & ANTRHO

Collecting the Past: Museums and Antiquities
Since antiquity, individuals and empires alike have collected antiquities and works of art; The Roman Emperor Nero wanted specific statues for his Domus Aurea, and Queen Christina of Sweden (1626-89) formed one of the most significant collections of Greco-Roman antiquities in Europe which is in the Prado Museum today. Many of these antiquities are now in museums, primarily in Europe and the United States. Debates about where antiquities belong have only intensified in the early twenty-first century. Should the Parthenon Marbles remain in London or return to Athens? Should the Benin Bronzes, if taken during the European conquest of West Africa, be repatriated to Nigeria? This course will investigate the history and theory of museums and collecting–starting with how museums developed from the Renaissance and early modern cabinets of curiosities and private collections to grand universal museums, such as the British Museum and Louvre, which were cultural projects of the Enlightenment and European empires. Specifically, this course will examine how collections of antiquities and historical objects were formed through purchase, excavation, donations, and illicit means. To understand the formation of these collections, we will consider the intersection of Enlightenment mindsets, nationalism, colonialism, anthropology, religion, and archaeology with museums. We will also consider the legal frameworks for owning such objects, the ethics of collecting, the challenges of looting, and the repatriation of specific objects. The UNESCO 1970 treaty and other legal rulings and conventions about the movement of cultural property will also be examined. Visits to particular New York City Museums and/or with curators will be a part of this class to encourage students to understand how museum collections in New York City were formed and the shifting landscapes of ownership in the twenty-first century. By investigating these questions and others, we can try to understand a future for the past. Because we will be visiting several museums in New York City, students should allow for travel time to and from class on a few occasions.

UED 71100: Educating for a New World: Social Control and the Politics of Interventionism
Tuesday, 6:30 – 8:30 PM, Prof. Brotherton

The United States has engaged in a range of educational/social work/criminal justice interventions in mostly urban neighborhoods designed to prevent youth crime and violence for almost a century. Starting with the Chicago Area Project in 1934 under the leadership of the sociologist Clifford Shaw to the New York City Street Club Project of the 1950s under the direction of the New York Youth Board to today’s credible messenger movement there has been a long history of using the concept of community mentoring to aid young people deemed “at-risk” of criminal deviance and lives cut short by interpersonal violence. While these interventions run the gamut of strategies and desired outcomes, they nearly always include a focus on education and the role that cognitive development plays in the life course trajectory of subjects as they navigate lives impacted as much by the politics of the street as they are by formal institutions like schools and the criminal justice system. It has long been accepted especially in sociology and criminology that a youth’s positive self-value is critical to their life chances and social mobility, crucial aspects of what is generally considered the American Dream. Thus, failure to hone basic educational skills that prepare a person for the workplace and/or further education, and which also help a person to develop a positive sense of self is often seen as a major factor in the emergence of “juvenile crime and delinquency.”

Starting in the late 1970s, however, with the punitive turn in criminal justice especially impacted by President Reagan’s war on drugs, many of our social problems were now considered crime problems. This helped promote an increased emphasis on the problem of youth crime and an ideology and related practices on issues of delinquency became dominated by policies of deterrence rather than prevention. Over the next several decades regardless of the actual levels of youth crime the numbers of incarcerated and criminal justice involved juveniles increased exponentially with marked statistical differences based on indicators of race, class and gender.

This penchant for pathologizing youth and their increased incarceration changed in the last decade with overall rates of juvenile crime and delinquency decreasing and the number of institutions housing these populations declining markedly e.g. youth held in juvenile justice facilities fell from over 100,000 in 2000 to approximately 27,000 in 2022.

This course introduces students to the history, theory and practice of youth crime and violence interventions in the United States, their relationship to the concept of social control and the evolution of alternative strategies based in holistic models of youth development and its crime/violence nexus. Drawing on scholarship from fields and disciplines including education, sociology, criminology and philosophy, we will examine the socio-historical context in which these interventions emerge and develop, their ideologies and practices and the relatively recent wave of projects noted for their adoption of a positive justice philosophy, the use of transformative mentoring practices and the application of restorative justice principles. In addition, I will use scholarly materials and data drawn from my recent evaluation research involving an initiative at the department of juvenile justice in Washington, D.C. where I led a team of investigators from 2017-2022 to study the project’s social processes, outcomes and implications. The results of this investigation are contained in a new publication scheduled for later this year (see “What’s Love Got to do With It: Credible Messengers and the Power of Transformative Mentoring,” Brotherton, Kessler, Rodriguez, Kontos and Muhammad, Temple University.

Jazz was a young music in the early teens when it started to make its way to the New York area, first to Brooklyn’s Coney Island. Johnson—was shaped by the culture of 1920s and 30s American modernism in New York. This course examines the work of both “concert music” composers such as Copland, Still, and others, as well as the music of jazz musicians themselves, to show how both contributed to the remarkable period of American musical modernism that flourished in New York between the World Wars. Along the way, the very concept of “modernism” will be taken to task, and essential issues of race and class will be addressed.

MALS 70000 Seminar in Interdisciplinary Studies, 3 credits
Thursday, 6:30-8:30 PM, Room: GC 3207, Prof: Anna Akasoy 
Mode of Instruction: In-Person
MALS students only

Truth and Persuasion 
This course serves as an introduction to key concepts and concerns of select disciplines of the humanities and social sciences with a particular focus on the ways academics think about the truth embedded in their research and the ways they seek to persuade others of their truth claims.

Academic research typically reflects the ambition to identify a truth of some kind. Depending on the academic discipline, views regarding the nature of that truth, our ability to identify and describe it, as well as conventions for such descriptions can vary substantially. Sometimes, creative exercises can complement academic research, although the resulting constellations can be contentious. Historical fiction, for example, shares with the academic study of history the ambition of describing past realities, but authors apply different standards in their presentation of these realities. Likewise, views differ as to whether academic research aims at establishing a single truth to the exclusion of other descriptions or the extent to which different truth claims can coexist, even where they might be mutually exclusive.

These different attitudes to truth in academic research are reflected in different strategies of persuasion in academic publications. Academics employ a range of strategies in order persuade their readers, including documentation (e.g., citations, bibliographical references, examples), organization of information and rhetorical styles. Persuasion does not happen in a social vacuum. Just how much power differentials can impact interpersonal relations, including the disposition of some individuals to agree with the assertions and desires of others has recently received much critical attention from social movements.

In this course, we will discuss a broad selection of accessible materials, including historical philosophical views how the truth should be demonstrated, explorations of interdisciplinarity and the ability of disciplines to cooperate despite their different conventions for truth claims, the significance of power in persuasion and the recognition of truth, the use of persuasion in conflict resolution, and strategies of critical reading of academic research, including criticisms of manipulation and how to decide where we trust authors and where and when we can realistically probe their arguments. A small selection of academic articles on topics of broad interest will be used for a close analysis of sample strategies of persuasion.

Students will explore academic pursuits in a diverse selection of disciplines as well as the nature of the knowledge produced in these disciplines and the status of this knowledge in specific disciplines, interdisciplinary contexts and the public sphere. Students will have the opportunity to analyze the truth claims in sources of their disciplines of interest as well as the truth claims they will be making themselves in their own academic work. Throughout the semester, they will reflect on the conventions and expectations of academic research and demonstration in their own fields in contrast and comparison with other academic traditions.

Feminine Phantasms in Early Modern India

Femininity in early modern India was mediated by visions, sounds, scents and things as well as bodies of all sexes. As an aesthetic, the feminine qualified experiences of mastery, surrender, vulnerability and longing. It could reveal divine beauty or be delusory, and it was a principal of Mughal sociability and mysticism, both of which it animated with erotic metaphor. Works of art acted on the senses like women and participated in a cultural logic of binaries that bound early modern society in a field of attraction: between conqueror and conquered, sovereign and subject, elite and slave, Muslim and Hindu, Persian and Hindi, and so on. The sexes shared in transfers of femininity and masculinity within inflexible gendered frameworks. While the feminine was defined in contrast with the masculine, it was queer, and the course looks at depictions of women with women. MALS 78500- Film Genres and Global Conflicts
Wednesday 4:15-6:15 PM, Room: GC 7395, Prof: Ria Banerjee
Mode of instruction: In-person
Cross listed with: FSCP 81000, THEA 71400

How does cinema play with genre expectations to normalize, sublate, or sometimes criticize the daily violences that shape our lives from microcosmic, interpersonal to global and planetary levels? What role is assigned to conceptions of peace in particular genres or subgenres? These are the motivating questions of this seminar, which will be a deep dive into a study of film genres and an opportunity to do formal analysis through the lens of violence.

The war film, the action movie, and the much-maligned subset of superhero movies will be our first stopping point. We will begin with a survey of so-called classic war films like Apocalypse Now (1979), Ran (1985), and remade works like All Quiet on the Western Front (1930, 2022). We will study the John Wick franchise in comparison with other franchises selected by seminarians to consider how such film series perform their politics and how their critiques of power manifest on the level of form.

In the second part of the course, we will broaden our scope of inquiry into popular genres that do not foreground any particular conflict and yet are rife with them. We will consider the Hollywood melodrama in light of domestic and sexual violences, particularly Todd Haynes’s queer reworking of Sirkian conventions in Far from Heaven (2002), Carol (2015), and May December (2023). We will spend some time with film noir, a peacetime genre marked narratively and materially by World War II, in works like The Third Man (1949), In a Lonely Place (1950), Touch of Evil (1958), Elevator to the Gallows (1958), and All Night Long (1962).

The third module will focus on the diffuse concept of “modernism” in Indian arthouse cinema, which registers rapidly changing urban modernity as a site of intersectional violences in Bengali and Hindi films like Ritwick Ghatak’s Cloud Capped Star (1960), Mrinal Sen’s Calcutta 71 (1972), Shyam Benegal’s Bhumika (1977) and Kundan Shah’s bitterly satirical Jaane Bhi Do Yaaron (1983). We will end with a survey led by seminarians of contemporary Indian non-mainstream filmmakers aided and abetted by Netflix and Amazon studios in contrast to stereotypical “Bollywood” fare.

What happens to interpersonal violences in movies that appear on the surface to be frivolous? In the final module of this course, we will discuss how comedy accommodates serious conflicts, and our questions will be driven by the silly-serious Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975) or Life of Brian (1979). Is there something clarifying or further obfuscatory about locating systemic violences towards women, the poor, disabled, and other kinds of minorities in historical spoofs? We will conclude with selections from The Great (2020-2023), and Yorgos Lanthimos’s The Favourite (2018) and Poor Things (2023).

Readings in genre theory will accompany each module. Assignments will include weekly short writing assignments, a book review in the field, and a final project that can be a scholarly academic paper.

MALS 70000 Seminar in Interdisciplinary Studies, 3 credits
Mondays, 6:30 PM - 8:30 PM, Prof Anna Akasoy
Mode of instruction: Fully In-person, Room 5417

The Pursuit of Happiness
Happiness is often understood to be an ultimate and universal human aim. Whatever purpose we identify for our actions and ambitions, the purpose of pursuing happiness seems to be self-explanatory. It appears that there is no need to explain that or why we want to be happy. Across social, cultural and intellectual contexts, however, the pursuit of happiness has been conceptualized in different ways. Key aspects such as happiness as ethical or spiritual improvement, happiness as a particular state of mental health or the relationship between happiness and economic prosperity have long been and are still widely discussed. Acknowledged in the US Constitution, the pursuit of happiness is also subject to commodification and cultural commentary. The collective happiness of societies has been gaining attention as a way to measure their general wellbeing and flourishing. Recent developments such as the rise of social media or the impact of the Covid pandemic have raised broad concerns about the happiness and wellbeing of large segments of society, in particular younger people.

In this course, we will explore concepts of happiness from a variety of historical, cultural and intellectual contexts, using a variety of methods mostly from the humanities such as history, intellectual history, cultural history, philosophy, and literary studies. Aspects of the social sciences will be taken into account as well. We will compare conceptual approaches to happiness to a variety of forms of cultural expression such as novels (e.g., Sally Rooney's Normal People for the impact of historical events and socio-economic inequality on the happiness of a particular generation; Angie Kim's Happiness Falls for happiness and neurodiversity) or film and television (e.g., Olive Kitteridge for happiness and interpersonal ethics or for the relationship between happiness and one's environment). Students are welcome to suggest additional examples. We will also discuss recent discussions about a broader mental health crisis and its impact on a variety of social and cultural activity.

Furthermore, we will analyze recent interdisciplinary studies of happiness in order to survey a variety of academic methods and discuss what the interdisciplinary nature of such scholarship might be. The class will offer the opportunity to explore recent scholarship produced at the Graduate Center which is related to the subject of happiness and collective wellbeing. We will also discuss the extent to which happiness constitutes an aim of academic work.

MALS 70000 Seminar in Interdisciplinary Studies, 3 credits
Tuesdays, 4:15 PM - 6:15 PM, Instructor Prof Elizabeth R. Macaulay
Mode of Instruction: Fully In-person, Room 5417

All the World’s a Fair: Culture, Politics, Economics, Art, and Architecture at America’s World’s Fairs This course takes its title from Robert Rydell’s book on World’s Fairs in the United States from the Centennial Exposition of 1876 in Philadelphia to the expositions in San Francisco and San Diego in 1915. The unexpected success of the “Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations,” in 1851 in London, established World’s Fairs, or International Expositions, as a major type of cultural event in Western Europe and later in the United States. Hosting a Fair or Exposition was a physical way that a nation and later specific cities could proclaim their innovations, economic development, technological advancements, artistic and architectural achievements, and cultural standing, as well as construct and articulate their history, as well as its current and future standing. But World’s Fairs were far more than the nineteenth-century equivalent of a trade show; they were spaces where imperial aspirations, tensions over race and gender, and questions of historical inclusion and exclusion played out. This course will take an interdisciplinary approach to the study of World’s Fairs, starting with the early European Fairs and then primarily focusing on specific World’s Fairs and Expositions in the United States. While the Columbian Exposition of 1893 is undoubtedly the most famous of all of the American fairs, it was one of many; cities such as St. Louis and Buffalo held such fairs. Using the fairs, this course will introduce students to graduate-level research, reading, and writing. Students will learn how to write (and demonstrate competency) in different academic genres, including the book review, annotated bibliography, and seminar paper. They will also learn how to investigate and use archival materials and primary sources. Contributions to the course website, discussion forum, and/or other digital platforms will serve as venues where students can exchange their ideas and engage in a reflective, writing process.
 

MALS 71300 The Business of Fashion: Culture, Technology, Design, 3 credits

Mode of Instruction: Fully In-person, Room 6496

Cross-listed with IDS, FSCP, WGS

Archaeology of Media: Film, Fashion, Montage
This graduate seminar-plus-workshops parallels an eponymous course at Parsons, The New School for Design.
Behind this course is the digital turn and ongoing experimentation that has triggered and inspired an archival excavation of films, newsreels, industry and utility films, advertising etc. In its examination of the fashion and film industries, the course brings together theory and practice, history and historiography. The course will explore and excavate the hybrid genre of fashion film, a phenomenon of the tail end of the twentieth century, and a multifaceted entity that escapes rigid definition. The course will critically examine the conceptual broadening of perspectives on fashion film by connecting the rapidly changing field of today’s digital fashion communications with a hidden history of fashion film, newsreels, and utility films at the time of its first emergence. We will look at and compare material of the twentieth century and the twenty-first century following a method that juxtaposes the past and the present in order to actively challenge our conceptualizations of both (Catherine Russell/parallax historiography). The course will draw extensively on seminal work on the archeology of media by Thomas Elsasser as well as on the latest scholarship on film and media by scholars such as Reese-Roberts, Parrika, Evans, Uhlirova and others.

A principal medium for our archaeology is the fashion film and its material culture. It is a medium where making and meaning come together through historical patterns and their contemporary revivals. The cutting of such patterns is a shared technique for garments and moving images. Our expanded understanding of the fashion film traces the original montage of material and its representation in moving images, from films of the silent era to today’s digital motion capture. Practical studio work with editing processes prepares our analyses of mediatic techniques across time and space.

Students can choose to write a traditional paper or make a short film.

MALS 74400 From Alexander to Mohammed: Introduction to the Cultures of the Ancient Mediterranean: Special Topics in the Archaeology of the Classical, Late Antique, and Islamic Worlds: Ottoman Archives, 3 Credits
Mondays, 4:15-6:15 PM, Prof. Anna Akasoy (aakasoy@gc.cuny.edu)
Mode of Instruction: In-Person

Cross Listed with MAMES & ART 74000

Archives of Imperial Archaeology: The Ottoman Empire, the United States and Germany

Recent discussions about the colonial legacies of museums in western Europe have drawn public attention to the function of archaeology as an imperial strategy, as a part of cultural self-fashioning as well as securing access to the material heritage of regions across the world. Two states and their iconic museums typically provide primary reference points, for reasons of scale as well as chronological priority: France with the Louvre and Britain with the British Museum. Both museums served as models for comparable endeavors in other countries.

This course will focus on three states at different stages of their imperial history and the role agents associated with these states played in archaeological explorations in the Middle East: the Ottoman Empire, Germany and the United States. We will explore how these agents developed different attitudes to the diverse archaeological and material histories of the Middle East and the extent to which access, ownership and display can be understood in the context of imperial strategies.

The course will be organized around three moments in that history:

In 1881, the Ottoman imperial museum was established in Istanbul under the directorship of Osman Hamdi Bey. The son of an enslaved man had studied in France and was also the most significant Ottoman painter of the period. We will be exploring the history of this museum, the circumstances of its foundation, the selection of objects in the collection and how the museum’s profile and purpose reflect approaches to Ottoman identity at the time. The section will be an opportunity to explore the case of an empire which was not western European. (In case of interest, the paintings of Osman Hamdi Bey can be discussed more extensively as well.)

In 1898, the German emperor Wilhelm II visited Baalbek in present-day Lebanon during his second visit to the Ottoman Empire. The entire Grand Tour as well as the visit to the ancient site were an expression of Wilhelm’s personal fascination with the region and reflected Germany’s role as a ‘late arrival’ to the global imperial and colonial competition. Seemingly untainted in the Islamic world, Germany developed a deliberate strategy to build an alliance against Britain, but also France. We will be exploring Wilhelm’s visit to Baalbek in this context of Germany’s global imperial ambition as well as German attitudes to classical antiquity and the establishment of prominent museums in Berlin. We will pay particular attention to the Pergamon Museum with its iconic objects the Pergamon Altar, the Ishtar Gate and the Mshatta Facade. Depending on interest, agents included in this section include the multi-faceted characters Heinrich Schliemann, who claimed to have discovered Troy, and Max von Oppenheim who conducted excavations in Tell Halaf.

In 1902, the American industrialist Charles Lang Freer purchased a pot from the New York-based dealer Dikran Kelekian. This piece of pottery, which had come from the Syrian town of Raqqa, formed the beginning of an entire set of Raqqa ware in Freer’s collection of Asian, Middle Eastern and American art. We will be using Freer as an example to explore the rise of private collectors in Gilded Age America and the establishment of collectors’ museums. We will discuss the relationship between these agents and the state and connections between their cultural and economic activities. This section will include research involving archival material such as letters, purchase records and photographs. These sources

will allow insights into Freer’s network including subalterns of the Ottoman Empire. The exercise will prospectively be organized in collaboration with the National Museum of Asian Art.

The course will begin with a prelude on Renaissance representations of Ottoman attitudes to the classical heritage, in particular antiquities. The contest over the ownership of the classical Graeco-Roman heritage will remain an important aspect throughout the class. Furthermore, the course will include at least one ‘crossroads’ topic for focused comparative perspectives. Depending on student interest, this could be the connection between archaeology and the railroad. The expansion of the railroad led to the immense wealth of collectors such as Freer. Railroad construction sometimes led to archaeological excavations. It facilitated a foreign presence in various countries as well as an increased mobility of people, goods and information. Due to its significance for warfare, the railroad was part of an imperial strategy.

In case of interest, we will also explore more recent discussions about repatriation.

CLAS 71100, Plato and the Foreigner in Philosophy, 3 credits
Tuesdays, 6:30-8:30PM Prof. Nickolas Pappas
Mode of Instruction: In-Person

The course will build to and then undertake a reading of the Republic, with attention to 1) governance by philosophers in the newly constituted city and 2) the identification of foreigners in the city’s homogeneous population. The question for the course is how the Platonic proposal for governance based on enlightenment intersects with the wish for a unified citizenry. The two come apart when ignorance and knowledge both signify foreign status.

With its hope for governance by philosophers, the Republic represents a landmark attempt to reform both politics and philosophy. The famously impractical subject of philosophy brought to the cynical practicalities of politics: Plato’s Republic assigns itself this task in a bid to re-imagine philosophy so that it engages with politics, and to re-imagine politics in a way that opens it to such engagement.

If governance by philosophy identifies the Republic at its most idealistic, the effort to maintain a unified citizenry reveals a most un-ideal enterprise of purification: the identification of foreigners. In the Republic this will mean identifying natives as alien and expelling them.

MES 78000 Muslims, Tolerance and Religious Pluralism, 3 credits
Mondays 6:30-8:30PM, Prof Anna Akasoy
Mode of Instruction: In-person

This course takes as its starting point and key interest the discursive function of tolerance as a fundamental value of modernity and explores a variety of ways in which debates about tolerance in western European discourse are related to Muslims. While Muslim societies, especially the Ottoman Empire, served as an example of tolerance to early modern western European observers, a prominent western European discourse now contrasts tolerance and Islam. The degree to which societies, especially Muslim-majority societies, are deemed compatible with modernity is often measured in terms of their tolerance, in particular as manifest in religious pluralism. Conversely, cases of public and religiously validated violence are regularly described as intolerance.

In this course, we will explore the nature and history of this discourse in conversation with select historical as well as literary examples. Methodologically, the course will thus combine elements of Islamic and Middle Eastern history with intellectual history, contemporary political philosophy and literary text analysis. We will begin with an introduction to contemporary western debates about tolerance and the history of this concept in western European thought. MALS 70500 Consent: Medieval Legacies from Europe and the Islamic Worlds in Comparative Perspective, 3 credits

With the #MeToo movement and the introduction of new Title IX regulations at US institutions of higher education, the definition of consent in sexual encounters has become the subject of public controversy. Activists, legal scholars, university administrators and other participants in these debates discuss the conditions of voluntary affirmation and its expression, personal autonomy and responsibility, and the multiple ways in which imbalances of power shape relationships. At the center of many of these debates are the conventions of binary gender roles which assign a passive position to women and which validate pursuit and persuasion on the part of men. MALS 70800 Transformations of Modernity, 1914-present, 3 credits
Thursdays, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. David Gordon dmgordon@mindspring.com
Mode of Instruction: Fully In-person

The twentieth century was a time of contradictions. Science revolutionized our understanding of the world, and the universe. Industrial manufacturing promised unlimited plenty. The evolution of mass politics seemed the dawn of greater equality and freedom. New art forms began to expand consciousness in previously unimagined ways.  There was renewed belief in the ability to achieve a utopian transformation of society.  Reality proved very different. New ideologies - Communism, Fascism, Nazism - won the fanatical support of millions. Two World Wars, massacres and genocide revealed the dark and malevolent passions that continued to rule many. Although peace and prosperity had begun to be achieved after mid-century, this has since become threatened again.  A Chinese industrial revolution is creating an uncertain economic future for the world. Russian aggression against Ukraine threatens war on a level unknown since 1945. As the hopes and sorrows of the past continue to inform our world today, where we have been, and where we are going can be better understood through MALS 70800 Transformations of Modernity,1914-present.

MALS 72200 Contemporary Feminist Theories, 3 credits
Wednesdays, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. Jean Halley jeanomalleyhalley@gmail.com
Mode of Instruction: Online Synchronous

This fully online synchronous course explores Contemporary Feminist Theories through feminist work about racial, economic and sexual justice, and in terms of “bodies with gender.” We investigate what it means to “have” gender and to “be female” with a focus on the United States. Making use of the frame offered by Patricia Ticineto Clough’s book, Feminist Thought, we consider contemporary feminist theories on differences and similarities in the experiences of women and other gendered bodies across lines of race, class, sexuality, species and ability. We examine how gender defines human experiences and how feminists resist these definitions.

Sigmund Freud once called work and love the central arenas of human life. We examine contemporary feminist thought on what it means to have gender in love and to be gendered at work; on the representation of women and gender in the larger culture; and about violence in the lives of gendered bodies particularly queer bodies, those of color, and those gendered female. We make use of a variety of texts in exploring feminist thinking on race; the “nature” of gender, love and sexuality; so-called women’s work; the expectations “experts” have of diversely gendered bodies; (dis)ability; nonhumans; and the representation of gender in the mass media.

This seminar focuses on the production, social reproduction, and unequal distribution, of environmental benefits and burdens. It traces theoretical lineages in the social sciences and applies them to the study of environmental problems. MALS 73100 American Culture and Values, 3 credits
Mondays, 6:30-8:30 PM, Prof. Justin Rogers-Cooper jrogers@lagcc.cuny.edu
Mode of Instruction: Fully In-person

What is American studies and what does it do? Perhaps as importantly, how might American studies inform your interests and projects? To answer the first question, our course will consider the histories, theories, and practices of American studies as an academic discipline. We will especially contemplate concepts thinking through racial capitalism, enslavement, and empire. We’ll consider a range of texts that represent major fields and topics within American Studies, including Asian American studies, Black marxism, gender and queer theory, Africana studies, native/indigenous studies, and abolition geography (among others). To press our scholarly inquiries into dramatic directions, we’ll contemplate Black cultural expressions by Octavia Butler, Billie Holiday, Paul Robeson, and James Baldwin. We will apply our learning in part by asking how our readings and conversations challenge received political wisdoms in the present. We’ll further mine the contradictions of past crises both for how they anticipate our current moment, and for how their anomalous elements could provoke speculative knowledge that unform assumptions about the U.S. nation(s), its culture(s), and its values.

Our class projects will be flexible enough to coincide with different student interests, tracks, and projects. Students will read from the “Keywords” project in American Cultural Studies by New York University Press, and write one of their own. They will read some event reviews from American Quarterly, and compose one. They will also digest conference papers and write an abstract based on their interests and projects. Students will leave the class with writing and notes that catalyze their studies and tracks with the questions and methods of American studies, and thus hopefully take a new set of ideas, questions, and concerns to propel their creativity and time in Liberal Studies at the Graduate Center.
