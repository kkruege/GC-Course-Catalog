UED 71100 Radical Care: Teaching & Leading for Justice in Urban Schools, W 630 - 830 pm, Rivera-McCutchen, hybrid 

This course examines the application of Critical Race Theory as a more humanistic approach to urban schooling, focusing specifically on critical conceptions of care, love, and hope as essential tools for disrupting structural inequality. We start with an examination of Black feminist and womanist approaches to schooling and the Black principalship tradition, then move on to other scholars whose work examines healing-centered practices in schools, culminating in freedom dreaming and counternarrative methodologies. Students will collaboratively generate course guiding questions and analyze the five interconnected components of Radical Care while interrogating its opposite, Limiting Care, which is characterized by deficit perspectives, lowered expectations, and institutional complicity. Through a combination of in-person, synchronous, and asynchronous sessions, students will engage in weekly dialogic exchanges, facilitate discussions that extend Radical Care scholarship, and systematically engage interdisciplinary literature. Students will develop critical research skills through a creative final project grounded in scholarly research that translates collectively-generated course questions for diverse audiences (inside and outside of the academy) through non-traditional formats such as editorials, podcasts, TEDTalks, zines, and curriculum guides. While grounded in educational leadership, this course is relevant to students in sociology, anthropology, social work, public policy, and related fields concerned with institutional transformation and justice-oriented practice in urban contexts

UED 72100 Anthropology of Education, H 630-830 PM Lamont Hill, hybrid

This graduate course examines the issues, approaches, and contributions of anthropology to the study of education. Drawing from foundational and contemporary ethnographic studies, the course examines the role of educational institutions and processes in cultural transmission, nation-state formation, socialization, and knowledge production. The course also uses anthropological theory and method to examine the practice of human learning across a variety of formal and informal contexts. Particular emphasis will be placed on issues of race, gender, immigration, class formation, and globalization.

UED 73100 Doing Visual Methods, W 4:15 - 6:15 PM Luttrell (fully in-person)

In the past two decades, there has been an explosion of participatory visual research projects. This course aims to situate these projects within overlapping disciplinary traditions (education, sociology, anthropology, cultural studies, and psychology) and to consider what makes this research “critical” (i.e. feminist, de-colonial, reflexive, transformative). The course affords students the opportunity to read and review exemplary projects; and to work directly with visual data utilizing different analytic/interpretive strategies. Students will have access to an audio-visual archive of data I have collected based on a longitudinal visual research project with children 10-18 or can utilize an archive of their own interest. We will consider issues of power and ethics in participatory visual research; how working with visual data can (but not necessarily) challenge traditional notions of knowledge production; the role of new technologies in disseminating and reaching new audiences; and how we align our work with the expectations and politics within the communities within which we work.

UED 73100 Conceptual and Methodological Foundations of Qualitative Research, W, 9:30 - 11:30 am Molly Andrews

This course introduces students to the shared principles and varied approaches to qualitative research theory and methods in the social sciences. The course presents the foundations of diverse qualitative research methodologies in terms of their epistemologies, practices, and contributions. Presentations by faculty members illustrate the range of traditions of qualitative methods presented by faculty actively using the approach and thus able to share it and reflect on it critically as well as practically. Faculty coordinating and guiding the course invite colleagues teaching qualitative courses and providing a diverse range of approaches, such as ethnography, grounded theory, narrative inquiry, the listening guide, study of lives, and so on. Students will be introduced to methods of data collection and analytic strategies typically employed in qualitative inquiry and will discuss special issues the confront qualitative researchers.

UED 71200 Agency and Social Transformation: Resistance and Activism Frameworks in Education and Human Development, H 415 - 615p, Stetsenko HYBRID    

“The need for change bulldozed a road down the center of my mind” – these words of Maya Angelou resonate with the core message of this course. Activists and social movements have always prioritized social change and the role of agency, yet these topics remain highly contested across major frameworks at the intersection of education and human development. However, social change and agency are central to approaches with activist-transformative agendas that strive not merely to understand the world but to change it. We will examine a broad spectrum of approaches – from critical pedagogy and sociocultural theories to philosophies of resistance, Global South perspectives, and decolonial methodologies – in terms of how they address agency at both individual and collective levels. The challenge is to explore how to break the ethos of passivity, political burnout and apathy that is permeating traditional theorizing and research to instead follow those who pave the ways for activism and radical change. The core questions to address are when and how people have the power to matter and make a difference in their own lives, their communities, and the world at large to move past current conditions and create a better world. Interrogating politics and ideologies that various approaches embody is also on the agenda – all while exploring how the tools of agency can leverage social resistance and activism in overcoming current crises in order to tackle power differentials and inequalities.     

UED 71200 Eugenic Legacies and Mad Futures: History, Policy, and Pedagogy at the Intersections of Disability Justice and Madness Studies, T 630 - 830 PM, Ilyes         

In this course, we trace historical pathways of eugenics into our present moment, watching as eugenic language and practices unabashedly reemerge, particularly in the narratives related to schools and systems of education. We'll examine how racialized and classed systems of psychiatric labeling and disability classification function within educational contexts: how over- and under-diagnosis shapes which students are tracked, segregated, or funneled into the school-to-prison pipeline, and how notions of whitewashed normalcy determine who belongs in classrooms and who gets pushed out. Alongside this critique, we'll attend to the refusals, the freedom dreams, and the resistance to systems of domination and erasure.

Through intersectional engagement with madness and disability studies, disability justice, and critical histories, we'll explore: How do we hold systems responsible so that communities can thrive? What does it mean to do ethical research with students and communities rather than on them? How can critical pedagogy animate new possibilities for teaching with integrity and fullness in spaces where students continue to be segregated and pushed to the margins? What can policy, activism, and imaginative, aesthetic practice teach us about refusing dehumanization and reimagining what schools could be?     

Drawing on history, politics, policy, pedagogy, and ethical arts-based and participatory research methods, course participants will have the opportunity to apply these frameworks to their individual research interests while learning from and with each other across programs and positionalities.   

UED 73220 Arts - Based Research and Visual Methodologies, M 630 - 830, Fellner 

In the past decade there has been an explosion of visual and arts-based research projects across several disciplines (e.g. anthropology, educational, public health, psychology, sociology). Arts-based research is an approach to inquiry, a methodology, which relies on artistic means to interrogate, reflect upon, and understand ideas, situations, relationships, experiences and structures. Like traditional research, arts-based inquiry seeks to raise awareness, broaden insight and understanding, and produce knowledge. Unlike traditional research, the value of arts-based research lies in its ability to evoke experience, celebrate nuance, validate feeling, promote reflexivity and generate empathy rather than adhere to established research concepts of generalizability, replicability and “truth.” Ambiguity, complexity, polyphony and polysemy – the welcoming of multiple voices, meanings and perspectives – are friends of an arts-based methodology. Visual methods are also used to document phenomena and make visible patterns that might otherwise remain invisible.

In this course we will explore different visual arts-based methods of inquiry, including photography, drawing and painting, video and collage, paying particular attention to projects that are dedicated to building a better future for all and making our world more just. Visiting arts-based researchers will discuss their visual explorations, and a number of assigned readings will help us to theorize our own work and that of others. Central to this course will be your own visual inquiry into a subject that is of importance to you. Students may opt to analyze arts-based data that you have already gathered or to represent existing research findings in an alternative visual or multimedia form. Through discussion and analysis of your own work and that of visiting artist-researchers, you will develop conceptual and methodological skills to be applied in your own visual arts-based explorations. The course is organized around a commitment to joy, pleasure and playful experimentation as vital ingredients to arts-based research, analysis, and scholarship

UED 75100 Qualitative Methods W 415 – 615 p, Luttrell   

This course focuses on the elements of qualitative research design as a vehicle to introduce students to different traditions, approaches and epistemologies that characterize qualitative research within the field of education. Thus, it includes an emphasis on formulating researchable questions; considering different strategies for data collection, analysis and the representation of findings; understanding and wrestling with ethical considerations; formulating strategies to ensure reliability, validity, and credibility; and preparing to engage the qualitative research process as a series of decisions in which some things are lost and some things are gained (i.e. being aware of both the limitations and strengths of one’s research design).  

This course distills knowledge from the social sciences and humanities, presenting qualitative inquiry as an art and a science. You will learn to approach qualitative research as an iterative, “discovery” process, and one that requires reflexivity – broadly defined. The course is designed to deepen your thinking about your research topic, questions, and various epistemological and intellectual conflicts in doing social analysis.

UED 71200 Solidarity Cities, H 2 - 4 PM, Pavlovskaya          

The new course “Solidarity Cities” will examine how people in the U.S. and around the world have been building cities (and beyond) based on solidarity rather than profit and competition. In doing so, they have collectively shaped urban spaces according to their needs and dreams. Is the production of solidarity-based urban space and related struggles a marginal utopian project? Or is it a widespread yet invisible practice rooted in social reproduction, livelihoods, and mutual aid? Although racial capitalism and neoliberal forces appear to have fully defined our cities, people have long drawn on various forms of solidarity (economic, social, cultural, and political) to remake urban areas—despite all barriers—into cities of collective resistance to harm, cities of collective consumption and public goods, and cities of cooperation and mutual aid.  

72200 Foundations of Queer Studies II- Praxis, W 630 - 830, Brown, Hybrid  

Building upon Foundations of Queer Studies I: Epistemologies, Foundations of Queer Studies II: Praxis explores the concept of praxis and its differentiation from theory, model, and framework. The course excavates how praxis operationalizes and puts theory into action. Students will survey distinctive (non)traditional methods. A deeper dive will move toward the “queering” of methodology and intersections with traditional investigative strategies often used within disciplines that inform LGBTQ studies such as literature, cultural studies, social sciences, history, humanities, women’s and gender studies, humanities, and sexuality studies. Course content will examine the implications of “queering” praxis on historical and current social change and cultural shifts in local, national, and global societies. Through applied areas such as health, law, education, business, and journalism, students will contrast praxis approaches and assess their utility to address queer-focused social justice issues. Students will formulate the expansive roles of ethics and reflection in “queering” methodology and “queering” praxis. While examining real-world praxis application to address LGBTQ issues, in an applied project, students will bridge epistemology to praxis. Students will critique if and how processes of praxis begin with theory and end with theory being reframed.

UED 73200 Research Methods Technology, Learning and Development , T 415 - 615 PM, Daiute   

This semester we focus on research methods in/on/with interactive digital narrative (IDN) as a case study providing conceptual foundations of digital technologies for use in education, mental health, public policy, and social change. IDN is a game-like narrative genre involving designer-player collaborations. In addition to gaining knowledge, appreciation, and methodologies relevant to IDN, we develop research rationales, questions, and designs for uses of interactive media by educators, social welfare practitioners, community activists, and others who work to support human development. We craft a dynamic critical and creative perspective on digital media in several ways, including to emphasize human purpose, systems knowledge, and creator/user ownership. Course readings, workshops, and assignments highlight complexities of IDN system-process-product dynamics including interactivity, play, multi-modal semiotics, game mechanics, choice poetics, character development, inter-subjectivity, and role play. Given the digital setting of IDN, methodologies may also employ AI-assistant tools to support rigorous process analyses for pilot case studies as well as for large scale studies. Class sessions begin with the professor’s brief lecture, a student presentation analyzing an IDN example with potential research design, and full-class discussion about the readings and presentations. The course includes a basic hands-on experience with authoring in Twine to familiarize students with how it works and to encourage their further pursuit of IDN design. The course is adapted to students' research interests. No prerequisite.

UED 71100 Educating for a New World: Social Control and the Politics of Interventionism, 3 credit, T, 630 - 830 PM, Brotherton

The United States has engaged in a range of educational/social work/criminal justice interventions in mostly urban neighborhoods designed to prevent youth crime and violence for almost a century. Starting with the Chicago Area Project in 1934 under the leadership of the sociologist Clifford Shaw to the New York City Street Club Project of the 1950s under the direction of the New York Youth Board to today’s credible messenger movement there has been a long history of using the concept of community mentoring to aid young people deemed “at-risk” of criminal deviance and lives cut short by interpersonal violence. While these interventions run the gamut of strategies and desired outcomes, they nearly always include a focus on education and the role that cognitive development plays in the life course trajectory of subjects as they navigate lives impacted as much by the politics of the street as they are by formal institutions like schools and the criminal justice system. It has long been accepted especially in sociology and criminology that a youth’s positive self-value is critical to their life chances and social mobility, crucial aspects of what is generally considered the American Dream. Thus, failure to hone basic educational skills that prepare a person for the workplace and/or further education, and which also help a person to develop a positive sense of self is often seen as a major factor in the emergence of “juvenile crime and delinquency.”

Starting in the late 1970s, however, with the punitive turn in criminal justice especially impacted by President Reagan’s war on drugs, many of our social problems were now considered crime problems. This helped promote an increased emphasis on the problem of youth crime and an ideology and related practices on issues of delinquency became dominated by policies of deterrence rather than prevention. Over the next several decades regardless of the actual levels of youth crime the numbers of incarcerated and criminal justice involved juveniles increased exponentially with marked statistical differences based on indicators of race, class and gender.

This penchant for pathologizing youth and their increased incarceration changed in the last decade with overall rates of juvenile crime and delinquency decreasing and the number of institutions housing these populations declining markedly e.g. youth held in juvenile justice facilities fell from over 100,000 in 2000 to approximately 27,000 in 2022.

This course introduces students to the history, theory and practice of youth crime and violence interventions in the United States, their relationship to the concept of social control and the evolution of alternative strategies based in holistic models of youth development and its crime/violence nexus. Drawing on scholarship from fields and disciplines including education, sociology, criminology and philosophy, we will examine the socio-historical context in which these interventions emerge and develop, their ideologies and practices and the relatively recent wave of projects noted for their adoption of a positive justice philosophy, the use of transformative mentoring practices and the application of restorative justice principles. In addition, I will use scholarly materials and data drawn from my recent evaluation research involving an initiative at the department of juvenile justice in Washington, D.C. where I led a team of investigators from 2017-2022 to study the project’s social processes, outcomes and implications. The results of this investigation are contained in a new publication scheduled for later this year (see “What’s Love Got to do With It: Credible Messengers and the Power of Transformative Mentoring,” Brotherton, Kessler, Rodriguez, Kontos and Muhammad, Temple University Press 2025).

UED 72100 Scholar-Activist Praxis, 3credit, M, 630 - 830 PM, Bishop, hybrid

Choosing to teach for justice in the U.S. in 2025 is a daring act. The landscape of the moment is stark: teachers can lose their jobs for teaching diverse and inclusive content; K-12 schools and universities stand to lose their funding for supporting equity efforts. Current attacks on teaching and learning inside educational institutions and out-of-school spaces are attempting to seed curricular fear and evoke an intellectual paralysis, hoping that educators and researchers will succumb to forms of self-censorship and engage in what CUNY professor Michelle Fine calls “anticipatory obedience.” As scholar-activists, we resist these efforts. The focus of this course will be on examining the socio-political conditions of two major facets of scholar-activist academic work: (1) culturally sustaining teaching and learning; and (2) forms of public scholarship that do not shy away from the pervasive dangers of the moment. We will read the work of scholar-activists working at/from/despite the margins of academia in the U.S. and internationally in order to illuminate routes and forge trajectories we can take to teach for peace and publish for justice. The final project of this course will provide enrolled students with options to create content for wider distribution that aligns with their scholar-activist goals.

UED 73100 Anti-racist Methodologies & Research Ethics for Black Participatory Community Engaged Research, 3 Credit, H 415-615 PM, Brown, T. Battle, Guishard, hybrid

In response to troubling movements to delegitimize/disappear critical race Black and Latine/x Studies from higher education (López et. al, 2021; Russel-Brown, 2022), and with hopes of providing substantive training for the next generation of Black & Latine/x scholars, we have developed a transgressively imagined syllabus for an Anti-racist Methodologies and Research Ethics for Black Participatory Community Engaged Research course. The fundamental objective of this course is to support graduate students with designing, conducting, ethically evaluating, and producing intersectional critical race scholarship (and policy) that can be utilized to address inequities rooted in systemic racism. This 3-credit, 3-hour course was designed with funding support from the Black, Race, and Ethnic Studies (BRES) Curriculum Development Faculty Fellowship program and as a function of that was envisioned as a core course under the Research Approaches with a Qualitative Emphasis area. In graduate research methods courses, there are often sparse or superficial attempts to infuse moments of criticality, by including whiffs of intersectionality praxis, critical race theories/methodologies, decoloniality, white queer theory, and BIPOC feminist perspectives. Research ethics training is designed similarly, with readings, practices, and theoretical frameworks that center cis-white normative ethics as they pertain to mostly individual research participants. These traditions are troubling, particularly when rich tomes of place-based and community-centered scholarship exist. This course pushes the academy to rethink graduate training curricular frames in keeping with aims of the CUNY Black, Race and Ethnic Studies Initiative Council and Collaboration Hub.

UED 72100 Postcolonial Thought, 3 Credit, T, 2 – 4 PM , Perkins

Postcolonial thought starts with the idea that empire and the cultural-ideological-epistemic-psychological structures of Western imperialism have been central to the making and unmaking of societies (Go, 2018). This course will critically engage with the fundamental claim of postcolonial theory: the world we inhabit is impossible to understand except in relationship to the history of imperialism and colonial rule. In this regard, we will draw upon the work of scholars in South Asia, Africa, Latin and South America (among other places) that: (1) interrogate the lingering forms of coloniality and (2) endeavor to imagine a world after colonialism.

EES 79903 Participatory Democracy & Social Movements, W, 2-4PM, Su (Satisfies 75200)

This seminar takes a look at what ordinary citizens do to shape public policies and engage in politics— in ways other than voting. We explore the notion that popular participation can make democratic governance more legitimate, fair, and effective. We examine theories and existing evidence on the promises and challenges of participatory democracy— alternatively called bottom-up participation, maximal democracy, or direct democracy. Specifically, we will examine forms and functions of civil society from a comparative perspective by looking at specific examples of (1) participatory institutions (neighborhood councils, urban budgeting, school governance, etc.), (2) participation in non-governmental organizations and development projects, and (3) social movements around the world (potential cases include landless people’s movements, transnational networks, mothers of political dissidents who have “disappeared,” AIDS protest groups, etc.). Sometimes, these three categories blur into one another. We will try to focus on case studies in “Global South” middle-income countries alongside domestic and European ones. How much should ordinary citizens participate in policymaking, and how? Under what circumstances? We pay particular attention to crucial tensions in on-going struggles, such as questions of authority and epistemic justice, contradictions in relationships between civil society and the state, and co-optation and non-reformist reforms.

CEDF 75000 (20768): Theoretical and Conceptual Frameworks: Research from an Ethical Center (3 credits, Dr. Cara Furman), Thursdays 4:30-7:00pm

This course is premised in the commitment to do research that deeply matters, that speaks to your core commitments. It asks you to consider, what lenses shape, reshape, and influence the way you, in the words of Paulo Freire, “read the world” and “read the word”? Applied to your projects, what commitments guide the core of your research (and probably teaching too)? How do you articulate and refine this lens? How do your core commitments influence your underlying reason for engaging in your study, your methods, your data collection, and analysis? These commitments are your Theoretical and Conceptual Frameworks in your research design, the backbone of your project. To help you in defining, refining, and building your framework, this class will focus on how you read and write about frameworks. Because we learn by doing and learn best when motivated by our own interests, I will introduce you to some common frameworks in alignment with your interests. After determining our course readings based on your tentative projects, we will first practice strategies for reading this more conceptual work. You will then work to draft a conceptual framework. Finally, we will read some data through different lenses.

UED 74100 Quantitative Methods, M, 4:15 - 6:15 PM Stoudt (fully in-person) 
Quantitative designs, methods and analyses persist as the dominant approach to research in the social sciences. In many ways, statistical data define knowledge and truth in our culture. Like any privileged standpoint, quantification – unlike its qualitative counterpart - is largely taken for granted as essential to science and is rarely asked to justify itself as a worthwhile pursuit. This course seeks to explore the traditional uses and misuses of quantitative research, the long history of critical inquiries into quantification and statistics, and the application of numbers in policy making, movements, and organizing. This class will emphasize issues of social justice, emancipatory research, and public-oriented scholarship. 

This course is for any student in Urban Education, Psychology, Social Welfare, Sociology, Earth and Environmental Sciences or anyone else interested in exploring the use of statistics in research and society from a critical perspective. If you are a student seasoned in statistics, this course will hopefully strengthen your future quantitative work by giving you an opportunity to unpack common practices and applications. If you are a student less invested in statistical discourse, this course will hopefully deepen your understanding of quantitative research as well as reframe its potential value for advancing your future scholarship.

UED 73200 Arts Based Research (In person)
Prof. Gene Fellner
Wednesdays, 4:15 p.m. – 6:15 p.m. 

In the past decade there has been an explosion of visual and arts-based research projects across several disciplines (e.g. anthropology, educational, public health, psychology, sociology). Arts-based research is an approach to inquiry, a methodology, which relies on artistic means to interrogate, reflect upon, and understand ideas, situations, relationships, experiences and structures. Like traditional research, arts-based inquiry seeks to raise awareness, broaden insight and understanding, and produce knowledge. Unlike traditional research, the value of arts-based research lies in its ability to evoke experience, celebrate nuance, validate feeling, promote reflexivity and generate empathy rather than in adherence to established research concepts of generalizability, replicability and “truth.” Ambiguity, complexity, polyphony and polysemy – the welcoming of multiple voices, meanings and perspectives – are friends of an arts-based methodology. Visual methods are also used to document phenomena and make visible patterns that might otherwise remain invisible.

In this course we will explore different visual arts-based methods of inquiry, including photography, drawing and painting, video and collage, paying particular attention to projects that are dedicated to building a better future for all and making our world more just. Visiting arts-based researchers will discuss their visual explorations, and a number of assigned readings will help us to theorize our own work and that of others. Central to this course will be your own visual inquiry into a subject that is of importance to you. Students may opt to analyze arts-based data that you have already gathered or to represent existing research findings in an alternative visual or multimedia form. Through discussion and analysis of your own work and that of visiting artist-researchers, students will develop conceptual and methodological skills to be applied in your own visual arts-based explorations. The course is organized around a commitment to joy, pleasure and playful experimentation as vital ingredients to arts-based research, analysis, and scholarship.

It is our hope that the knowledge that grows out of Black Diasporic Visions may inform and continue to be informed by urgent interventions and creations today.

African people and people of African descent have always, envisioned, created. It is in part for the capture of innovation for profit, that early African civilizations were enslaved and African developments redirected. Let us read African and African descendant innovations and demands for being, with as much rigor as we read exploitation and oppression. In Black Diasporic Visions we consider how the tools of literary archaeology and magical realism inform how freedom dreams and provide possibilities for just existences and being seen. We examine what may be gleaned from the use of the ringshout by artist Common to honor the life of Freddie Grey, the Free Breakfast Programs organized by the Black Panther Party for educational reform, large statutes of African descendants by artists such as Simone Leigh and Kehinde Wiley that reclaim and redefine public space, community incorporation of solar panels and farming into educational programming in post hurricane Puerto Rico, embodied avatars as a means of survival as defined by Uri McMillan, and the call and response of #sayhername?

New technologies of expulsion and racial capital call for us to consider what it means to be in the wake, doing wake work, as described by Christina Sharpe. The range of constructions and visions reviewed in this course serve as correctives and prescriptives to the problems of omission and misrepresentation in academia, archives and society at large. Ultimately, Black Diasporic Visions, centralizes historically and globally informed liberatory possibilities, imperative to our lives today, that challenge divides between theory and practice.
