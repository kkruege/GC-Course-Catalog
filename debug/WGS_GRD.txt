WGS 71601 / WSCP 81601: Special Topics: Reading Feminist Lives

GC: Wednesdays, 11:45-1:45pm, 3 credits, Prof. Dana-Ain Davis, Hybrid Synchronous

Departmental Permission Required: Contact our APO (msheehan@gc.cuny.edu) with your CUNYFirst ID number to request permission. Seats are granted on a first-come, first-served basis.

This seminar approaches feminist theory and method through whole books rather than excerpts. We read four full-length works by feminist authors who build theory from lived experience, whether the experience of the women they work with or their own: women porters in Accra, forensic nurses and survivors of sexual assault in the United States, workers and residents of a tax-haven economy in the US Virgin Islands, and a memoirist writing about her own body. Each book is paired with a shorter text that gives us a conceptual, methodological, or genealogical lens, and each unit ends with a conversation/podcast/interview with/by the author.

We ask how feminist scholars write lives, how they decide whose stories are told, by whom, and with what obligations. Themes include African and Black feminist theory, the erotic as knowledge, care and violence, racial capitalism and gendered labor, embodiment and fatness, and the ethics of ethnographic and autobiographical storytelling. Students learn to read a book as an argument, a method, and a piece of craft, and to think about how they might write their own work.

WSCP81000: Race, Gender, and Criminal Justice

GC: Monday, 6:30-8:30PM

This interdisciplinary course examines how gender and race shape social experiences and inequalities in contemporary society. The course draws on concepts, theories, and current scholarship from a variety of disciplines, including sociology, criminology, criminal justice, gender studies, history, and economics. Topics may include mass incarceration and justice-involved women; gendered migration; sexual victimization and gender-based violence; drug use, mental illness, and the “War on Drugs” in the United States; educational inequality and achievement; reproductive rights; capitalism and labor markets; and contemporary social movements, among others. By the end of the course, students will gain a deeper understanding of major contemporary social problems and the ways in which race, gender, class, and other social identities shape individual experiences, life opportunities, and achievements. Students will learn to critically analyze social issues through an intersectional lens, evaluate current policies, and consider alternative approaches to promoting social justice, equality, and social change. The course will culminate in student presentations on topics of their choice related to gender, race, and social justice.

COMPARATIVE LITERATURE

WSCP81000: Neapolitans Narratives, from Elena Ferrante to Gomorrah: Literature, Cinema, Television

GC: Monday, 2/4 credits, Prof. Giancarlo Lombardi, In person

THIS COURSE CAN ONLY BE TAKEN FOR 2 OR 4 CREDITS, NOT 3 CREDITS. WHEN YOU REGISTER, BE SURE TO SELECT THE COURSE CREDIT OPTION YOU WANT TO TAKE. THIS CANNOT BE CHANGED AFTER REGISTRATION. 

Elena Ferrante’s Neapolitan tetralogy shined a spotlight on an Italian city which had long exercised the imagination of philosophers, literati and visual artists. Called by Walter Benjamin a porous city for its theatrical architecture and for its ‘inexhaustible law of life’, Naples is not merely setting but protagonist in recent literary, cinematic, and televisual texts. Starting from the critical reflections of Benjamin, Antonio Gramsci and Franco Cassano, this course will discuss the complex portrayal of contemporary Naples in three different modules: the first and most extensive will center on Elena Ferrante’s entire literary production, while the second will analyze the cinematic works of a group of directors strongly associated to Naples: Mario Martone, Paolo Sorrentino, and Matteo Garrone. The seminar will conclude with a module that examines the representation of Naples in three series: the adaptations of Ferrante’s My Brilliant Friend and Roberto Saviano’s Gomorrah, and the breakout hit The Sea Outside. The course will be conducted in English.

FRENCH

WSCP81000: Reading Annie Ernaux

GC: Thursdays, 4:15-6:15PM, 2 credits, Prof. Nancy Miller, Fully in-person

THIS IS A SEVEN-WEEK COURSE. THIS COURSE WILL BE OFFERED FOR THE FIRST SEVEN WEEKS OF THE SEMESTER.

In her 2022 speech upon receiving the Nobel Prize in Literature, Annie Ernaux describes what she felt when she first studied literature: “Literature was a sort of continent which I unconsciously set in opposition to my social environment. And I conceived of writing as nothing less than the possibility of transfiguring reality.” Since 1974, Annie Ernaux has published books in several genres: novel, memoir, personal diaries, exterior diaries, dialogues and interviews. She writes in the first person as a woman from the working classes who identifies as a “transfuge,” a class defector. The books, slim in format, are also meditations on recurrent and often interlocking themes: love, death, sex, identity in the social world—a practice that Ernaux named "autosociobiographie"–and always, the stakes of writing itself. Looking back now over Ernaux’s long career, what will we make of her project?

During a seminar of seven weeks, we will read Ernaux’s work across several genres. Students may read in English or in French, though class discussion will be in English.

HISTORY

WSCP81000: Desire’s Politics: History and Theory

GC: Tuesday, 415-615, 3 credits, Prof. Dagmar Herzog, In-person

INSTRUCTOR PERMISSION REQURIED. Students must contact Professor Herzog directly to ask for permission. If the instructor allows you in the course, you must then ALSO forward that exchange to the WGS APO (msheehan@gc.cuny.edu) WITH YOUR CUNYFIRSTID NUMBER to obtain the necessary permission.  

What can historians and others with adjacent curiosities learn from classics in the history of sexuality, a field that burgeoned from the 1990s onwards, and in what directions might the field go now? What value could potentially be gleaned from revisiting psychoanalysis, that by now 125-year-old, so oft-reviled but also oft-revised originary theory of desire? What even is the “it” that we call sexuality, what are its boundaries, and how can we theorize its relationship to other realms of existence? Initially for Europe and the U.S., but increasingly for every part of the globe, scholars not only investigated the ever-intricate interplay of laws and customs, values and practices surrounding abortion, contraception, eugenics, hetero- and homosexuality, marriage, orgasm, pornography and prostitution, sex advice and education, and sexual abuse and violence, but also demonstrated conclusively that, at least for modern history, there simply was no major phenomenon that could not be more fully and deeply understood if attention to the history of sexuality was brought to bear – from the Enlightenment and the French Revolution to industrialization and imperialism, from the world wars and the rise and defeat of fascisms to the Cold War and then the aftermath of communism. Any history of the present, however, would have to test that proposition anew. As a class, we will study historical, contemporary, and theoretical materials and identify fresh questions as well as preliminary answers. Instructor permission required.

INTERNATIONAL MIGRATION STUDIES

WSCP81000: Global Immigrant Cities

GC: Tuesday, 6:30-8:30PM, 3 credits, Prof. Francois Pierre-Louis, In person

Global immigrant cities are crucial research sites for exploring the possibility of going “beyond” the nation state and national society as traditional foci of migration research. This course asks the question of how immigrant-receiving global cities experience, respond to, and are transformed by the changing composition of their ethnic and racial populations. It will explore global immigrant cities’ histories of ethnic and racial difference; how their ideologies about diversity, pluralism, and multiculturalism have evolved and changed over time; the extent to which they incorporate (or not) their immigrants (including forced migrants and refugees); and the different economic, cultural, and political impacts that immigration has had on these places. Specific examples of cities will vary but will include geographically diverse places in both the Global North (e.g., New York City, London) and Global South (e.g., Lagos, Manila). The main focus will be on international comparison, and students will be trained in the use of comparative perspectives to illustrate similarities and differences between global cities. While a crucial dimension of globalization, the comparative study of migration also opens up fresh comparative and international perspectives on the urban experiences of immigrants.

WSCP81000: Migration Policy

GC: Tuesday, 4:15-6:15PM, 3 credits, Prof. Utku Sezgin, In person

The class will address how law and politics shape migration policy in an international comparative framework. It will cover some of the main themes in migration policy historically as well as how legal migration regimes come about. It will look specifically at documented versus unauthorized migration and the role of civil society actors such as community organizations and ethnic groups in making migration policy. Other topics will include how nations decide who can and cannot migrate, how citizenship is established, what factors shape policies and their implementation, the increased role of municipalities in making policy towards immigrants, refugee resettlement, and immigrant integration and deportation.

LGBTQ+ Studies

WSCP81000: Foundations of Queer Studies II – Praxis

GC: Wednesday, 6:30-8:30PM, 3 credits, Prof. Matt Brim

Building upon Foundations of Queer Studies I: Epistemologies, Foundations of Queer Studies II: Praxis explores the concept of praxis and its differentiation from theory, model, and framework. The course excavates how praxis operationalizes and puts theory into action. Students will survey distinctive (non)traditional methods. A deeper dive will move toward the “queering” of methodology and intersections with traditional investigative strategies often used within disciplines that inform LGBTQ studies such as literature, cultural studies, social sciences, history, humanities, women’s and gender studies, humanities, and sexuality studies. Course content will examine the implications of “queering” praxis on historical and current social change and cultural shifts in local, national, and global societies. Through applied areas such as health, law, education, business, and journalism, students will contrast praxis approaches and assess their utility to address queer-focused social justice issues. Students will formulate the expansive roles of ethics and reflection in “queering” methodology and “queering” praxis. While examining real-world praxis application to address LGBTQ issues, in an applied project, students will bridge epistemology to praxis. Students will critique if and how processes of praxis begin with theory and end with theory being reframed.

MUSIC

WSCP81000: Seminar in Musicology: Feminist Listening to Contemporary Music

GC: Thursdays, 10:00-1:00PM, 3 credits, Prof. Ellie M. Hisama

Feminist Listening to Contemporary Music develops modes of feminist listening across a range of c20 and c21 music. Using intersectional critical tools, this course examines a wide repertory of music, including popular music (rock, R&B, hip-hop, jazz, blues, traditional/folk, country) and art music. It aims to develop your ability to hear, discuss, and respond to music and sound through lenses of gender/sexuality/race/ethnicity/ability/power. We will think about what it means to “do” feminist listening and how intersectional listening—a concept offered by the ethnomusicologist Allie Martin—can change our relationships to music, sound, and the world. Together we’ll develop a critical vocabulary and grammar for discussing and analyzing the effects and meanings of contemporary music as understood by twenty-first century listeners. Course readings include theoretical, analytical, musicological, poetic, and autobiographical texts drawn from music studies, feminist/gender/queer/trans studies, ethnic studies, Third World studies, anthropology, sociology, literary study, and visual art. Through close listening to an array of music and reading of texts, the course uses a variety of intersectional sieves, opening new vistas for understanding music, sound, and art in everyday life. Responses to readings, listening, and viewing; a midterm project; and a final presentation and project that may include creative work.

PHILOSOPHY

WSCP81000: Oppression, Nonideal Theories of Justice, and Social Change

GC: Tuesday, 11:45-1:45, 4 credits, Prof. Serene Khader, In person

Contemporary political philosophers have had shockingly little to say about to transition from this world to a more just one. Ideal theorists have assumed this is mostly an empirical question. But most nonideal theorists have also unintentionally sidelined the question by focusing on reparative justice. This course will examine normative questions raised by transitions out of injustice, with an emphasis on the particular injustice of oppression. Though readings will largely be drawn from contemporary Anglo-American political philosophy, we will also examine work on institutional design currently being developed by social movements that emphasize the need for “just transitions,” such as degrowth, ecoterritorial feminisms, anti-extractivist movements, and landless movements.

WSCP81000: Black Feminist Approaches to Education Research

GC: Mondays. 630-830PM, 3 credits, Prof. Watson, Hybrid

The seminar centers on Black feminist thought and methodology to critically examine educational theory, policy, and praxis. It is designed for students in the Urban Education Ph.D. program, though relevant to students across CUNY interested in social justice, critical race studies, and feminist theory.

This seminar builds on existing CUNY strengths in critical race studies and feminist scholarship by providing a dedicated space for Black feminist methodological praxis in education. By centering Black women's intellectual traditions and lived experiences, the course challenges and expands traditional research methods that often overlook or misrepresent the experiences of marginalized communities. It directly addresses the call for more substantive training in critical race and feminist methodologies in graduate programs.

This seminar offers an in-depth exploration of Black feminist approaches to education research. It covers foundational Black feminist theory, key methodological tools, and the ethical considerations of conducting research with and within Black communities. Students will engage with a transdisciplinary body of scholarship, develop critical inquiry skills, and design a research project that applies Black feminist principles to an educational topic of their choosing.

WGS71701/WSCP71700 - Global Feminisms

GC: Thursdays, 6:30-8:30PM, 3 credits, Professor Saadia Toor, In Person

 

*Departmental Permission Required: Contact our APO (msheehan@gc.cuny.edu) with your CUNYFirst ID number to request permission. Seats are granted on a first-come, first-served basis.*

 

With the rise of authoritarian regimes around the world, what insights do feminist movements and theorizing offer? What are the fault lines between different forms of feminisms? How do liberal feminist ideals and principles intertwine with an imperial agenda? What are the links and divergences between Islamaphobia and racism? Who should be the arbiter of “equality,” “fairness,” and “human rights”? What ethical questions shape the practices of feminism and feminist politics both domestically and internationally? What is the relationship between modes of production, political economy, and gender politics? What are the possibilities and limits of a transnational feminist politics? What are the material conditions/structural factors which enable and/or undermine transnational feminist solidarity? This course grapples with some of these questions in the wake of rapid world altering changes. We will explore the gender dynamics of racial, ethnic, and economic relations of power in domestic, international, and transnational settings. We will examine feminist scholarship produced by and about American women of color, women from the global south, and other social and political actors whose experiences and thinking have shaped contemporary ideas about gender, power, and international political economies. We will explore how both self-identified feminists and people who do not consider themselves feminists write about and understand gender, justice, human rights, tolerance, agency, imperialism, and other relevant topics. We will also examine how women and self-identified feminists practice solidarity across and within national boundaries, paying attention to the possibilities and constraints that shape transnational feminist activism. We will look at both empirical and theoretical texts from a range of academic disciplines.

WSCP81000: Postcolonial Autobiography and Memoir

GC: Thursday, 6:30-8:30PM, 3 credits, Prof. Harold Veeser

Postcolonial memoir and autobiography offer unique personal accounts of self-transformation. WSCP81000: Early-Modern Bodies: Civilized, Rationalized, Medicalized, Sexualized, Deviant, Monstruous, Possessed, Racialized

GC: Tuesdays, 4:15-6:15pm, 4 credits, Fully In-person, Professor Domna Stanton

 

THE WSCP81000 SECTION OF THIS COURSE CAN ONLY BE TAKEN FOR 4 CREDITS

 

This course will examine diverse and dissimilar constructions of bodies and embodiment in Early Modern Europe. We will begin by examining the influential theories of Elias, kantarowicz, Foucault and Butler. These readings (and other critical texts) will inform our discussion of different – potentially contradictory – discourses imbricated in the production of Early Modern gendered bodies of color (white, brown, black), beginning with the civilized courtier and the honnête homme and femme as well as royal bodies of kings and queens. We will then re-examine Cartesian dualism, followed by medical (anatomical and dissected) corpses; the reproductive, impotent, and deviantly sexual (erotomanic, cross-dressed, hermaphroditic, sodomitic, tribadic and masturbating bodies); and those that are monstrous (eunuchs), possessed and/or mystically ecstatic. Our readings will culminate with the racial matrix that affects/infects the perceptions of Ottoman and black (enslaved) bodies. For any questions about the course, please contact Domna Stanton (dstanton112@yahoo.com).

Primary text authors we will read: Ancillon, Aretino, Bartolinus, Baudier, Castiglione, Chorier, Choisy, De Certeau, De Erauso, De Zayas, Descartes, Du Bosc, Ferrand, Jacob, Labat, Marin, Marten, McClive, Mongin, Ndiaye, Paré, Park, Saint Simon, Sharp, Sinistrari, Venette

FUTURES INITIATIVE

WSCP81000: Performing Futurities

GC: Monday, 11:45-1:45, 3 credits, In-person, Prof. Anaar Desai-Stephens and Prof. RaShelle Peck

This course investigates the ever-contested category of "the future" through the lens of performance, performativity, and embodied praxis. We ask: What might different futures look, feel, and sound like? Which actors shape futures, and who gets to have a future? When “the future” is under contestation, how might forms of performance – from the spectacular to the daily quotidian – serve as sites for the articulation, negotiation, and even refusal of certain futures? Drawing on scholarship from performance studies, anthropology, Black critical studies, feminist studies, and more, we will critically engage questions regarding temporality, ephemerality, and iterativeness while examining the role of affects such as hope, anticipation, pessimism, and doubt in the study of futurities. Work on South Asian, Black, and Indigenous futurities, in particular, will guide us in considering how differently sited lineages have imagined alternative anti-racist and anti-capitalist societies.

Alongside our reading and discussion, we will experiment with creative embodied praxis as methods of thinking and feeling otherwise. Through creative writing, embodiment, movement, sounding, and other modalities, we will catalyze our own radical imaginations. This course thus offers daily practices of imagining, dreaming, and experimenting as forms of intellectual praxis that are crucial to the production of the futures we want to bring into being.

HISTORY

WSCP81000: Taboo? The Politics of Black Beauty & Sexuality

GC: Mondays, 4:15-6:15pm, 3 credits, In-person, Prof. Tanisha Ford

This graduate seminar examines beauty and sexuality as contested sites of power and resistance in the United States and the broader Black world. We will explore how scholars have transformed seemingly trivial concerns into rigorous analytical frameworks for understanding racial hierarchies, gender construction, economies of capitalism, and embodied politics. Topics of focus include: colorism/featurism, hair, cleanliness, corpulence, pleasure, sex work, BDSM, pornography, skin bleaching and cosmetic surgery. Drawing from history and gender and sexuality studies, the course interrogates how beauty standards and sexual norms for Black people have been defined by and against white supremacist and colonial logics. We will examine how anti-Black attitudes aimed at the body have functioned as mechanisms of social control, while simultaneously exploring how these same sites serve as spaces for Black self-fashioning and collective identity formation. Employing "taboo"—something or someone forbidden by social custom—as an analytic, the course examines the evolution of this field of study. We will trace how early scholarship focused on challenging myths and stereotypes of Black bodies as hyperracialized and hypersexualized, and how contemporary work engages more robust dialogues about the complexities of Black sexual expression, aesthetics, and erotic economies. In effect, this contemporary scholarship is transgressing, reimagining, and, in some instances, reclaiming “the taboo.” The course will provide a foundation for students who are preparing for exams or who plan to write a thesis or dissertation on African American history; African diaspora history; women, gender, and sexuality studies; race and ethnic studies; or cultural studies. Assignments will include short response essays and a longer analytical paper or creative project related to the major themes of the course.

WSCP81000: Introduction to Race and Ethnicity

Tuesdays/Thursdays, 6:00-9:00 PM, 3 credits, Prof. Nathalie Etoke (Netoke@gc.cuny.edu)

Online

Start date: 5/28

Polarizing societal dynamics continually shape American society, as displayed by the 2024 presidential election and the centrality of the migrant question in public debates, which is doubled by intricacies of voting patterns according to race, gender, and class. The dynamics of polarization find their roots in the origin story of the U.S. Focusing on the longue durée of imperialism since 1492 and accounting for the consequences of the genocide of Indigenous Peoples, racial slavery, settler colonialism, historical violence, and the reactionary opposition to ongoing struggles for social justice and freedom, we will unearth the roots of white supremacy and systemic racism in the U.S. We will also examine U.S. society’s contradictory impulses to value both belonging and exclusion. The course explores the relationship between race and power, analyzing how their convergence shapes American citizenship and identity. We will draw from a variety of disciplines, spanning the Humanities, Social Sciences and the Arts, in order to think critically about race, racism, identity formation, everyday experience, and American history.

We will address the following questions:

What does it mean to study race and ethnicity?

How have conversations about race changed over the last few years?

How are racial hierarchy and racism woven into the fabric of The United States of America?

How does race shape our daily life and our sense of self?

How does it structure inequality in our society?

What are the sociohistorical processes that have shaped our understandings of race and ethnicity?

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students should be able to:

Explain the difference between race and ethnicity.
Describe and explain key ideas and concepts concerning the social construction of race and ethnicity.
Think critically about their own racial position, recognize, and appreciate human experiences that differ from their own, and explain the significance of racism in today’s world.
Describe how issues of race and ethnicity have shaped American institutions, laws, and practices over time.
Identify and evaluate the strategies a variety of scholars use to make their argument as well as the theoretical claims that they present.
Have a holistic and complex understanding of what it means to be racialized in the U.S.

WSCP81000: Archaeology of Media: Film, Fashion, and Montage

GC: Wednesday, 4:15-6:15pm, 3 credits, In person, Prof. Eugenia Paulicelli

 

This graduate seminar-plus-workshops parallels an eponymous course at Parsons School of Design at The New School.

This course is suggested by the digital turn and ongoing experimentation that has triggered and inspired an archival excavation of films, newsreels, industry and utility films, advertising etc. In its examination of the fashion and film industries, the course brings together theory and practice, history and historiography. The course will explore and excavate the hybrid genre of fashion film, a phenomenon of the tail end of the twentieth century, and a multifaceted entity that escapes rigid definition. The course will critically examine the conceptual broadening of perspectives on fashion film by connecting the rapidly changing field of today’s digital fashion communications with a hidden history of fashion film, newsreels, and utility films at the time of its first emergence. We will look at and compare material of the twentieth and twenty-first century, following a method that juxtaposes the past and the present to actively challenge our conceptualizations of both (Catherine Russell/parallax historiography). The course will draw extensively on seminal work on the archeology of media by Thomas Elsasser as well as on the latest scholarship on film and media by scholars such as Reese-Roberts, Parrika, Evans, Uhlirova and others.

The course concept and syllabus are conceived together by instructors from CUNY GC and Parsons; classes are shared by the two cohorts and group projects situate collaborative work among our two institutions. In our courses, material processes of repairing, remaking and reusing are combined with original techniques of editing and montage, to learn about shared principles in diverse fields of creative practice. We study these processes through media archaeology that analyses digital culture and memory, unearthing layers of past and present media that represent cultural movements and fashions. Histories of montage show how divergent elements are brought together in new and unexpected combinations, with distinct rhythms and types of sequencing that unite many creative techniques: from editing film and sampling sounds, via collaging images and assembling materials, to fusing fabrics and patchworking textiles. In the fashion film and its material culture, making and meaning come together through historical patterns and their contemporary revivals. The cutting of such patterns is a shared technique for garments and moving images.

Theoretical seminars are paired with practical studio work on editing processes, to further our analyses of mediatic techniques across time and space. Our students therefore can choose to write an academic paper or create and edit moving images.

One hundred years before Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie proclaimed “We should all be feminists” and Beyoncé popularized that decree, the word feminist was first being used in the United States. WSCP81000: Based Research and Visual Methodologies

GC: Monday, 6:30-8:30PM, 3 credits, In person, Prof. Fellner

In the past decade there has been an explosion of visual and arts-based research projects across several disciplines (e.g. anthropology, educational, public health, psychology, sociology). Arts-based research is an approach to inquiry, a methodology, which relies on artistic means to interrogate, reflect upon, and understand ideas, situations, relationships, experiences and structures. Like traditional research, arts-based inquiry seeks to raise awareness, broaden insight and understanding, and produce knowledge. Unlike traditional research, the value of arts-based research lies in its ability to evoke experience, celebrate nuance, validate feeling, promote reflexivity and generate empathy rather than adhere to established research concepts of generalizability, replicability and “truth.” Ambiguity, complexity, polyphony and polysemy – the welcoming of multiple voices, meanings and perspectives – are friends of an arts-based methodology. Visual methods are also used to document phenomena and make visible patterns that might otherwise remain invisible.

In this course we will explore different visual arts-based methods of inquiry, including photography, drawing and painting, video and collage, paying particular attention to projects that are dedicated to building a better future for all and making our world more just. Visiting arts-based researchers will discuss their visual explorations, and a number of assigned readings will help us to theorize our own work and that of others. Central to this course will be your own visual inquiry into a subject that is of importance to you. Students may opt to analyze arts-based data that you have already gathered or to represent existing research findings in an alternative visual or multimedia form. Through discussion and analysis of your own work and that of visiting artist-researchers, you will develop conceptual and methodological skills to be applied in your own visual arts-based explorations. The course is organized around a commitment to joy, pleasure and playful experimentation as vital ingredients to arts-based research, analysis, and scholarship.

WSCP81000: Educating for a New World: Social Control and the Politics of Interventionism
GC: Tuesday, 6:30 – 8:30 PM, 3 credits, Brotherton, fully in person

The United States has engaged in a range of educational/social work/criminal justice interventions in mostly urban neighborhoods designed to prevent youth crime and violence for almost a century. Starting with the Chicago Area Project in 1934 under the leadership of the sociologist Clifford Shaw to the New York City Street Club Project of the 1950s under the direction of the New York Youth Board to today’s credible messenger movement there has been a long history of using the concept of community mentoring to aid young people deemed “at-risk” of criminal deviance and lives cut short by interpersonal violence. While these interventions run the gamut of strategies and desired outcomes, they nearly always include a focus on education and the role that cognitive development plays in the life course trajectory of subjects as they navigate lives impacted as much by the politics of the street as they are by formal institutions like schools and the criminal justice system. It has long been accepted especially in sociology and criminology that a youth’s positive self-value is critical to their life chances and social mobility, crucial aspects of what is generally considered the American Dream. Thus, failure to hone basic educational skills that prepare a person for the workplace and/or further education, and which also help a person to develop a positive sense of self is often seen as a major factor in the emergence of “juvenile crime and delinquency.”

Starting in the late 1970s, however, with the punitive turn in criminal justice especially impacted by President Reagan’s war on drugs, many of our social problems were now considered crime problems. This helped promote an increased emphasis on the problem of youth crime and an ideology and related practices on issues of delinquency became dominated by policies of deterrence rather than prevention. Over the next several decades regardless of the actual levels of youth crime the numbers of incarcerated and criminal justice involved juveniles increased exponentially with marked statistical differences based on indicators of race, class and gender.

This penchant for pathologizing youth and their increased incarceration changed in the last decade with overall rates of juvenile crime and delinquency decreasing and the number of institutions housing these populations declining markedly e.g. youth held in juvenile justice facilities fell from over 100,000 in 2000 to approximately 27,000 in 2022.

This course introduces students to the history, theory and practice of youth crime and violence interventions in the United States, their relationship to the concept of social control and the evolution of alternative strategies based in holistic models of youth development and its crime/violence nexus. Drawing on scholarship from fields and disciplines including education, sociology, criminology and philosophy, we will examine the socio-historical context in which these interventions emerge and develop, their ideologies and practices and the relatively recent wave of projects noted for their adoption of a positive justice philosophy, the use of transformative mentoring practices and the application of restorative justice principles. In addition, I will use scholarly materials and data drawn from my recent evaluation research involving an initiative at the department of juvenile justice in Washington, D.C. where I led a team of investigators from 2017-2022 to study the project’s social processes, outcomes and implications. The results of this investigation are contained in a new publication scheduled for later this year (see “What’s Love Got to do With It: Credible Messengers and the Power of Transformative Mentoring,” Brotherton, Kessler, Rodriguez, Kontos and Muhammad, Temple University Press 2025).

WGS 71600 / WSCP 81600: Research Methods: Queer Feminist Methods
GC: Wednesdays, 11:45 AM – 1:45 PM, 3 credits, Prof. Matt Brim, In person

**Departmental Permission Required. If you would like to take this course, contact msheehan@gc.cuny.edu with your CUNYFirst ID number**

How do scholars create new knowledge in gender and sexuality studies? How do we work with interviewees, key texts, and other data sources while putting not only protocols but political commitments into practice? How do we do gender and sexuality studies? These questions, each of which asks how we do something, point to the topic of methods. Methods may seem like a straightforward set of standard protocols for collecting and analyzing data. Feminist researchers count, survey, interview, transcribe, and code. Queer Studies scholars, like others, use qualitative approaches, quantitative techniques, and critical lenses to understand culturally and historically significant phenomena. But does queer feminist academic inquiry always require an ordered approach? How can ordered and disordered methods generate knowledge?

Queer Feminist Studies recognizes both order and disorder as generative. Interdisciplinary feminist scholars have long mixed different types of research methods, resulting in a blossoming and blurring of fields. Likewise, rather than dismissing outlier data or trying to straighten up its unruly subjects, scholars who work in the emerging field of queer methods exploit the possibilities that arise from the “messiness” of LGBTQI social life. Methods of queer feminist inquiry can also be messy because they are inseparable from the political investments that orient our work toward social justice, yet those connections can be tenuous.

The protocols of Queer Feminist Studies reflect not only researchers’ decisions about how to collect data but more fundamentally their own understandings about what counts as evidence, including the ephemeral, the unique, the intuitive, and the fictive. Whereas Western epistemologies and scholarship have produced protocols that privilege empirical observations in the guise of the scientific method, queer feminist researchers also draw on Indigenous and non-Western knowledge systems to understand worldmaking. At another level, class-based queer feminist priorities remind us that the question of method—the how of research—is inseparable from the resources we require to do our work. Methods must therefore be understood as material practices as well as enactments of protocols and political commitments.

These are some of the ways we'll be thinking about queer feminist methods in our class. Coursework will involve careful reading, interesting and generous participation, sharp weekly response papers, and a 12-15-page final project that points toward a future iteration. Why are we still in the midst of a global refugee crisis that now involves 43.8 million people and 63.3 million internally displaced persons (UNHCR)? WSCP81000: Critical and Decolonial Approaches to Migration: from Racial Capitalism to Border Abolition
GC: Tuesday, 2:00 – 4:00 PM, 2/3/4 credits, Prof. Miriam Ticktin and Prof. Sofya Aptekar, Fully In-Person

*Instructor Permission Required. Students should email one paragraph explaining how the course fits into their studies to mticktin@gc.cuny.edu and to Sofya.Aptekar@slu.cuny.edu*

The displacement of people has been noted as the biggest and most urgent global challenge, next to climate change. This class explores how we might understand the movement of people today, in critical terms that move beyond the older political and scholarly literatures on migration. These often take sovereign nation-states for granted as the spatial basis of political life, where migration across borders is seen as a linear movement from one nation-state to another, ending with the process of “integration.” Rather than seeing migration as ‘problematic mobility’ (Anderson, 2017, p. 1532), signaling the need for control, this class shifts to understanding the movement of people through the lenses of critical migration studies and mobility studies, as both of these literatures provide a different set of conceptual, analytic and empirical grammars by which to understand what are more often unruly, unpredictable, and non-linear movements. This can mean unlearning taken for granted assumptions to consider such constructions as migration as reparations or decolonization. Critical migration studies draws attention to major transformations in the way that global migration works today that have not been sufficiently accounted for by established theories of immigration, especially theories of immigrant integration and assimilation. Similarly, the turn to mobility as a frame attends to movement (including pauses, stasis, and friction), understanding it in larger contexts and constellations of non-human things, such as commodities or pathogens. Mobility and its counterpart, immobility, help us to widen the frame beyond both migrants and the nation-state, to look at movement and emplacement, and how these work together, in intersection or at cross-purposes—never alone—but also how movement itself generates new political possibilities. We will look at migration – also known as “people on the move” – in relation to the politics of empire, labor, the military, prisons, racial capitalism, climate collapse and gender.

Readings will draw from anthropology, sociology, urban studies, politics, and other cognate disciplines, including literature on the autonomy of migration, political geographies of space and place, and mobility studies. We will also read novels and watch films, as these areas have been incredibly rich in terms of evoking not only the experiences of people on the move, but the larger transnational apparatus of control which they negotiate every day.

WSCP81000: Feminine Phantasms in Early Modern India
GC: Tuesday, 2-4 PM, 3 credits, Prof. Molly Aiken, Fully In-person

Femininity in early modern India was mediated by visions, sounds, scents and things as well as bodies of all sexes. As an aesthetic, the feminine qualified experiences of mastery, surrender, vulnerability and longing. It could reveal divine beauty or be delusory, and it was a principal of Mughal sociability and mysticism, both of which it animated with erotic metaphor. Our way in is through the visual arts, including gendered objects like wine cups, mirrors, and hookahs (for smoking), but students will also study poetry, music, dance, and dress. We are interested in what I call a feminine "phantasm," intimated by gazes, music, the garden, empty spaces, the longing between lovers, and the threshold that separated images from viewers. We acknowledge how haunted the feminine was and wonder about absence, melancholy, and disavowal. Students visit local museum collections. Readings include affect, psychoanalytic, and queer theory with selections from art history, literary history, and musicology.

WSCP81000: Doing Visual and Arts Based Research
GC: Monday, 4:15-6:15 PM, 3 credits, Prof. Fellner, Fully in-person

In the past decade there has been an explosion of visual and arts-based research projects across several disciplines (e.g. anthropology, educational, public health, psychology, sociology). Arts-based research is an approach to inquiry, a methodology, which relies on artistic means to interrogate, reflect upon, and understand ideas, situations, relationships, experiences and structures. Like traditional research, arts-based inquiry seeks to raise awareness, broaden insight and understanding, and produce knowledge. Unlike traditional research, the value of arts-based research lies in its ability to evoke experience, celebrate nuance, validate feeling, promote reflexivity and generate empathy rather than in adherence to established research concepts of generalizability, replicability and “truth.” Ambiguity, complexity, polyphony and polysemy – the welcoming of multiple voices, meanings and perspectives – are friends of an arts-based methodology. Visual methods are also used to document phenomena and make visible patterns that might otherwise remain invisible.

In this course we will explore different visual arts-based methods of inquiry, including photography, drawing and painting, video and collage, paying particular attention to projects that are dedicated to building a better future for all and making our world more just. Visiting arts-based researchers will discuss their visual explorations, and a number of assigned readings will help us to theorize our own work and that of others. Central to this course will be your own visual inquiry into a subject that is of importance to you. Students may opt to analyze arts-based data that you have already gathered or to represent existing research findings in an alternative visual or multimedia form. Through discussion and analysis of your own work and that of visiting artist-researchers, students will develop conceptual and methodological skills to be applied in your own visual arts-based explorations. The course is organized around a commitment to joy, pleasure and playful experimentation as vital ingredients to arts-based research, analysis, and scholarship.

In recent semesters, a few participants have also explored phenomena through dance and music. Opera was born in Florence at the end of the 16th century as an attempt to revive Greek classical theater, or what at the time Greek drama was thought to have been. Librettos will include some centered on opera’s mythical numen, Orpheus, and others adapted from early modern narrative masterpieces such as Ariosto’s Orlando furioso and Tasso’s Gerusalemme liberata.

We will also devote particular attention to librettos that, while ostensibly narrating ancient historical events, actually tackled (or alluded to) contemporary political controversies (Monteverdi’s Incoronazione di Poppea, Haendel’s Agrippina), and discuss how opera, more than other theatrical genres, toyed (often comically) with issues like gender identities (as in Francesco Cavalli’s and Giovanni Faustini’s La Calisto, and Cavalli’s and [probably] Aurelio Aureli’s Eliogabalo, for instance). The relationship between opera production and the discourse on power at the time the works were conceived and presented will be another essential element of discussion. On the more contemporary end of the spectrum, we will consider works such as Terence Blanchard’s and Kasi Lemmons’ Fire Shut up in my Bones, and also, in light of the critical and box office success of the movie Oppenheimer, John Adams’ and Peter Sellars’ Dr. Atomic. The course will feature guest speakers (musicologists, singers, composers) and will address, among others, issues of gender, theory and practice of dramatic adaptation, and history of operatic performance.

WSCP 81000: Women Making Films in North Africa 
GC: Thursday, 4:15 p.m. - 7:15 p.m., 4 credits, Prof. Nicole Beth Wallenbrock, Fully in-person

In 1978, the directors Selma Baccar, Heiny Srour and Magda Wassaf wrote, “For the Self-Expression of the Arab Woman” to encourage women to pick up movie cameras and to critique their societies. Since this manifesto, many more films have been made by women from the Maghreb, with a notable increase in the early 2000s.

According to Flo Martin, a brief history of Maghrebi women’s filmic narratives points to three phases: 1) women’s histories of the nation that resisted male official narratives, such as The Nubah of the Women of Mount Chenoua (Assia Djebar, 1979) and Fatma 75 (Selma Baccar, 1976) 2) women’s resistance to Arab and/or Western patriarchal systems such as Silences of the Palace (Moufida Tlatli, 1994) and The Sleeping Child (Yasmine Kassari, 2004) 3) political and social commentaries on their country before and after the Arab Revolution of 2010 such as Marock, (Layla Marrakchi, 2005) and As I open my eyes, (Leyla Bouzid, 2015).1

This course will provide students with a historic overview of women’s filmmaking in Algeria, Morocco and Tunisia in the first two phases, before focusing on the directors and screenwriters from the region who represent this third phase of filmmaking that continues today. Some factors in North African women’s recent access and success in filmmaking that the course will address include the digitization of filmmaking, the many festivals in the region, and a new financial commitment from Arab states to fund cinema—lessening a previous dependence on French and European production. We will also examine the streaming economy and ask how online viewing and international distribution color films’ political commentary—films to be considered in our response to this question may include Adam (Maryam Touzani, 2019), The Blue Caftan (Maryam Touzani, 2022) and 4 Daughters (Kaouther Ben Hania, 2023). Furthermore, we will reflect on the ways in which the streaming economy allows for an international success even when a film is banned in its country of origin, such as in the case of Papicha (Mounia Meddour, 2019).

Students will write weekly response papers to films and articles under discussion and in this way build a portfolio, all of which will guide them to a final project

WSCP 81000: Archeology of Media: Film, Fashion, Montage 
GC: Wednesday, 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m., 3 credits, Prof. Eugenia Paulicelli, Fully in-person

This graduate seminar-plus-workshops parallels an eponymous course at Parsons, The New School for Design.

Behind this course is the digital turn and ongoing experimentation that has triggered and inspired an archival excavation of films, newsreels, industry and utility films, advertising etc. In its examination of the fashion and film industries, the course brings together theory and practice, history and historiography. The course will explore and excavate the hybrid genre of fashion film, a phenomenon of the tail end of the twentieth century, and a multifaceted entity that escapes rigid definition. The course will critically examine the conceptual broadening of perspectives on fashion film by connecting the rapidly changing field of today’s digital fashion communications with a hidden history of fashion film, newsreels, and utility films at the time of its first emergence. We will look at and compare material of the twentieth century and the twenty-first century following a method that juxtaposes the past and the present in order to actively challenge our conceptualizations of both (Catherine Russell/parallax historiography). The course will draw extensively on seminal work on the archeology of media by Thomas Elsasser as well as on the latest scholarship on film and media by scholars such as Reese-Roberts, Parrika, Evans, Uhlirova and others.

The course concept and syllabus are conceived together; shared classes with all students and group projects are combined with sessions that are discrete to the respective cohort and feature individual work on texts, sounds and images. In our courses, material processes of repairing, remaking and reusing are combined with original techniques of editing and montage, to learn about shared principles in diverse fields of creative practice. We study these processes through media archaeology that analyses digital culture and memory, unearthing layers of past and present media that represent cultural movements and fashions. Histories of montage show how divergent elements are brought together in new and unexpected combinations, with distinct rhythms and types of sequencing that unite many creative techniques: from editing film and sampling sounds, via collaging images and assembling materials, to fusing fabrics and patchworking textiles. A principal medium for our archaeology is the fashion film and its material culture. It is a medium where making and meaning come together through historical patterns and their contemporary revivals. The cutting of such patterns is a shared technique for garments and moving images. Our expanded understanding of the fashion film traces the original montage of material and its representation in moving images, from films of the silent era to today’s digital motion capture. Practical studio work with editing processes prepares our analyses of mediatic techniques across time and space.

Students can choose to write a traditional paper or make a short film.

WSCP 81000: Seminar in Theatre and Criticism: Trans/Racial 
GC: Tuesday, 2:00 p.m. – 4:00 p.m., 3 credits, Prof. Miles Grier, Fully in-person

The term transracial was originally invoked by the nonwhite children of white adoptive parents, whose domestic situation stems from the material disparities of race on both local and global scales. These adoptees often testify to a lack of identity, while their notorious successors, such as Rachel Dolezal and Oli London, have become scandalous effigies—charged with executing an identity theft to crown European empires’ ongoing expropriation of land and labor. Both those claiming a transracial identity and those decrying such assertions have invoked transgender as a comparable category. Yet, despite the impulse to yoke racial crossing with fraud, the term transracial opens onto a social problem not easily resolved by intoning “to thine own self be true.” It might well be impossible to be true to durable but incoherent social categories that owe their tenacity to their openness to rearticulation.

Clearly, confronting the transracial problematic requires a capacious historical and geographical scope combined with fine-tuned analysis of languages, performances, and rituals of social reclassification. Consequently, in this course, we will investigate cases that range from the slave cultures of ancient Greece and Rome to the playhouses of early modern Europe, from indigenous adoption ceremonies of colonial Americas to present-day media scandals. If theories of gender performativity suggest that performances of gender always fail to approximate the ideals they cite, should we understand this failure to be a site of possibility or of further containment? Can we locate origins of gender and racial performativity, the citationality through which these social categories endure and change across time? Is the relationship of these categories causal, parallel, or mutually reinforcing? And what of the place of voluntary theatricality, strenuously denied in Judith Butler's account? Do trans and race theories restore agency to Butler's arguably constrained subject?

We will likely cover such scholars as Judith Butler, Joseph Roach, Page DuBois, Colby Gordon, Hamit Arvas, Urvashi Chakravarty, Mark Rifkin, Audra Simpson, Greta LaFleur, Hortense Spillers, Omise’eke N. Tinsley, Joseph Pierce, and Eric Lott. Students will be responsible for two presentations: one on a scholarly text and one on a performance, either theatrical or quotidian. Finally, students will produce a seminar paper of approximately fifteen pages.

WSCP81000: International Ethics 
Prof. Carol Gould

“In the face of persistent war and violence (including gender- and racially-based forms), along with poverty and global economic inequalities, climate change, and the spread of authoritarian forms of rule, the question arises of a role for ethics and other norms in countering the most malicious formations and perhaps contributing to more just forms of global interrelationships among nation-states and among people worldwide. This seminar will consider some key ethical issues in international affairs and what our global obligations or responsibilities might be, viewed from the classic perspectives offered by consequentialist and human rights approaches, as well as through the newer lens of a feminist global ethics of care and from the standpoint of critical social theory. The course will begin with an analysis of just war theory and will consider whether it needs to be updated to apply to current forms of warfare and violence, particularly using new technologies. Feminist critiques of just war theory pose challenges to our understandings of the justice of going to war and of fighting in war, as well as to theorizing care in the “post bello” context. Gender-based violence will be considered, including in its extreme forms of genocidal rape and femicide, in conjunction with the leading notion of crimes against humanity. The course will then take up issues arising from globalization—economic and technological, focusing on how diverse theoretical and ethical perspectives propose to address global injustices. Women’s burden of care labor and the phenomenon of global care chains will be examined as important supplements to existing analyses of the ethics of capitalist development and labor rights. A related issue is climate change and its ethical consequences, including the seemingly inevitable increase in climate migration, along with the crucial question of how policies can mitigate or adapt to such climate change in a normatively acceptable way. Here the course will add ecofeminist and indigenous perspectives on understanding our relation to nature to existing approaches in terms of justice and cost-benefit analyses. Finally, all these issues call for greater international cooperation and new forms of global governance, but these come up against the rising nationalism and authoritarianism. The course will conclude with a look at the literature on regional and global democracy, in the face of claims for an exclusive focus on nations and states. Readings will include selections from Michael Walzer, Iris Marion Young, Debra Bergoffen, Sarah Clark Miller, Peter Singer, Martha Nussbaum, Arlie Hochschild, Fiona Robinson, Simon Caney, Val Plumwood, Maria Mies, Jurgen Habermas, and Daniele Archibugi.”

WSCP8100: Mad Studies in the United States Today 
GC: THURS, 6:30 – 8:30 pm, 3 credits, Prof. Aubry Threlkeld

Mad Studies has emerged over the last fifty years as a scattered myriad of theories, approaches, methods, and content. Mad studies addresses both the reality of living with a mental disability and the desire to reclaim, from stigma alone, an identity borne out of that lived reality. In that radical reclaiming, theorists of Madness redefine so-called “mental illness” outside of psychiatric and psychological contexts alone. In this course, we will explore the foundations of Mad studies and its overlaps with other critical fields of inquiry including Black studies, Feminist and queer studies, critical mental health, and disability studies. We will examine and apply Mad theories and approaches to a range of contemporary texts including music, television, film, memoirs/autoethnographies, psychiatric survivor stories, and poetry. We will cultivate curiosity and creativity to make existing theories in other fields Madder.

GC: Thurs 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m., 2 or 4 Credits, Prof. Nathalie Etoke, Fully In-Person

Cross-listed with French

According to a report by Amnesty International, homosexuality is still illegal in thirty-eight African countries and is punishable by the death penalty in four. Despite these repressive laws, sexual subjectivities beyond the heteropatriarchal stranglehold are no longer taboo to name. They are a part of public debates. WSCP 81000: Madame de Stael and Napoleon 
Professor Helena Rosenblatt
3 Credits, Thursdays 2:00 PM- 4:00 PM, Fully In-Person

Cross-listed with History

In this course, we will study the lives of two giants in the history of Western Civilization, whose riveting personal stories are inextricably entwined, although one them has been forgotten by the general public. While thousands of books and articles have been devoted to Napoleon, and the list keeps growing, a relatively minuscule number concern themselves with the woman who was one of his most powerful and persistent enemies. So influential was Madame de Staël that Napoleon felt the need to censor her, spy on her, exile her, and threaten her with arrest, but she never gave up and, in the end, was instrumental in his downfall. We will explore this fierce battle of wills, which one contemporary called the battle between the mind and the sword and which, we will learn, helped to give rise to “liberalism” as we know it.

WSCP 81000: American Social Institutions
GC: Tues 6:30 p.m. - 8:30 p.m., 3 Credits, Prof. Karen Miller, Fully In-Person
Cross-listed with MALS

This class will examine American Studies through the lens of institutions. We will begin by exploring what we mean when we say “institution.” We will think together about why this may be a productive frame for assessing and interrogating the world around us. What does it offer? And what might it elide? How do studies of institutions help expose the myriad ways that power functions in culture, society, and politics? How do institutions, themselves, shape these power relations? And how do different approaches to understanding institutions give us different sorts of answers? American Studies scholars have been asking these questions for decades. We will turn to their texts as sites for exploring them. We will put questions about inequality and how it operates at the core of our inquiry. Thus, we will ask how institutions help amplify and/or mitigate the often-crushing hierarchies that have been (and continue to be) based on racial, gender, sexual, national, and other forms of difference. 

 The class will be organized thematically. Each week, we will take a specific institution or idea about institutions as our starting point. We will examine how scholars from different American Studies subfields have developed approaches for exploring such an institution. The work we examine uses both creative and conventional scholarly tools to explore questions about life, infrastructure, health, race, ethnicity, indigeneity, gender, sexuality, transnationality, borders, architecture, foreign relations, language, politics, economics, literature, art, music, work, social movements, and more. Finally, we will discuss how these institutions may help offer us strategies for imagining new, and possibly better futures. Students will be asked to write one book review, one genealogy of an institution, and a final paper. 

WSCP 81000: Introduction to Mass Violence in the Modern Era
GC: Thurs 6:30 p.m. - 8:30 p.m., 3 Credits, Prof. Elissa Bemporad, Fully In-Person
Cross-listed with MALS

This course explores instances of unprecedented mass violence in modern Europe during the twentieth century. It is based on several case studies, including events in German South-West Africa, Germany, Ukraine, the Soviet Union, and Chechnya. By analyzing some of the most recent scholarship on genocide, ethnic cleansing, and sexual violence, the course examines the short-term and long-term causes for mass violence, assessing the extent to which, in different contexts, it resulted from political ideologies, colonialism, bureaucratic pressures, or ethnic and religious hatred. The course will also focus on the repercussions of mass violence, including acts of revenge, changes in international law and human rights, and attempts to create sites of memory in those places where atrocities were committed. Finally, this course aims at tracing how such unprecedented violence against civilians was experienced by ordinary citizens of European countries, and how it transformed and affected their everyday lives, political choices, and social attitudes during and after the events.

POLITICAL SCIENCE

WSCP 81000: Social Policy & Socio-Economic Outcomes in Industrialized Countries
GC:Tues 4:15 p.m. - 6:15 p.m., 4 Credits, Prof. Janet Gornick, Fully In-Person
Cross-listed with Political Science
By Instructor Permission only.

This course provides an introduction to cross-national comparative research, with a focus on socio-economic outcomes and on the policies and institutions that shape those outcomes. The course is organized around two databases available through LIS, a data archive located in Luxembourg, with a satellite office here at the Graduate Center.  LIS contains two main micro-databases. The Luxembourg Income Study (LIS) Database includes 300+ micro-datasets from over 50 high- and middle-income countries. These datasets contain comprehensive measures of income, employment, and household characteristics. A smaller, companion dataset – the Luxembourg Wealth Study (LWS) Database – provides microdata on assets and debt. Since the mid-1980s, the LIS data have been used by more than 8000 researchers – mostly sociologists, economists, and political scientists – to analyze cross-country and over-time variation in diverse outcomes such as poverty, income inequality, employment status, wage patterns, gender inequality and family structure.  Many researchers have combined LIS’ microdata with various macro-datasets to study, for example, the effects of national social or labor market policies on socio-economic outcomes, or to link socio-economic variation to national-level outcomes such as child well-being, health status, political attitudes and voting behavior. (The LIS and LWS data are accessed through an internet-based “remote-execution system”. All students are permitted to use the LIS microdata at no cost and without limit.) The course has two main components:  1) Students will read and assess a selection of published studies based on the data. 2) Students will carry out an original piece of empirical research using the LIS or LWS microdata. That work will culminate in a term paper. While there are no formal prerequisites, students must have a working knowledge of basic statistics, and beginner-to-intermediate capacity in one of these programming languages: SAS, SPSS, Stata, or R. Neither statistics nor programming will be part of the course's curriculum. Extensive documentation about the data, self-teaching materials, and instructional videos are available on the LIS website.

Note:  All M.A. students must receive clearance from the professor before registering.

GC: Wed 4:15 p.m. -6:15 p.m., 3 Credits, Prof. Lakersha Smith, Hybrid

The legacy of schooling and education for Black Americans has transformed how we conceptualize teaching and learning.  It has also exposed how Black people have been excluded from traditional educational institutions and the powerful ways Black people have disrupted marginalization and disenfranchisement.  This course explores the education and schooling of Black Americans through an interdisciplinary lens. The course begins with an examination of the early efforts taken in educating Black Americans during slavery, the period right after Emancipation, and throughout Reconstruction.  The second portion of the course investigates political movements that coincide with the Black American educational imagination.  Using case studies that focus on Brown v. WSCP 81000 – Consent: Medieval Legacies from Europe and the Islamic Worlds in Comparative Perspective

GC: THURS, 4:15-6:15PM, 3 Credits, Profs. Anna Akasoy & Sara McDougall, Fully In-Person.

Cross-listed with MALS, History, the Futures Initiative, Medieval Studies Certificate Program.

With the #MeToo movement and the introduction of new Title IX regulations at US institutions of higher education, the definition of consent in sexual encounters has become the subject of public controversy. Activists, legal scholars, university administrators and other participants in these debates discuss the conditions of voluntary affirmation and its expression, personal autonomy and responsibility, and the multiple ways in which imbalances of power shape relationships. At the center of many of these debates are the conventions of binary gender roles which assign a passive position to women and which validate pursuit and persuasion on the part of men. Just how much ideals of sexual encounters and gender relations prevalent in the medieval west have shaped these conventions, whether empirically or in the public imagination, is obvious in modern representations such as Ridley Scott’s The Last Duel (2021). In the proposed course, we will explore case studies from both western Europe and the Middle East during the premodern period in which consent in sexual encounters plays a significant role. We will be discussing these examples in two sets of comparative perspectives. On the one hand, we will discuss how historical authors thought of consent and the implications of a lack of consent in sexual encounters in comparison with the way that these issues are approached in present-day debates. On the other hand, by considering material from western Europe and the Islamic world, we will be identifying some of the cultural, social and legal variables which defined and reflected historical realities and maintain an impact on present-day discussions. We will be discussing the larger philosophical, legal, cultural and social categories which shaped representations of consent and disagreements surrounding it. Aspects for consideration include the importance of domestic slavery in the Islamic world as well as economic rights of women in both cultural spheres. Apart from familiarizing themselves with a variety of sources from diverse areas of premodern Europe, the Middle East and Asia, students will be encouraged to reflect on the significance of these sources for modern debates. We expect to consider a balance of western and Middle Eastern material. This course does not require any prior knowledge of medieval history or Middle Eastern studies.

GC: TUES, 2:00-4:00PM, 3 Credits, Prof. Jesse Prinz, Fully In-Person.

Cross-listed with Philosophy.

Philosophical work on identity tends to divide into two very different literatures. One focus on “personal identity", where that is characteristically understood as the investigation of what makes someone count as the same person across possible transformations. Another literature focuses on “social identity”, which characteristically investigates membership is social categories, such as race, gender, class and nationality. Increasingly, it has been recognized that many aspects of identity that are both personal and social. For example, preservation of moral values can be experienced as important for personal continuity, while also linking a person to a social group. This seminar will explore literatures on personal and social identity as well as their interrelationships.

WSCP 81000 - Italian Fascism: History and Interpretations

GC: TUES, 2-4 PM, 3 Credits, Prof. Eugenia Paulicelli, Fully In-Person.

On October 28, 1922, fascist squads headed by Benito Mussolini organized “the march on Rome.” One hundred years later (but also in the last two decades), debate on fascism has again taken center stage. Fascism is a term that often comes back in conversation in several historical epochs and political and cultural contexts. Questions have been asked about its origin and its different declinations throughout the years and in various countries.

The course focuses on specific themes such as violence, empire, gender, race, war, culture and the arts, antifascisms, propaganda and the impact of fascism abroad.

WSCP 81000 - Security and Surveillance: The Production of Fear and Anxiety

GC: THURS, 11:45 AM-1:45 PM, 3 Credits, Prof. Setha Low, Hybrid.

Cross-listed with EES.

This graduate seminar examines the efforts of anthropologists and geographers to attend to the spaces and politics of security. Today, security is one of the most prominent topics on the public’s mind midst a global pandemic and increasing concerns about the biopolitics of everyday as people search for safety and security in a war torn world.  The current crisis draws upon a long history of security concerns that are as diverse in their objects (terrorism, crime and criminals, immigration, war and violence) as the socio-political moments that produce them. A growing ethnographic literature explores security’s various dimensions, from studies of military action and everyday violence to specific security infrastructures (e.g. Lutz 2002; Low 2003; Masco 2006). Some anthropologists are also exploring the contemporary problematization of security by exploring assemblages and novel experiments (e.g. Lakoff and Collier 2009; Maguire 2014; Samimian-Darash and Stalcup 2016). Of course, the breath of this ethnographic literature presents a significant challenge to integrate and critique and this is the primary objective of our readings and discussion.

After a review of the foundational work of Foucault (1977-1978), Beck (1986), Deleuze (1992), Brown (2010), Jackson (2008) and Daniel and Berwick (2020) , the course explores how specific securitization processes are promoted, enhanced and reproduced by 1) infrastructures of exclusion and inclusion; 2) increased visibility through borderization, stereotyping and racial profiling; 3) spatial governance through enclosure and surveillance; and 4) discursive practices that obscure the violence and destruction potential of securitization practices.  Students are asked to offer their own ethnographic research or research proposal that considers various contexts and historical periods to build a theoretical framework that emphasizes the spatial and temporal as well as the political in the study of security.  Through our discussions we should better understand this elusive but dangerous concept that produces a reality that is changing the life chances of millions of people

Course requirements:

Ethnographic or historical project of some aspect of security and/or the politics, infrastructures or technologies that contribute to security claims and the creation of affective configurations.
Read and discuss readings in class
Lead a section of the class focused on their interest area

Course learning goals:

To understand and be able to critique the concept of security

WSCP 81000 -  Aestheticism, Decadence, Modernism, 1880-1930

GC: THURS, 6:30-8:30PM, 4 Credits, Prof. Richard Kaye, Hybrid.

Cross-listed with English .

This class explores the relation between the aestheticist and decadent movements as well as their determination of modernist aesthetics. We will begin with late-nineteenth-century British, American, and French works by Hardy, Wilde, James, and Huysmans. The fin de siècle was a time of pervasive fears and fantasies dominated by such figures as the New Woman, the urban detective, the homosexual bachelor, the Anarchist, the Oriental, the overreaching colonialist, the self-preening aesthete, the vampire, and the femme fatale, the latter reaching an apotheosis in Wilde’s Salome. In the diverse writings of Pater, Olive Schreiner, Vernon Lee, and Wilde, aestheticism emerged as a theoretically coherent and varied movement absorbed in exquisite surfaces, tantalizing ambiguities, and useless artifice. For decadent writers and artists, scientific theories of “degeneration” could be recalibrated as erotically charged, non-teleological experiments, while Freud drew on “decadent” scenarios for his proto-modernist narratives dealing with hysteria and sexual disorder, preeminently in Dora: Fragment of a Case of Hysteria, Women writers, meanwhile, struggled to find a place within the male-defined coteries of aestheticism and decadence, a theme dramatized in Henry James’ tale “The Author of Beltraffio,,” narrated by a decadent acolyte, in which the aestheticist project must be sequestered from female readers, who can only misconstrue its aims as immoral. Yet there were also alliances between male decadents and feminist writers (Wilde promoted Schreiner’s novel The Story of an African Farm because of its bold challenge to realist conventions and depiction of colonialist malaise.)  In France, the woman writer Rachilde publishes Monsieur Venus (1884), a symbolist/decadent novella concerning a dominatrix noblewoman, Raoule, who gradually transforms her working-class lover Jacques into her mistress by emptying him of all his “masculine” traits. 

In the class’s second part we will explore how the fin outlasted the siècle, maintaining an intense afterlife in the Anglo-American modernist writing of Yeats, James, Eliot, Conrad, Joyce, Lawrence, and Djuna Barnes.  The morbidity, subjectivism, sexual experimentalism, and excesses of literary technique characteristic of 1890s sensibility foment modernist revisions that seek to repress their origins in decadent poetics. Wilde's Picture of Dorian Gray, with its hero who cannot "develop," inspires modernist counter-bildungsromanae. We consider Joyce’s Stephen Hero, an early version of Portrait of the Artist as Young Man, arguably a satire of aestheticism as well as a novel with an explicitly Paterian protagonist. The keenly observing, detached bachelor familiar from James also narrates Rilke’s lyrical The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge (arguably the first modernist novel) and that helps to form the paralyzed solitary consciousness of Eliot’s “Prufrock.” Arguably the narrator of Conrad’s Heart of Darkness adopts decadent techniques for his coiled narrative of colonial tragedy.  We will consider, too, Eliot’s absorption in the figure of the Jew as an emblem of a malevolent decadent cosmopolitanism versus Djuna Barnes’ depiction of the decadent Jew in her novel Nightwood as a more positively transformative cultural agent. We will consider how the Aestheticst and Decadent movements shaped the writing of the Harlem Renaissance. Our class concludes with James’ The Golden Bowl, a novel of twinned adulteries that is one of James’ most topical, aesthetically difficult, and decadent works of fiction. Among the works we will read: Hardy, Jude the Obscure, Huysmans, Against Nature; Wilde, The Picture of Dorian Gray, Salome; Schreiner, Story of an African Farm; Freud, Dora: Analysis of a Fragment of a Case of Hysteria; Conrad, Heart of Darkness, Joyce, Stephen Hero, Yeats, Selected Poems; Lawrence, Selected Short Fiction; Eliot, Selected Poetry; James, The Golden Bowl; Barnes, Nightwood; Showalter, Elaine, ed., Daughters of Decadence.  We will read relevant critical and theoretical texts, including Walter Pater, “Conclusion” to The Renaissance; Arthur Symons, “The Decadent Movement in Literature”; Mario Praz, from The Romantic Agony; George Bataille, from Literature and Evil; Richard Ellmann, “The Uses of Decadence”; Richard Gilman, from Decadence: The Strange Life of an Epithet; Linda Dowling, “The Decadent and The New Woman”; Michael Riffaterre, “Decadent Paradoxes,” Leo Bersani, from The Culture of Redemption; Regenia Gagnier, “Individualism, Decadence, and Globalization,” Vincent Sherry, from Decadence and the Reinvention of Modernism”; Matthew Potolsky, from The Decadent Republic of Letters.  A mid-term paper as well as a final paper.

WSCP 81000 - Politics of Race and Slavery in the Early Republic

GC: TUES, 11:45AM-1:45PM, 3 Credits, Prof. James Oakes, Hybrid.

Cross-listed with History.

It has become clear that slavery was a contested issue in American politics for a much longer period than previous generations of scholars once suggested.  Where it was common to start the history of the sectional crisis with the Mexican War, historians now speak of a “long emancipation” that involved “eight-eight years” of conflict.  At the same time, the contours of the struggle over slavery have widened.  Where it was once reduced to a dispute over slavery in the territories, it has now become clear that the fugitive slave crisis was equally important in the developing conflict between the North and the South.  That, in turn, raised questions about the rights of free Blacks in the free states and territories.  As a result, the politics of slavery have become inseparable from the politics of race.  “Politics” itself is no longer confined to parties and elections, but embraces the active participation of Blacks and women.  Gender ideology is now understood to be a key component of antislavery thought.  Finally, where historians once contrasted the radical egalitarianism of abolitionists with the moderation of antislavery politicians, more recent scholars have highlighted interconnections between antislavery politics and radical abolitionism.

This seminar will focus on the politics of race and slavery, primarily in the northern states, between the Revolution and the Civil War.  Readings will range from classic accounts that stressed the role of racism in limiting antislavery politics, to more recent studies that have recovered an enduring anti-racist tradition that arose as the analogue to antislavery politics.  A persistent theme is the way antislavery politics repeatedly raised the question of citizenship rights for African Americans and women.

Cross-listed with Political Science.

This course will examine social welfare policy in the United States, in historical and cross‐national perspective.

We will begin with an overview of the development of social welfare policy in the United States. We will focus on crucial historical periods – including the Civil War years, the Progressive Era, the New Deal, the Great Society, and the “welfare reform” of the 1990s and early 2000s.

Second, we will analyze a set of contemporary challenges that call for active social welfare policy responses in the United States – including inequality, poverty, housing affordability, retirement security, mass incarceration, and the need for care, both paid and unpaid.

Third, we will survey selected social policy lessons from other high‐income countries, especially in Europe, where social provisions are typically more extensive than they are in the United States.

Students will complete weekly reaction papers, and a semester-long research project which will culminate in a paper.
